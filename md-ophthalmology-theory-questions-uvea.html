MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Uvea and Ocular Inflammation (38 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 38 uvea and ocular inflammation theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Classification & Approach

UV 1. Classify uveitis. Enumerate the role of the various investigations (including HLA typing) with respect to management modalities. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification (SUN Working Group)

  • Anatomical: anterior, intermediate, posterior, panuveitis.
  • Onset (sudden, insidious), duration (limited ≤ 3 months, persistent), course (acute, recurrent, chronic).
  • Also: granulomatous vs non-granulomatous; infectious vs non-infectious; IUSG and SUN disease-specific classification criteria (2021).

Investigations: tailored, not a “shotgun” panel

  • Baseline: CBC, ESR, CRP, VDRL/TPHA, chest X-ray, Mantoux or IGRA (TB-endemic India), urine.
  • By phenotype: HLA-B27 (acute anterior uveitis with back pain), HLA-A29 (birdshot), HLA-B51 (Behçet), ACE, lysozyme and HRCT chest (sarcoid), ANA (JIA), toxoplasma serology, HIV.
  • Ocular: OCT (CME), FFA and ICGA (vasculitis, choroiditis), B-scan, aqueous or vitreous PCR, vitreous biopsy for masquerade (lymphoma).

How results guide management

  • Infection found → specific antimicrobials (with steroids where needed); HLA-B27 → monitor systemic disease, rheumatology; non-infectious chronic disease → steroid-sparing immunosuppression or biologics.

Revise from: Uveitis Work-up: A Targeted Approach for Residents, Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

UV 2. Uveitis — types, aetiologies and recent advances in management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types and aetiologies

  • Anterior: HLA-B27, JIA, Fuchs, Posner–Schlossman, herpetic, idiopathic.
  • Intermediate: pars planitis, sarcoid, MS, TB.
  • Posterior: toxoplasmosis, TB, CMV, ARN, serpiginous, birdshot, white dot syndromes.
  • Panuveitis: VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, Behçet, sarcoid, TB, syphilis, endophthalmitis.
  • Masquerades: lymphoma, retinoblastoma, RD, IOFB.

Recent advances in management

  • Biologics: adalimumab (VISUAL I and II trials; SYCAMORE for JIA uveitis), infliximab (Behçet), tocilizumab (CME, JIA), rituximab.
  • Local: dexamethasone implant, fluocinolone implants (Yutiq), suprachoroidal triamcinolone, intravitreal methotrexate and sirolimus.
  • Diagnostics: multiplex PCR, metagenomic sequencing, widefield imaging, OCT angiography.
  • Treat-to-target, steroid-sparing early; MUST trial: implant vs systemic therapy.

Revise from: Uveitis Made Easy: SUN Terms, Patterns and Work-up, Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

UV 3. Granulomatous vs non-granulomatous uveitis; iris nodules. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Granulomatous

  • Insidious, chronic; large “mutton-fat” KPs; iris nodules (Koeppe at pupil margin, Busacca on iris surface), synechiae; choroidal granulomas.
  • Causes: sarcoid, TB, syphilis, VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, toxoplasmosis, leprosy, phacoanaphylactic, fungal.

Non-granulomatous

  • Acute, recurrent; fine KPs, fibrin, hypopyon possible.
  • Causes: HLA-B27, Behçet, JIA, Fuchs, Posner–Schlossman, idiopathic.

Iris nodules: differential diagnosis

  • Koeppe and Busacca nodules, Lisch nodules (NF1), iris naevi and melanoma, juvenile xanthogranuloma, leprosy (iris pearls), Brushfield spots (Down syndrome), metastases.

Revise from: Iris Nodules and Spots: Koeppe, Busacca, Lisch and More, Granulomatous Uveitis: TB, Sarcoidosis and Syphilis

UV 4. Keratic precipitates, hypopyon and pseudohypopyon. DNB / UNIV · outline

Keratic precipitates (KPs)

  • Inflammatory cell deposits on the corneal endothelium, mostly inferiorly (Arlt triangle) due to convection currents.
  • Types: fine (non-granulomatous: lymphocytes), mutton-fat (granulomatous: macrophages, epithelioid cells), stellate diffuse (Fuchs uveitis, herpetic, CMV, toxoplasmosis), pigmented (old), coin-shaped (CMV endotheliitis).
  • Fresh KPs are white and round; old KPs are pigmented, shrunken and crenated (ghost KPs).

Hypopyon

  • Layered white cells in the AC; causes: severe anterior uveitis (HLA-B27, Behçet: mobile, shifting hypopyon), infective keratitis (fungal), endophthalmitis, masquerade (leukaemia, lymphoma, retinoblastoma: pseudohypopyon), drug-induced (rifabutin), TASS.

Pseudohypopyon

  • Layering of non-inflammatory material: tumour cells (retinoblastoma, leukaemia, lymphoma, melanoma), ghost cells, lens material (phacolytic), triamcinolone after injection, silicone oil (inverse hypopyon at the top).

Revise from: Uveitis OSCE Stations: 8 Spot Stations on KPs, Hypopyon, Synechiae, Toxoplasmosis, CMV, VKH and Biologic Safety

UV 5. Masquerade syndromes — primary intraocular lymphoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Masquerade syndromes

  • Conditions mimicking uveitis: neoplastic (vitreoretinal lymphoma, leukaemia, retinoblastoma, uveal melanoma, metastases, juvenile xanthogranuloma) and non-neoplastic (RD, IOFB, retinitis pigmentosa, pigment dispersion, ischaemic ocular syndrome, endophthalmitis, drug reactions).

Primary vitreoretinal lymphoma (PVRL)

  • Usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma; older adults; bilateral; part of primary CNS lymphoma (most develop CNS disease).
  • Features: vitreous cells in sheets or clumps (aurora borealis), creamy subretinal infiltrates, RPE disturbance (leopard spots), poor response to steroids; minimal macular oedema.
  • Diagnosis: diagnostic vitrectomy (cytology, flow cytometry, IL-10 to IL-6 ratio over 1, MYD88 L265P mutation, gene rearrangement), MRI brain, CSF cytology, OCT (subretinal hyper-reflective infiltrates).
  • Treatment: intravitreal methotrexate (400 µg) or rituximab, ocular radiotherapy, systemic high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy for CNS disease; poor long-term survival.

Revise from: Uveitis Masquerade Syndromes

UV 6. Investigative profile in uveitis. · outline

Investigative profile in uveitis

  • Targeted by history and anatomical type (SUN), not a “shotgun” panel.

Baseline

  • CBC, ESR, CRP, VDRL and TPHA, chest X-ray, Mantoux or IGRA, urine routine, blood sugar, liver and renal function (before immunosuppression).

Specific tests

  • HLA-B27 (acute anterior uveitis), HLA-A29 (birdshot), HLA-B51 (Behçet), ACE, lysozyme, serum calcium and HRCT chest (sarcoidosis), ANA (JIA), ANCA (vasculitis), toxoplasma, toxocara and Lyme serology, HIV, HSV and VZV serology (limited), sacroiliac X-ray or MRI.

Ocular investigations

  • OCT (CME), FFA (vasculitis, leakage, CME), ICGA (choroiditis: VKH, birdshot, serpiginous), FAF, B-scan (choroidal thickening, posterior scleritis), UBM (pars planitis).

Invasive

  • Aqueous tap (PCR: HSV, VZV, CMV, toxoplasma, TB; Goldmann–Witmer coefficient), vitreous biopsy (lymphoma, infection), chorioretinal biopsy.
UV 7. Differential diagnosis and management of bilateral panuveitis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Differential diagnosis of bilateral panuveitis

  • Non-infectious: VKH (serous RD, Indian and Asian patients), sympathetic ophthalmia (history of injury or surgery), sarcoid, Behçet (occlusive vasculitis, oral ulcers).
  • Infectious: TB (serpiginous-like choroiditis, vasculitis), syphilis (placoid lesions), toxoplasmosis (bilateral in immunosuppressed), endogenous endophthalmitis, viral (ARN).
  • Masquerade: vitreoretinal lymphoma (older patient, vitreous sheets, poor steroid response).

Workup

  • History (trauma, surgery, systemic features), serology (VDRL/TPHA, IGRA, ACE), chest imaging, FFA and ICGA (VKH: pinpoint leaks and dark dots), B-scan (choroidal thickening), OCT, vitreous biopsy if suspicion of lymphoma.

Management

  • Rule out infection first; high-dose steroids (oral or IV pulse) with early immunosuppression for VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, Behçet; specific antimicrobials for infections.

Revise from: Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada Disease, Sympathetic Ophthalmia: Recognition, Treatment and the Enucleation Question

Anterior & Intermediate Uveitis

UV 8. Acute anterior uveitis (iridocyclitis) — aetiology, HLA-B27 association, signs, complications and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Aetiology

  • Idiopathic (commonest), HLA-B27-associated (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, IBD, psoriatic arthritis), herpetic (HSV, VZV, CMV), JIA, sarcoidosis, syphilis, TB, Posner–Schlossman, drug-induced (rifabutin, bisphosphonates), post-operative and trauma.

HLA-B27 association

  • About 50% of acute anterior uveitis in Western populations (lower in India); unilateral, recurrent, alternating, acute onset; fibrin, hypopyon; men; associated with back pain.

Signs

  • Ciliary flush, AC cells and flare (SUN grading), fine KPs, fibrin, hypopyon, posterior synechiae, miosis, IOP usually low (or high in herpetic).

Complications

  • Posterior synechiae (iris bombé), cataract, glaucoma, CME, band keratopathy (chronic), hypotony.

Management

  • Intensive topical steroids (prednisolone acetate 1% hourly, tapered), cycloplegics (atropine or homatropine: relieve pain and break synechiae), subconjunctival mydriatics (mydricaine) for stubborn synechiae, periocular steroids for severe cases, systemic disease evaluation (rheumatology), steroid-sparing therapy (sulfasalazine, methotrexate, anti-TNF) for frequent recurrences.

Revise from: Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

UV 9. Anterior uveitis with raised IOP (hypertensive uveitis). DNB / UNIV · outline

Hypertensive anterior uveitis

  • Anterior uveitis with raised IOP (trabeculitis or blockage of outflow).

Causes

  • Herpetic (HSV, VZV: sectoral iris atrophy, unilateral), CMV anterior uveitis (Asian men, coin-shaped KPs, chronic: Posner–Schlossman-like), Posner–Schlossman syndrome (glaucomatocyclitic crisis), toxoplasmosis, Fuchs uveitis, sarcoidosis, rubella.

Investigation

  • Aqueous PCR for HSV, VZV, CMV (useful in recurrent unilateral cases).

Management

  • Topical steroids (careful), aqueous suppressants (avoid prostaglandins in active inflammation), antivirals: oral acyclovir or valacyclovir for HSV and VZV; topical ganciclovir 2% gel and oral valganciclovir for CMV; long-term prophylaxis for recurrences; surgery for uncontrolled IOP.
UV 10. Uveitis in spondyloarthropathies and reactive arthritis (Reiter syndrome). DNB / UNIV · outline

Spondyloarthropathies

  • HLA-B27-associated inflammatory arthritis: ankylosing spondylitis (sacroiliitis, bamboo spine), reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, enteropathic arthritis (Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis), undifferentiated.

Uveitis features

  • Acute, unilateral, recurrent, alternating anterior uveitis with fibrin and hypopyon; psoriatic and IBD uveitis may be bilateral and chronic, sometimes with posterior involvement.

Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter syndrome)

  • After genitourinary (Chlamydia) or enteric (Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter) infection; triad of arthritis, urethritis and conjunctivitis (mucopurulent, papillary); acute anterior uveitis, keratitis; skin lesions (keratoderma blennorrhagicum, circinate balanitis).

Management

  • Topical steroids and cycloplegics for uveitis; rheumatology for systemic disease (NSAIDs, sulfasalazine, methotrexate, anti-TNF agents reduce uveitis recurrences); treat infection in reactive arthritis.
UV 11. Intermediate uveitis and pars planitis — aetiopathogenesis, clinical features, complications and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Intermediate uveitis and pars planitis

  • Inflammation mainly in the vitreous and peripheral retina; pars planitis is the idiopathic form with snowbanks or snowballs.

Aetiopathogenesis

  • Idiopathic (most), sarcoid, MS (HLA-DR15), TB, Lyme, toxocara.

Clinical features

  • Floaters, blurred vision, mild anterior chamber reaction, vitreous cells and snowballs, inferior snowbank, peripheral periphlebitis.

Complications

  • CME (main cause of vision loss), cataract, glaucoma, ERM, vitreous haemorrhage, RD, neovascularisation.

Management (stepwise, Kaplan)

  • Treat if vision affected or CME: periocular or intravitreal steroids, oral steroids, immunosuppression (methotrexate, mycophenolate), biologics; peripheral laser or cryotherapy to the snowbank; pars plana vitrectomy for persistent inflammation or complications; MRI if MS suspected (avoid anti-TNF in MS).

Revise from: Intermediate Uveitis and Pars Planitis

UV 12. Heterochromic iridocyclitis (Fuchs uveitis syndrome). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Fuchs uveitis syndrome

  • Chronic, low-grade, usually unilateral anterior uveitis; often rubella virus associated.

Features

  • Heterochromia (affected iris lighter), diffuse small stellate KPs over the whole endothelium, no posterior synechiae, iris atrophy, Amsler sign (filiform haemorrhage on paracentesis), vitreous opacities.
  • Complications: cataract (posterior subcapsular), glaucoma.

Management

  • Usually no long-term steroids needed; good outcome with cataract surgery; treat glaucoma (can be difficult); vitrectomy for dense floaters.

Revise from: Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

UV 13. Short note: white iritis. · outline

White iritis

  • Anterior uveitis in a white (non-injected), quiet eye; chronic, insidious, often asymptomatic.

Causes

  • JIA-associated uveitis (classic: young girls, ANA positive; band keratopathy and cataract), Fuchs uveitis syndrome, chronic sarcoidosis, chronic idiopathic anterior uveitis, syphilis, CMV anterior uveitis.

Significance

  • Detected late with complications (cataract, glaucoma, band keratopathy, CME, synechiae); requires regular slit-lamp screening in children with JIA.

Management

  • Treat as chronic anterior uveitis: topical steroids, steroid-sparing immunosuppression (methotrexate, adalimumab) in JIA.
UV 14. Presentation, investigations and management of paediatric uveitis; Still's disease (JIA). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Presentation

  • Often asymptomatic (JIA) or with red eye, photophobia, white pupil, strabismus, poor vision; band keratopathy and cataract at presentation.

Causes

  • JIA (most common), idiopathic, pars planitis, TINU syndrome, toxocara, toxoplasmosis, Behçet, VKH, masquerades (retinoblastoma, leukaemia).

JIA (Still’s disease)

  • Oligoarticular, ANA-positive, young girls at highest risk; chronic, white-eye anterior uveitis.
  • Screening by slit lamp every 3 months in high-risk groups (age at onset, ANA, duration).

Investigations

  • ANA, RF, HLA-B27, CBC, ESR, urine (TINU), chest X-ray, toxocara serology.

Management

  • Topical steroids, methotrexate (first-line systemic), adalimumab (SYCAMORE trial), avoid long-term systemic steroids; treat amblyopia, band keratopathy (EDTA), cataract (cautious IOL use), glaucoma.

Revise from: JIA-Associated Uveitis: Screening Schedule and Treatment

UV 35. Uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema (UGH) syndrome. NEW · outline

Uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema (UGH) syndrome

  • Chafing of the iris, ciliary body or angle by an IOL (or its haptics) causing recurrent inflammation, raised IOP and bleeding; UGH plus when vitreous haemorrhage occurs.

Causes

  • Anterior chamber IOLs (closed-loop and poorly sized), single-piece acrylic IOLs placed in the sulcus (thick square-edged haptics), malpositioned or tilted IOLs, one haptic in the sulcus, iris-fixated lenses, pseudoexfoliation with IOL subluxation, cosmetic iris implants.

Features

  • Intermittent blurring (white-out episodes), recurrent microhyphaema, pigment in the AC, transillumination defects matching haptic position, raised IOP, CME.

Investigations

  • Gonioscopy (haptic in angle), UBM or AS-OCT (IOL–iris contact, haptic position), B-scan.

Management

  • Topical steroids, cycloplegics, IOP-lowering drugs; laser to bleeding vessels (rare).
  • Definitive: IOL repositioning (in the bag, sulcus with optic capture), exchange for a three-piece sulcus IOL or scleral-fixated IOL, or removal; glaucoma surgery if IOP stays high.

Revise from: Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

Posterior & Panuveitis

UV 15. Posterior uveitis — clinical features, aetiology, investigations and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Posterior uveitis

  • Inflammation of the retina and/or choroid (retinitis, choroiditis, retinochoroiditis, vasculitis, neuroretinitis).

Clinical features

  • Floaters, blurred vision, photopsia, scotomas; vitritis, focal or multifocal lesions, vasculitis, macular oedema, papillitis, exudative RD.

Aetiology

  • Infectious: toxoplasmosis (commonest), TB, syphilis, CMV, ARN, toxocara, fungal (candida), DUSN.
  • Non-infectious: Behçet, sarcoidosis, VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, birdshot, serpiginous, white dot syndromes, SLE.
  • Masquerade: lymphoma.

Investigations

  • As per phenotype: serology (toxoplasma, syphilis, TB), HIV, chest imaging, ACE, OCT, FFA, ICGA, aqueous or vitreous PCR, vitreous biopsy.

Management

  • Infection: specific antimicrobials ± steroids; non-infectious: systemic or local steroids, immunosuppression (antimetabolites, calcineurin inhibitors), biologics (adalimumab), steroid implants; treat complications (CNV with anti-VEGF, RD, glaucoma, cataract).
UV 16. Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada disease — sunset-glow fundus and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease

  • Autoimmune T-cell disease against melanocytes; pigmented races; bilateral granulomatous panuveitis with meningeal, auditory and skin signs.

Stages

  • Prodromal (headache, tinnitus, CSF pleocytosis), acute uveitic (bilateral serous RDs, disc hyperaemia, choroidal thickening), convalescent (sunset-glow fundus, Dalen–Fuchs nodules, vitiligo, poliosis, alopecia), chronic recurrent (anterior uveitis).

Sunset-glow fundus

  • Depigmentation of the choroid after inflammation; orange-red fundus; predicts chronicity.

Diagnosis

  • Revised international criteria (2001), FFA (pinpoint leaks, pooling), ICGA (dark dots), OCT (subretinal fluid, septae), B-scan (choroidal thickening).

Management

  • High-dose systemic steroids (IV pulse then oral, tapered over at least 6 months); early immunosuppression (cyclosporine, mycophenolate, adalimumab) reduces sunset glow and recurrences.

Revise from: Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada Disease

UV 17. Sympathetic ophthalmitis — pathogenesis, histopathology (Dalen-Fuchs nodules) and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Sympathetic ophthalmia

  • Bilateral granulomatous panuveitis after penetrating trauma or surgery (especially vitreoretinal) to one eye (exciting eye); the other is the sympathising eye; onset 2 weeks to years (90% within 1 year).

Pathogenesis

  • T-cell mediated autoimmune response to uveal melanocyte or retinal antigens (tyrosinase, S-antigen) exposed to the lymphatic system through the injury; HLA-DR4 association.

Histopathology

  • Diffuse granulomatous infiltration of the uvea (lymphocytes, epithelioid cells, giant cells containing pigment) sparing the choriocapillaris; Dalen–Fuchs nodules (collections of epithelioid cells between RPE and Bruch’s membrane); no necrosis.

Clinical features

  • Photophobia, blurred near vision (accommodation loss early), granulomatous anterior uveitis, vitritis, yellow-white choroidal lesions (Dalen–Fuchs), serous RD, papillitis; sunset-glow fundus later.

Management

  • Prevention: early primary repair; enucleation of a blind injured eye within 2 weeks (debated).
  • Treatment: high-dose systemic steroids (IV pulse then oral), early immunosuppression (cyclosporine, mycophenolate, azathioprine, adalimumab), intravitreal steroid implants; enucleation of the exciting eye after disease onset does not help (it may be the better-seeing eye).

Revise from: Sympathetic Ophthalmia: Recognition, Treatment and the Enucleation Question

UV 18. Ocular sarcoidosis — clinical features, diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Clinical features

  • Anterior granulomatous uveitis (mutton-fat KPs, iris nodules), intermediate uveitis (snowballs, string of pearls), periphlebitis (candle-wax drippings), multifocal choroiditis, optic nerve granulomas; lacrimal gland enlargement, conjunctival nodules.

Diagnosis (IWOS criteria)

  • Clinical signs plus investigations: chest X-ray (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy), HRCT, raised ACE and lysozyme, hypercalcaemia, abnormal liver enzymes, biopsy (non-caseating granulomas: conjunctiva, lacrimal gland, lymph node), gallium or PET.

Management

  • Topical, periocular or intravitreal steroids for ocular disease; systemic steroids for posterior disease; methotrexate, mycophenolate, anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab); involve a physician for systemic disease.

Revise from: Granulomatous Uveitis: TB, Sarcoidosis and Syphilis

UV 19. Behçet disease — aetiology, ocular features and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiology

  • Systemic vasculitis of unknown cause; HLA-B51; Silk Road countries; young men worse.

Diagnostic criteria (International Study Group)

  • Recurrent oral ulcers plus two of: genital ulcers, eye lesions, skin lesions (erythema nodosum), positive pathergy test.

Ocular features

  • Recurrent non-granulomatous anterior uveitis with mobile (shifting) hypopyon in a white eye; occlusive retinal vasculitis (veins and arteries), retinitis, vitreous haze, macular oedema, optic atrophy; explosive attacks.

Management

  • Acute: high-dose steroids. Long-term: azathioprine, cyclosporine; for sight-threatening disease, infliximab or adalimumab and interferon alfa-2a.
  • Avoid cyclosporine with neuro-Behçet; laser for neovascularisation.

Revise from: Behcet Disease: The Treatment Revolution

UV 20. White dot syndromes, including APMPPE. HIGH-YIELD · outline

White dot syndromes

  • Multiple evanescent white dot syndrome (MEWDS: unilateral, young women, foveal granularity, transient).
  • APMPPE (acute posterior multifocal placoid pigment epitheliopathy): young adults, bilateral, flu-like prodrome, creamy placoid lesions; FFA early hypo and late hyperfluorescence (block-early, stain-late); can be associated with cerebral vasculitis (headache: neuroimaging).
  • Serpiginous choroiditis (geographic spread from disc; rule out TB: serpiginous-like choroiditis).
  • Birdshot chorioretinopathy (HLA-A29, cream ovoid lesions).
  • Multifocal choroiditis and panuveitis, punctate inner choroidopathy (myopic women, CNV risk).

Management

  • Many self-limiting (MEWDS, APMPPE); steroids for macular involvement or vasculitis; immunosuppression for serpiginous and birdshot; anti-VEGF for CNV.

Revise from: White Dot Syndromes: MEWDS, APMPPE, Serpiginous, Birdshot and MCP

UV 21. Chorioretinitis. · outline

Chorioretinitis

  • Inflammation of the choroid and retina; may start in the retina (retinochoroiditis: toxoplasmosis) or choroid (choroidoretinitis: TB, syphilis).

Causes

  • Infections: toxoplasmosis, TB, syphilis, CMV, HSV and VZV (ARN), toxocara, candida, histoplasmosis (POHS), DUSN, onchocerciasis.
  • Non-infectious: sarcoidosis, serpiginous, birdshot, multifocal choroiditis, VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, white dot syndromes.

Features

  • Active: white or yellow fluffy lesions, vitritis, vasculitis, oedema; healed: atrophic scars with pigment.

Management

  • Determine cause; treat infection; anti-inflammatory therapy for immune causes; anti-VEGF for secondary CNV.
UV 22. Serpiginous choroiditis and birdshot chorioretinopathy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Serpiginous choroiditis

  • Bilateral, chronic recurrent inflammation of the inner choroid, RPE and choriocapillaris; middle-aged adults.
  • Features: grey-white geographic lesions spreading from the disc (helicoid) in a snake-like pattern; active edges; healed areas atrophic with pigment.
  • Serpiginous-like choroiditis: TB-associated in India (multifocal, posterior pole, with vitritis); test with IGRA and chest CT.
  • Imaging: FAF (hyperautofluorescent active edges), ICGA, FFA (early hypo, late hyperfluorescent edges).
  • Management: high-dose steroids with long-term immunosuppression (triple therapy: cyclosporine, azathioprine, prednisolone; mycophenolate); ATT with steroids in TB-related; anti-VEGF for CNV.

Birdshot chorioretinopathy

  • Bilateral, chronic posterior uveitis; HLA-A29 (over 95%); middle-aged Caucasians (rare in India).
  • Features: multiple cream ovoid choroidal lesions radiating from the disc (birdshot pattern), vitritis, retinal vasculitis, CME, optic disc oedema; nyctalopia, colour vision loss.
  • Investigations: ICGA (hypofluorescent spots, more than seen clinically), ERG (delayed 30 Hz flicker: monitor disease), visual fields, OCT.
  • Management: long-term immunosuppression (mycophenolate, cyclosporine, anti-TNF), local steroid implants.

Revise from: White Dot Syndromes: MEWDS, APMPPE, Serpiginous, Birdshot and MCP

Infectious Uveitis

UV 23. Ocular tuberculosis — pathogenesis, manifestations, differential diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathogenesis

  • Direct infection (choroidal tubercles, tuberculomas) or immune (hypersensitivity to mycobacterial antigens: Eales disease, serpiginous-like choroiditis).

Manifestations

  • Granulomatous anterior uveitis with broad synechiae, intermediate uveitis, choroidal tubercles and tuberculoma, serpiginous-like choroiditis (multifocal), retinal vasculitis (occlusive periphlebitis), neuroretinitis, scleritis, phlyctens, interstitial keratitis, orbital disease.

Differential diagnosis

  • Sarcoid, syphilis, VKH, serpiginous choroiditis, toxoplasmosis, fungal, lymphoma.

Diagnosis

  • Clinical phenotype plus IGRA or Mantoux, chest imaging (CT), aqueous PCR (low sensitivity); COTS-CON consensus guidelines on when to start ATT.

Management

  • Anti-tubercular therapy (4 drugs for at least 6 months, often 9–12 months) with systemic or local steroids to control inflammation; paradoxical worsening possible; immunosuppression after ATT cover if needed.

Revise from: Granulomatous Uveitis: TB, Sarcoidosis and Syphilis

UV 24. Ocular toxoplasmosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular toxoplasmosis

  • Toxoplasma gondii (cat is definitive host); congenital or acquired; commonest cause of posterior uveitis worldwide.

Features

  • Focal necrotising retinochoroiditis next to an old pigmented scar (“satellite lesion”); dense vitritis (“headlight in fog”); retinal vasculitis, Kyrieleis plaques; atypical in immunosuppressed (large, bilateral, no scar).

Diagnosis

  • Clinical; serology (IgG, IgM); aqueous PCR or Goldmann–Witmer coefficient in atypical cases.

Treatment

  • Indications: lesions near disc or macula, large lesions, severe vitritis, immunosuppressed, neonates.
  • Regimens: pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + folinic acid (classic), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, azithromycin, intravitreal clindamycin 1 mg with dexamethasone 400 µg; oral steroids added after 24–48 hours of antimicrobials.
  • Prophylaxis (TMP-SMX every 2–3 days) in frequent recurrences.
UV 25. Toxocariasis. · outline

Toxocariasis

  • Toxocara canis (dog roundworm) or T. cati; children with puppy exposure and pica (eating soil).

Clinical forms

  • Visceral larva migrans (young children, systemic: fever, hepatomegaly, eosinophilia; rarely ocular).
  • Ocular larva migrans: unilateral; chronic endophthalmitis (leukocoria: retinoblastoma mimic), posterior pole granuloma, peripheral granuloma with traction band to the disc (macular dragging), optic nerve granuloma.

Diagnosis

  • Clinical, ELISA serology (titres may be low), aqueous ELISA, B-scan (calcification absent: unlike retinoblastoma), CT, OCT.

Management

  • Steroids (periocular and systemic) for inflammation; albendazole (with steroids) in active disease; vitrectomy for traction RD, ERM, vitreous opacities; prevent with deworming pets and hand hygiene.
UV 26. Ophthalmic manifestations of Hansen's disease (leprosy). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Hansen’s disease (leprosy)

  • Mycobacterium leprae; eye involvement in lepromatous (direct invasion) and tuberculoid (nerve damage) forms.

Ocular manifestations

  • Lids: madarosis, lagophthalmos (facial nerve), trichiasis, entropion, ectropion.
  • Cornea: exposure keratopathy, corneal anaesthesia, beaded corneal nerves, interstitial keratitis, superficial avascular keratitis, leproma.
  • Sclera: episcleritis, scleritis, scleral nodules.
  • Uvea: chronic iridocyclitis with iris pearls, iris atrophy, miosis, cataract; acute uveitis in type 2 (ENL) reactions.

Management

  • WHO multidrug therapy; topical steroids and cycloplegics for uveitis; lubrication and lid surgery for lagophthalmos; protect the anaesthetic cornea; cataract surgery with care.

Revise from: Eye Disease in Leprosy: Exam, Care and Patient Education

UV 27. Ocular manifestations of HIV, the significance of CD4 count and the impact of HAART. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular manifestations of HIV

  • Adnexal: Kaposi sarcoma, molluscum, herpes zoster ophthalmicus (in young people: test for HIV), OSSN.
  • Retinal microvasculopathy: cotton-wool spots (commonest).
  • Opportunistic infections: CMV retinitis (CD4 < 50), toxoplasmosis (atypical), PORN (progressive outer retinal necrosis), ARN, syphilis, TB, cryptococcal choroiditis, pneumocystis choroidopathy.
  • Neuro-ophthalmic: cryptococcal meningitis (papilloedema), lymphoma.

CD4 count

  • Over 500: like immunocompetent; 200–500: HZO, TB, syphilis; under 200: toxoplasmosis, PCP; under 50: CMV, PORN, MAC.

Impact of HAART

  • CMV retinitis is far less common; immune recovery uveitis (vitritis, CME, ERM); need to continue CMV therapy until CD4 over 100–150 for 3–6 months.
UV 28. CMV retinitis — pathogenesis, fundus features, differential diagnosis and treatment. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathogenesis

  • Reactivation of latent CMV in severe immunosuppression (CD4 under 50, transplant, chemotherapy); haematogenous spread; full-thickness retinal necrosis.

Fundus features

  • Fulminant (“pizza pie” or “cottage cheese with ketchup”: haemorrhagic necrosis along vessels), indolent granular (peripheral, little haemorrhage), frosted branch angiitis; little vitritis.

Differential diagnosis

  • PORN and ARN (rapid, varicella), toxoplasmosis, syphilis, cotton-wool spots, candida.

Treatment

  • Induction: oral valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, or IV ganciclovir or foscarnet; intravitreal ganciclovir 2 mg or foscarnet 2.4 mg for sight-threatening zone 1 lesions.
  • Maintenance: valganciclovir 900 mg daily until immune recovery on ART.
  • Complications: RD (vitrectomy with silicone oil), immune recovery uveitis.

Revise from: CMV Retinitis: Recognition and Treatment in Immunocompromised Patients

UV 29. Acute retinal necrosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Acute retinal necrosis

  • VZV (older) and HSV (younger, HSV-2 in the very young); immunocompetent as well as immunosuppressed.

Criteria (American Uveitis Society)

  • One or more foci of retinal necrosis in the periphery, rapid circumferential spread, occlusive arteriolitis, prominent vitritis and anterior chamber inflammation.

Diagnosis

  • Clinical with aqueous or vitreous PCR (VZV, HSV, CMV).

Treatment

  • IV acyclovir 10 mg/kg three times daily for 7–14 days, or high-dose oral valacyclovir (2 g three times daily); intravitreal ganciclovir or foscarnet to speed control; oral steroids after antivirals start; aspirin (debated).
  • Prophylactic laser demarcation is debated; RD in up to 75%: vitrectomy with silicone oil.
  • Second eye involved in about one third without treatment.
UV 30. Ocular syphilis and post-fever retinitis (rickettsial, dengue, chikungunya). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular syphilis

  • “The great masquerader”; any form of uveitis; acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis, necrotising retinitis, vasculitis, optic neuritis, interstitial keratitis (congenital), Argyll Robertson pupils.
  • Tests: treponemal (TPHA, FTA-ABS) plus non-treponemal (VDRL, RPR); HIV test; lumbar puncture.
  • Treat as neurosyphilis: IV aqueous penicillin G for 10–14 days; steroids if needed.

Post-fever retinitis

  • 2–4 weeks after systemic febrile illness in South India and elsewhere: rickettsia (typhus), dengue (maculopathy, haemorrhages), chikungunya, typhoid, West Nile.
  • Features: retinitis patches, macular oedema, star, vasculitis, neuroretinitis.
  • Management: doxycycline for rickettsia, systemic steroids for inflammation once infection is treated; most recover with good vision.

Revise from: Granulomatous Uveitis: TB, Sarcoidosis and Syphilis

UV 31. Parasitic and tropical infestations — onchocerciasis, Loa loa, myiasis, rhinosporidiosis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Onchocerciasis (river blindness)

  • Onchocerca volvulus; transmitted by Simulium blackflies (rivers in Africa and Latin America).
  • Eye: live microfilariae in the AC and cornea, punctate keratitis (snowflake opacities), sclerosing keratitis, uveitis, chorioretinitis (atrophy), optic atrophy; skin nodules and dermatitis.
  • Treatment: ivermectin (mass drug administration: Mectizan), doxycycline (targets Wolbachia), nodulectomy.

Loa loa (eye worm)

  • Transmitted by Chrysops flies (West and Central Africa); adult worm migrates under the conjunctiva; Calabar swellings.
  • Treatment: surgical removal of worm, diethylcarbamazine (caution: encephalopathy with high microfilarial loads).

Ophthalmomyiasis

  • Infestation by fly larvae (Oestrus ovis: sheep nasal botfly); external (conjunctival larvae), internal (larvae in the eye, subretinal tracks).
  • Treatment: mechanical removal of larvae, topical anaesthetic and antibiotics; laser or vitrectomy for internal.

Rhinosporidiosis

  • Rhinosporidium seeberi (aquatic organism); endemic in South India and Sri Lanka (pond bathing); polypoid, strawberry-like, friable vascular mass on the conjunctiva or lacrimal sac.
  • Treatment: complete excision with cauterisation of the base; dapsone to reduce recurrence.
UV 36. Immune recovery uveitis. NEW · outline

Immune recovery uveitis (IRU)

  • Intraocular inflammation in patients with previous CMV retinitis whose immune function recovers on antiretroviral therapy (CD4 rises), as the immune system reacts to residual CMV antigens.

Features

  • Vitritis, CME (main cause of vision loss), ERM, anterior uveitis, cataract, papillitis, neovascularisation; occurs in eyes with large inactive CMV lesions or prior intravitreal ganciclovir implants.

Differential diagnosis

  • Reactivation of CMV retinitis, other infections (syphilis, TB, toxoplasma), drug-induced uveitis (rifabutin, cidofovir), lymphoma.

Management

  • Rule out active infection; periocular or intravitreal steroids (with valganciclovir cover in some cases), topical steroids, anti-VEGF for CME, vitrectomy for ERM; continue ART.

Revise from: CMV Retinitis: Recognition and Treatment in Immunocompromised Patients

Complications & Therapy

UV 32. Surgical management of complications of uveitis; cataract surgery in uveitis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Complications needing surgery

  • Cataract, glaucoma, band keratopathy, vitreous opacities, ERM, RD, hypotony, CME (refractory).

Cataract surgery in uveitis

  • Quiet eye for at least 3 months; pre-operative steroids (oral or periocular or topical); synechiolysis, pupil stretching, hooks or rings; thorough cortex removal; hydrophobic acrylic IOL in the bag (avoid in some JIA cases); intensive post-operative steroids, sometimes intravitreal dexamethasone implant at surgery.

Glaucoma surgery

  • Trabeculectomy with MMC (higher failure), drainage devices preferred in many; cyclophotocoagulation in refractory cases.

Others

  • EDTA chelation for band keratopathy; vitrectomy for vitreous opacities, diagnostic biopsy, ERM and RD.

Revise from: Cataract Surgery in Uveitis and Diabetes: Planning and Aftercare

UV 33. Post-uveitic cataract. · outline

Post-uveitic cataract

  • Usually posterior subcapsular; from chronic inflammation and steroid therapy; common in JIA, Fuchs uveitis, pars planitis, chronic anterior uveitis.

Pre-operative preparation

  • Quiet eye for at least 3 months; peri-operative steroids (oral prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg starting 1 week before, or periocular or intravitreal steroids); antivirals for herpetic uveitis; control IOP; assess macula (OCT for CME) and posterior segment (B-scan); realistic counselling.

Surgical challenges and techniques

  • Small pupil and synechiae: synechiolysis, iris hooks, Malyugin ring.
  • Pupillary membranes and fibrosis; zonular weakness; thorough cortical clean-up.
  • IOL choice: hydrophobic acrylic in the bag; avoid silicone and AC IOLs; aphakia sometimes in JIA (selected cases).
  • Intraoperative intracameral or intravitreal dexamethasone (implant) in high-risk cases.

Post-operative

  • Intensive topical steroids with slow taper, NSAIDs (CME), IOP monitoring, early YAG for PCO, management of flare-ups.
UV 34. Biologics and local steroid implants (fluocinolone, dexamethasone) in non-infectious uveitis. · outline

Biologics in non-infectious uveitis

  • Adalimumab (anti-TNF): approved for non-infectious intermediate, posterior and panuveitis (VISUAL I: reduced treatment failure in active disease; VISUAL II: in inactive disease), JIA uveitis (SYCAMORE, ADJUVITE); subcutaneous 80 mg then 40 mg every 2 weeks.
  • Infliximab (IV anti-TNF): Behçet uveitis, refractory uveitis.
  • Tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor): refractory uveitic CME, JIA uveitis.
  • Rituximab, interferon alfa-2a (Behçet), JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib in JIA uveitis), abatacept.
  • Screening: TB (IGRA, chest X-ray), hepatitis B and C, HIV, vaccines; avoid anti-TNF in MS and demyelination.

Local steroid implants

  • Dexamethasone 0.7 mg implant (Ozurdex): biodegradable, about 3–6 months; HURON trial (intermediate and posterior uveitis); uveitic CME.
  • Fluocinolone acetonide implants: 0.59 mg (Retisert, surgically sutured, about 2.5 years; MUST trial: similar vision to systemic therapy, more cataract and glaucoma), 0.19 mg (Iluvien) and 0.18 mg (Yutiq: injectable, about 3 years; reduced recurrences).
  • Suprachoroidal triamcinolone (Xipere) for uveitic macular oedema (PEACHTREE).
  • Side effects: cataract, raised IOP, endophthalmitis; useful in unilateral disease or when systemic therapy is unsuitable.

Revise from: Uveitis OSCE Stations: 8 Spot Stations on KPs, Hypopyon, Synechiae, Toxoplasmosis, CMV, VKH and Biologic Safety, Eye Steroids and Infection Regimens: Potency, Tapers and Antiviral Doses

UV 37. Drug-induced uveitis — rifabutin, bisphosphonates, immune checkpoint inhibitors and intravitreal agents. NEW · outline

Drug-induced uveitis

  • Rifabutin (dose-related; hypopyon anterior uveitis, with clarithromycin or fluconazole raising levels).
  • Bisphosphonates (zoledronate, alendronate): anterior uveitis, scleritis, orbital inflammation.
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ipilimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab): anterior uveitis, VKH-like panuveitis, optic neuritis, orbital inflammation, myasthenia.
  • MEK and BRAF inhibitors: uveitis, serous retinopathy (MEK-associated retinopathy).
  • Intravitreal agents: brolucizumab (retinal vasculitis and occlusion), pegcetacoplan (occlusive vasculitis), cidofovir (hypotony and uveitis), triamcinolone (pseudo-endophthalmitis), vancomycin (HORV).
  • Others: sulfonamides, topical prostaglandin analogues and brimonidine (anterior uveitis), vaccines (BCG, influenza, COVID-19: rare), fluoroquinolones (bilateral acute iris transillumination, BAIT), dabrafenib, TNF inhibitors (paradoxical sarcoid-like).

Management

  • Recognise timing; stop or adjust drug when possible (discuss with oncologist); topical or periocular steroids; systemic steroids for severe disease; most resolve.

Revise from: Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes

UV 38. Uveitic macular oedema — management (MERIT trial; local vs systemic therapy). NEW · outline

Uveitic macular oedema (UME)

  • Commonest cause of vision loss in uveitis; seen in intermediate uveitis, birdshot, panuveitis and after cataract surgery.

Assessment

  • OCT (central subfield thickness, cysts, subretinal fluid, ERM), FFA (leakage); control inflammation first.

Treatment options

  • Topical NSAIDs and difluprednate (mild, pseudophakic).
  • Periocular triamcinolone (sub-Tenon or orbital floor), intravitreal triamcinolone, dexamethasone implant, fluocinolone implants, suprachoroidal triamcinolone (PEACHTREE).
  • MERIT trial (2023): for persistent or recurrent UME, the intravitreal dexamethasone implant reduced macular thickness more than intravitreal methotrexate or ranibizumab at 12 weeks; methotrexate and ranibizumab were less effective but caused less IOP rise.
  • Systemic therapy: oral steroids, acetazolamide, immunosuppression (methotrexate, mycophenolate), biologics (adalimumab; tocilizumab for refractory UME), interferon alpha in refractory cases.
  • MUST trial: implant vs systemic therapy: comparable long-term vision; implant caused more cataract and glaucoma.
  • Vitrectomy with membrane peel for ERM or vitreous traction.

Revise from: Uveitis Classification: SUN Criteria and Causes, Intermediate Uveitis and Pars Planitis
