Clinical Optics Made Easy: Vergence, Prisms and Magnification

Last updated: October 2026

Optics frightens residents because it is taught as a page of formulas. In practice almost every clinical optics question comes back to a handful of ideas: vergence, the far point, prisms, accommodation and magnification. Learn these and the formulas become common sense.

Five ideas

1. Everything is vergence: U + P = VVergence is 1 ÷ distance in metres (dioptres). Light from a real object diverges (negative), a lens adds its power, and the result tells you where the image forms. An object at 25 cm has a vergence of −4 D.
2. Correct the far pointA myope sees clearly only out to the far point, 1/D metres in front (−2 D → 50 cm). The correct lens has its focal point at the far point. Moving a lens changes this: a plus lens moved away from the eye becomes stronger, a minus lens becomes weaker.
3. Prisms bend light to the base, images to the apexOne prism dioptre moves the image 1 cm at 1 m. Prentice's rule: prism (Δ) = decentration (cm) × lens power (D). 1° is about 1.75 Δ.
4. Accommodation shrinks with age; keep half in reserveA comfortable near add = the near demand minus half the amplitude of accommodation.
5. Magnification depends on where the lens sitsSpectacles magnify (plus) or minify (minus) by about 1.5–2% per dioptre; contact lenses hardly change image size. Simple magnifier: M = F ÷ 4.

Effective power and vertex distance

When a lens moves towards the eye by d metres, its new power is F ÷ (1 − dF). If it moves away, use + d. It matters above about ±4 D: a −10.00 D spectacle lens at 12 mm becomes about −8.90 D at the cornea, so the contact lens is weaker for a myope and stronger for a hyperope.

Memory hook: Contact lens for a myope: minus less. Contact lens for a hyperope: higher plus.

Astigmatism in three sentences

  • A cylinder has no power along its axis and full power at 90° to it. Light through an astigmatic system forms two focal lines (the conoid of Sturm) with the circle of least confusion at the spherical equivalent.
  • With-the-rule: the vertical meridian is steepest (plus cyl at 90 or minus cyl at 180), common in the young. Against-the-rule is common in older people.
  • Transposition: add the cylinder to the sphere, change the cylinder's sign, rotate the axis by 90°. −1.00/+2.00 × 90 = +1.00/−2.00 × 180.

Memory hook: The plus-cylinder axis lies along the steep meridian. Jackson cross-cylinder: refine the axis first, then the power; keep the spherical equivalent constant by changing the sphere half as much in the opposite direction.

Worked example: a 50-year-old emmetrope (amplitude 2.5 D) wants to read at 40 cm (demand 2.5 D). Keep half (1.25 D) in reserve, so the add is 2.5 − 1.25 = +1.25 D. A higher add brings the reading distance closer and narrows the range.

Prisms in practice

  • Prentice's rule: 5 mm decentration of a +6 D lens gives 0.5 × 6 = 3 Δ.
  • Base direction for a decentred lens: plus lenses act like two prisms base to base, so decentring a plus lens in gives base-in prism; minus lenses give the opposite.
  • Correcting a deviation: the apex points the way the eye turns: esotropia → base-out, exotropia → base-in, right hypertropia → base-down over the right eye.
  • Vertical imbalance in anisometropic bifocal wearers when reading: fix with slab-off (bicentric grinding) on the more minus lens.

Magnification and aniseikonia

  • Knapp's law: in axial ametropia, a spectacle lens at the anterior focal point of the eye (about 15 mm) gives a retinal image the same size as an emmetrope's. So axial anisometropia tolerates glasses; refractive anisometropia (aphakia, post-keratoplasty) does better with contact lenses.
  • Most people tolerate about 5% aniseikonia (roughly 2–3 D of spectacle anisometropia).
  • Low vision: magnification needed = target acuity ÷ current acuity, as decimals (from 6/60 to 6/12 is 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5×). A +20 D magnifier gives 5×.
  • Telescopes: M = − eyepiece power ÷ objective power. Galilean (plus objective, minus eyepiece) is short with an upright image; Keplerian (two plus lenses) is longer and needs a prism to turn the image upright.

Where residents slip

  • Forgetting the working distance in retinoscopy: subtract 1.50 D at 67 cm.
  • Mixing up which way the image moves through a prism: the light bends to the base, the image shifts to the apex.
  • Using the full amplitude of accommodation to calculate a reading add: keep half in reserve.
  • Ignoring vertex distance above ±4 D when converting glasses to contact lenses.
  • Giving glasses to a high refractive anisometrope (unilateral aphakia): the aniseikonia is too large; use a contact lens or IOL.
