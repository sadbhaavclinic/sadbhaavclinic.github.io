MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Strabismus and Paediatric Ophthalmology (48 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 48 strabismus and paediatric ophthalmology theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Anatomy of Extraocular Muscles

SQ 1. Describe the origin, insertion, actions, functional anatomy and development of the extraocular muscles; discuss the basis of restrictive squints. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Origin and insertion

  • Four recti arise from the annulus of Zinn; insert at increasing distances from the limbus (spiral of Tillaux): MR 5.5 mm, IR 6.5 mm, LR 6.9 mm, SR 7.7 mm.
  • Superior oblique: from the lesser wing of the sphenoid, through the trochlea, inserts posterolateral behind the equator.
  • Inferior oblique: from the orbital floor near the lacrimal fossa, inserts posterolateral near the macula.

Actions (primary, secondary, tertiary)

  • MR adduction; LR abduction; SR elevation, intorsion, adduction; IR depression, extorsion, adduction; SO intorsion, depression, abduction; IO extorsion, elevation, abduction.

Functional anatomy

  • Pulleys (connective tissue sleeves) act as functional origins; Sherrington law (reciprocal innervation) and Hering law (equal innervation to yoke muscles); fascia bulbi and check ligaments.

Development

  • From cranial mesoderm and neural crest (connective tissue); differentiated by the 3rd month.

Basis of restrictive squints

  • Mechanical limitation: fibrosis (thyroid eye disease, CFEOM), entrapment (blow-out fracture), tight muscle or tendon (Brown syndrome), scarring after surgery; positive forced duction test; IOP rise on attempted gaze.

Revise from: Orbital Anatomy Made Easy: Walls, Fissures and Muscles, Ocular Motility Made Easy: Muscle Actions, Palsies, Three-Step

SQ 2. Annulus of Zinn. · outline

Annulus of Zinn (common tendinous ring)

  • Fibrous ring at the orbital apex surrounding the optic canal and the central part of the superior orbital fissure; origin of the four recti.

Structures passing through the annulus

  • Optic canal part: optic nerve, ophthalmic artery.
  • Oculomotor foramen (SOF part): superior and inferior divisions of the third nerve, nasociliary nerve, sixth nerve, sympathetic root of the ciliary ganglion.

Structures outside (above) the annulus

  • Lacrimal, frontal and trochlear nerves, superior ophthalmic vein.

Clinical relevance

  • Pain on eye movement in optic neuritis (dura and recti origin), orbital apex syndrome, retrobulbar anaesthesia (muscle cone), thyroid eye disease (compressive optic neuropathy at the apex), intraconal tumours.
SQ 3. Superior oblique — anatomy, actions in various gazes, and management of unilateral and bilateral palsy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Anatomy

  • Longest and thinnest muscle; tendon passes through the trochlea and turns posterolaterally at 51–54° to the visual axis; supplied by the contralateral fourth nerve.

Actions in different gazes

  • Primary: intorsion, depression, abduction; depression greatest in adduction; intorsion greatest in abduction.

Unilateral palsy

  • Hypertropia worse on contralateral gaze and ipsilateral head tilt (Parks three-step test); excyclotorsion, head tilt to the opposite shoulder, face turn and chin down; congenital (large fusional vertical amplitudes, facial asymmetry) or acquired (trauma, microvascular).
  • Management: observe acquired for 6 months; prisms; surgery by Knapp classification: IO weakening (commonest), SO tuck, contralateral IR recession; Harada–Ito for torsion.

Bilateral palsy

  • V-pattern esotropia, excyclotorsion over 10° (double Maddox rod), reversing hypertropia on side gaze, chin-down posture; often after head trauma.
  • Management: bilateral Harada–Ito, bilateral IO weakening, inferior rectus recessions as needed.

Revise from: Ocular Motility Made Easy: Muscle Actions, Palsies, Three-Step, Third, Fourth & Sixth Nerve Palsies

SQ 4. Inferior oblique — anatomy and surgery. DNB / UNIV · outline

Inferior oblique anatomy

  • Origin: orbital floor just lateral to the nasolacrimal canal (only muscle not arising from the apex).
  • Course: backwards and laterally under the inferior rectus, to insert on the posterolateral globe near the macula (inferior temporal quadrant, about 2 mm from the macula and close to the inferotemporal vortex vein).
  • Nerve: inferior division of the third nerve (with parasympathetic fibres to ciliary ganglion nearby).
  • Actions: extorsion (primary), elevation and abduction (most elevation in adduction).

Overaction

  • Primary (idiopathic) or secondary (SO palsy); V pattern, upshoot in adduction; DVD must be distinguished.

Surgery

  • Weakening: recession (Fink, Parks: 10 mm), anterior transposition (also for DVD: anti-elevation), myectomy, disinsertion, denervation and extirpation (severe).
  • Strengthening (rare): IO tuck or advancement.
  • Complications: anti-elevation syndrome, fat adherence syndrome (violating Tenon), vortex vein bleeding, incomplete hooking, macular damage (very rare), adherence.

Revise from: Anti-Elevation Syndrome after Inferior Oblique Surgery

SQ 5. Hering's and Sherrington's laws; sequelae of paralytic squint over time. DNB / UNIV · outline

Hering’s law of equal innervation

  • Yoke muscles (in the two eyes) receive equal and simultaneous innervation during conjugate movements.
  • Explains secondary deviation (larger when fixing with the paretic eye) and over-action of the contralateral yoke muscle.

Sherrington’s law of reciprocal innervation

  • Increased innervation to an agonist is accompanied by reduced innervation to its antagonist in the same eye.
  • Basis of Duane retraction (co-contraction when violated), and of surgical planning.

Sequelae of paralytic squint over time

  • Weakness of the paretic muscle, overaction of the ipsilateral antagonist (contracture), overaction of the contralateral yoke (Hering), inhibitional palsy of the contralateral antagonist; spread of comitance; head posture; later diagnosis of the original muscle becomes difficult (Hess chart helps).

Revise from: Laws in Ophthalmology: Hering to Poiseuille in Tables

Binocular Vision & Sensory Tests

SQ 6. Anatomical and physiological basis of binocular vision — grades, development, horopter and Panum's area; its comprehensive assessment and tests for stereopsis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Binocular single vision

  • Simultaneous perception of an image by both eyes and fusion into a single image with depth.

Grades (Worth)

  • Grade 1 simultaneous macular perception, grade 2 fusion (with amplitude), grade 3 stereopsis.

Anatomical and physiological basis

  • Overlapping visual fields, partial decussation at the chiasm, binocular cells in the visual cortex, retinal correspondence, motor co-ordination; requires clear images, aligned eyes and intact neural pathways.

Development

  • Fixation by 6 weeks; fusion and stereopsis develop by 3–6 months; sensitive period until about 7–8 years.

Horopter and Panum’s area

  • Horopter: locus of points in space imaged on corresponding retinal points (Vieth–Müller circle; empirical horopter flatter).
  • Panum’s fusional area: zone around the horopter where non-corresponding points still fuse (gives stereopsis); objects outside cause physiological diplopia.

Assessment

  • Synoptophore (grades), Worth four-dot, Bagolini, 4-prism dioptre base-out test, fusional amplitudes.
  • Stereopsis: TNO (random-dot), Titmus fly, Lang, Randot, Frisby; in seconds of arc (normal about 40–60 s).

Revise from: Orthoptic Tests Made Easy: Cover Test, Hess Chart, Synoptophore, Worth 4-Dot and Stereopsis

SQ 7. What are the sensory adaptations in strabismus (suppression, ARC)? Discuss their diagnosis and clinical significance. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Sensory adaptations in strabismus (to avoid diplopia and confusion)

  • Suppression: cortical inhibition of the image from the deviating eye; central or peripheral, alternating or constant.
  • Abnormal retinal correspondence (ARC): fovea of the fixing eye corresponds with a peripheral point of the deviating eye; harmonious or unharmonious.
  • Amblyopia and eccentric fixation.

Diagnosis

  • Suppression: Worth four-dot, Bagolini, 4-prism dioptre base-out test, red filter.
  • ARC: Bagolini (single image with manifest squint), after-image test (Hering–Bielschowsky), synoptophore (subjective and objective angle differ), Worth four-dot.

Clinical significance

  • ARC and deep suppression explain absence of diplopia and give poor binocular prognosis; risk of post-operative diplopia after correction in adults with ARC (do a prism adaptation or diplopia test); treat amblyopia before surgery.

Revise from: Orthoptic Tests Made Easy: Cover Test, Hess Chart, Synoptophore, Worth 4-Dot and Stereopsis

SQ 8. Worth four-dot test. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Worth four-dot test

  • Patient wears red-green glasses (red over right eye) and views four lights: one red, two green, one white.

Interpretation

  • Four lights: normal fusion (or ARC with manifest squint).
  • Two red: suppression of the left eye; three green: suppression of the right eye.
  • Five lights: diplopia (red right of green = esotropia, uncrossed; red left of green = exotropia, crossed).
  • Alternating 2 and 3: alternating suppression.

Notes

  • Near (33 cm) tests central fusion; distance (6 m) tests peripheral; dissociative test, so less natural than Bagolini.

Revise from: Orthoptic Tests Made Easy: Cover Test, Hess Chart, Synoptophore, Worth 4-Dot and Stereopsis

SQ 9. Method and significance of Bagolini striated glasses test. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Bagolini striated glasses

  • Plano lenses with fine striations at 45° and 135°; a point light appears as an oblique line; least dissociating test of binocularity.

Results

  • Perfect X (cross): normal fusion if no manifest deviation (or harmonious ARC with squint).
  • One line only: suppression.
  • Cross with a gap in one line: central suppression scotoma (microtropia, monofixation).
  • Two lines not crossing at centre: diplopia (esotropia or exotropia by position).

Significance

  • Tests retinal correspondence in casual seeing conditions; useful in microtropia, ARC and before surgery.

Revise from: Orthoptic Tests Made Easy: Cover Test, Hess Chart, Synoptophore, Worth 4-Dot and Stereopsis

SQ 10. Cover tests; Maddox rod and wing; Hess/Lees screen and diplopia charting. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Cover tests

  • Cover-uncover: detects tropia (manifest) by movement of the uncovered eye.
  • Alternate cover: shows the total deviation (tropia plus phoria); with prisms (PCT) measures the angle.
  • Simultaneous prism cover: measures manifest deviation only (microtropia, monofixation).

Maddox rod

  • Red striated lens converts a spot light into a line; measures horizontal and vertical phorias; double Maddox rod for torsion.

Maddox wing

  • Near (33 cm) measurement of horizontal, vertical and cyclo deviations by scale and arrows.

Hess or Lees screen

  • Dissociated charting of eye positions in 9 gazes (red-green goggles or mirror); shows under-acting and over-acting muscles; smaller field = paretic eye; serial charts track recovery.

Diplopia charting

  • Red glass in front of the right eye, patient fixes a light in 9 gazes; separation greatest in the field of action of the paretic muscle; peripheral image belongs to the paretic eye.

Revise from: Orthoptic Tests Made Easy: Cover Test, Hess Chart, Synoptophore, Worth 4-Dot and Stereopsis, Strabismus Measurement and Surgical Dose Tables: Quick Reference

SQ 11. AC/A ratio — measurement and importance. DNB / UNIV · outline

AC/A ratio

  • Amount of accommodative convergence (prism dioptres) per dioptre of accommodation; normal 3–5:1.

Measurement

  • Heterophoria method: AC/A = PD (in cm) + (near deviation − distance deviation)/accommodation (in dioptres at near, e.g. 3 D at 33 cm); esodeviations positive.
  • Gradient method: change in deviation at near with plus or minus lenses (e.g. +3 D or −1 D) divided by lens power; measures the true AC/A (no proximal convergence influence).
  • Clinical comparison of near and distance deviations.

Importance

  • High AC/A: convergence excess esotropia (bifocals, MR recession with posterior fixation), intermittent exotropia with high AC/A (pseudo-divergence excess); patch test.
  • Low AC/A: convergence insufficiency, divergence insufficiency.
  • Helps choose spectacles (bifocals), surgery (amount and type), and understand accommodative esotropia.
SQ 12. Eccentric fixation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Eccentric fixation

  • Uniocular condition in which a non-foveal retinal point is used for fixation by the amblyopic eye under monocular viewing.

Types

  • By location: parafoveal, macular, paramacular, peripheral; nasal (commonest with esotropia) or temporal.
  • Steady or unsteady.

Diagnosis

  • Visuscope (projected star onto the fundus), direct ophthalmoscope fixation target, Haidinger brushes, after-image test (Bielschowsky).

Significance

  • Poor visual prognosis in amblyopia; relation to ARC (with identity in microtropia).

Management

  • Conventional occlusion (preferred), inverse occlusion (historical, for eccentric fixation), pleoptics (Bangerter, Cüppers: historical), red filter therapy.
SQ 13. Microtropia — definition, types and features. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Microtropia

  • Small manifest strabismus (under 10 prism dioptres) with ARC and good binocular function (gross stereopsis); also called monofixation syndrome (Parks).

Types

  • With identity (eccentric fixation point coincides with the point of ARC: no movement on cover test), without identity (small movement on cover), primary or secondary (after squint surgery).

Features

  • Anisometropia commonly, mild amblyopia, central suppression scotoma (4-prism base-out test, Bagolini gap), reduced stereopsis (about 200–3000 seconds of arc), fusional amplitudes preserved.

Management

  • Correct refraction, treat amblyopia; no surgery.

Esodeviations

SQ 14. Infantile (congenital) esotropia — features, differential diagnosis, timing of surgery, protocols of treatment and binocular prognosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Features

  • Onset before 6 months; large constant angle (over 30 prism dioptres); cross-fixation, low hyperopia, latent nystagmus, DVD and IO over-action, pseudo abduction limitation, asymmetric OKN.

Differential diagnosis

  • Accommodative esotropia (early onset), sixth nerve palsy, Duane syndrome, Möbius syndrome, nystagmus blockage syndrome, sensory esotropia (exclude ocular disease), pseudo-esotropia (epicanthus).

Timing of surgery

  • Early (before 18–24 months, some as early as 6 months) gives better chances of gross binocularity (ELISSS, Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group studies).

Treatment protocols

  • Full cycloplegic refraction, amblyopia treatment and alternation, bilateral MR recession (or recess-resect), botulinum toxin as an alternative, treat IO over-action and DVD.

Binocular prognosis

  • Fine stereopsis rare; peripheral fusion (monofixation) achievable with early alignment.

Revise from: Esotropia and Exotropia: Types, Tests and Surgery, Landmark Trials in Paediatric Ophthalmology: CRYO-ROP, ETROP, BEAT-ROP, PEDIG, IATS, LAMP and CHAMP

SQ 15. Accommodative and partially accommodative esotropia. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Refractive accommodative esotropia

  • Onset 2–3 years; moderate hyperopia (+2 to +6 D); normal AC/A ratio; corrected fully by glasses.

Non-refractive (high AC/A)

  • Esotropia greater at near than distance; normal or low hyperopia; bifocals (executive), miotics, or MR recession (with or without posterior fixation).

Partially accommodative

  • Residual esotropia despite full correction: surgery for the residual (non-accommodative) part.

Management principles

  • Full cycloplegic refraction (cyclopentolate or atropine), full spectacle correction, treat amblyopia, review regularly, reduce plus slowly if needed; surgery only for the residual deviation.

Revise from: Esotropia and Exotropia: Types, Tests and Surgery

SQ 16. Classification of esotropia; investigations and management in a 1-year-old and in a 3-6-year-old child. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification of esotropia

  • Infantile; accommodative (refractive, non-refractive, partially accommodative); acquired non-accommodative (basic, acute, cyclic); incomitant (sixth nerve palsy, Duane, Möbius); sensory; consecutive; secondary to nystagmus (nystagmus blockage).

Investigations

  • Cycloplegic refraction, cover test and prism measurement (near, distance, side gaze), versions and ductions, fundus, binocular tests, AC/A, neuro-imaging for acute or neurological cases.

One-year-old child

  • Likely infantile esotropia: correct hyperopia over +2 D, treat amblyopia, early surgery (bilateral MR recession) or botulinum toxin; manage DVD and IO over-action later.

Three- to six-year-old child

  • Likely accommodative or acquired: full cycloplegic refraction, glasses, amblyopia therapy, bifocals for high AC/A, surgery for residual deviation; exclude neurological cause (acute esotropia, sixth nerve palsy: MRI).

Revise from: Esotropia and Exotropia: Types, Tests and Surgery

SQ 17. Non-surgical management of esotropia. · outline

Non-surgical management of esotropia

  • Full cycloplegic refractive correction (accommodative and partially accommodative esotropia).
  • Bifocals or progressive add (+2.5 to +3 D) for high AC/A convergence excess.
  • Amblyopia treatment (occlusion, atropine penalisation) before surgery.
  • Miotics (echothiophate, pilocarpine: historical) to reduce accommodative effort.
  • Prisms (base-out) for small deviations, decompensated phorias, and in adults with diplopia; Fresnel prisms.
  • Botulinum toxin to the medial rectus (infantile esotropia, acute acquired comitant esotropia, sixth nerve palsy).
  • Orthoptic exercises (fusional divergence) for some decompensating phorias.
  • Treat underlying conditions (neurological imaging in acute acquired esotropia; limit near screen work).
SQ 18. Pseudostrabismus and epicanthus. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pseudostrabismus

  • Appearance of squint without true misalignment (cover test normal).

Pseudo-esotropia

  • Broad nasal bridge and epicanthal folds (covering nasal sclera), small interpupillary distance, negative angle kappa, facial asymmetry.

Pseudo-exotropia

  • Wide interpupillary distance, positive angle kappa (ROP-related macular dragging, high hyperopia), hypertelorism.

Pseudohypertropia

  • Facial or orbital asymmetry, ptosis.

Epicanthus

  • Vertical skin fold over the medial canthus; types: palpebralis (commonest), tarsalis (East Asian), inversus (blepharophimosis), supraciliaris; usually reduces with growth of the nasal bridge.

Management

  • Reassure after a careful cover test and cycloplegic refraction; follow-up because true strabismus may coexist; surgery (Mustardé double Z, Y-V plasty) for persistent epicanthus.

Exodeviations & Heterophoria

SQ 19. Primary exodeviation — classification, work-up and management; intermittent exotropia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Classification of exodeviation

  • Exophoria, intermittent exotropia (commonest childhood exotropia; X(T)), constant exotropia (infantile, sensory, consecutive), convergence insufficiency.
  • Intermittent exotropia by distance-near relationship (Burian): basic, divergence excess (true or simulated: patch test), convergence insufficiency type.

Work-up

  • History and photographs, frequency of manifest phase (Newcastle Control Score, office control score), cover test at near and distance (6 m and far distance), patch test (1 hour occlusion), +3 D lens test (AC/A), refraction, stereopsis, fusional amplitudes, amblyopia.

Management

  • Observation if well controlled (PEDIG: many do not worsen), correct refraction, overminus lenses (−1 to −2.5 D: small benefit), part-time occlusion, orthoptic exercises (convergence insufficiency type), prisms.
  • Surgery for poor or worsening control: bilateral LR recession or unilateral recess-resect (PEDIG: similar outcomes), aim for small initial overcorrection; slanted recessions for divergence excess; long-term recurrence common.

Revise from: Newcastle Control Score for Intermittent Exotropia

SQ 20. Heterophoria — aetiology, diagnosis and treatment. DNB / UNIV · outline

Heterophoria

  • Latent deviation controlled by fusion; manifests when fusion is broken (cover test).

Aetiology

  • Anatomical (orbital shape, interpupillary distance, muscle insertions), refractive (uncorrected hyperopia: esophoria; myopia: exophoria), accommodative (AC/A abnormal), poor fusional reserves, fatigue, ageing, illness.

Symptoms (decompensation)

  • Asthenopia, headache, blurring, intermittent diplopia, difficulty reading, closing one eye.

Diagnosis

  • Alternate cover test, prism cover test, Maddox rod and wing, fusional amplitudes (prism bar, synoptophore), near point of convergence, Sheard and Percival criteria.

Treatment

  • Correct refractive error; orthoptic exercises (convergence and fusional training); prisms (relieving: about one-third of the deviation); surgery for large symptomatic phorias.
SQ 21. Convergence insufficiency. DNB / UNIV · outline

Convergence insufficiency (CI)

  • Exophoria greater at near, receded near point of convergence (over 6–10 cm), reduced positive fusional vergence at near, low AC/A.

Symptoms

  • Eye strain, headache, blurring and diplopia with reading, words moving, poor concentration (CISS questionnaire).

Causes

  • Idiopathic (commonest), head injury, Parkinson disease, myasthenia, presbyopia correction, prolonged screen use.

Management

  • Office-based vergence and accommodative therapy with home reinforcement (CITT: most effective in children), home pencil push-ups and computer exercises (less effective), base-in prisms for reading (adults), correct refraction (avoid under-correcting myopia), surgery rarely (MR resection or plication).

Revise from: Convergence Insufficiency and Convergence Excess: Diagnosis, Treatment and Exercises

Vertical, Pattern & Special Forms

SQ 22. Aetiology, diagnosis and management of superior oblique overaction. · outline

Superior oblique overaction (SOOA)

  • Excessive depression in adduction with intorsion; A pattern exotropia or esotropia.

Aetiology

  • Primary (idiopathic, associated with horizontal strabismus and craniofacial anomalies), secondary (contralateral inferior rectus palsy, ipsilateral IO palsy, Brown syndrome after surgery).

Diagnosis

  • Versions (downshoot in adduction, graded +1 to +4), A pattern (measure in up and down gaze: difference of 10 prism dioptres or more), fundus intorsion, Hess chart.

Management

  • Weakening of the SO tendon: tenotomy or tenectomy (risk of SO palsy), silicone tendon expander (Wright), split tendon lengthening, posterior tenectomy, recession; avoid in patients with good binocular vision (torsional diplopia); combine with horizontal surgery.
SQ 23. Dissociated vertical deviation. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Dissociated vertical deviation (DVD)

  • Slow upward drift with extorsion of the non-fixing (covered) eye that does not follow Hering law; usually bilateral and asymmetric; associated with infantile esotropia and latent nystagmus.

Features

  • Upward drift when fatigued or covered, no corresponding hypotropia of the other eye, Bielschowsky phenomenon (drift decreases with neutral density filters), head tilt.

Differential diagnosis

  • Inferior oblique over-action (V-pattern, elevation in adduction), superior oblique palsy.

Management

  • Observation if small; surgery for cosmetic drift: SR recession (large), IO anterior transposition (if IO over-action co-exists), SR Faden.
SQ 24. Management of vertical diplopia. · outline

Management of vertical diplopia

  • History: onset, trauma, systemic disease (diabetes, thyroid, myasthenia), variability, head tilt, old photographs.

Examination

  • Cover test, three-step test (Parks), double Maddox rod (torsion), versions and ductions, forced duction, Hess chart, field of binocular single vision, upright-supine test (skew deviation), fatiguability, orbit (fractures, proptosis).

Causes

  • Fourth nerve palsy, third nerve palsy, skew deviation, thyroid eye disease (IR restriction), blow-out fracture, myasthenia, Brown syndrome, orbital tumours, DVD (rarely diplopia), sagging eye syndrome (elderly), after cataract or retinal surgery (anaesthetic myotoxicity, buckles).

Management

  • Treat cause; prisms (Fresnel then ground-in); occlusion; botulinum toxin; surgery after stable for 6 months (IR recession in TED with adjustable sutures, IO weakening for SO palsy, Knapp classification guides choice).
SQ 25. A and V patterns — aetiology, diagnosis, clinical significance and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

A and V patterns

  • Horizontal deviation that changes significantly between up gaze and down gaze: V pattern (more eso in down gaze or more exo in up gaze: difference of 15 prism dioptres or more); A pattern (more eso in up gaze or exo in down gaze: 10 prism dioptres or more).

Aetiology

  • Oblique muscle dysfunction (IO overaction: V; SO overaction: A), horizontal recti dysfunction, vertical recti, orbital pulley heterotopia, craniofacial anomalies (Crouzon, Apert: V), Brown syndrome (V), Duane syndrome, SO palsy (V).

Diagnosis

  • Measure deviation in primary, 25° up and 25° down gaze at distance; versions (oblique overaction), fundus torsion.

Clinical significance

  • Abnormal head posture (chin up or down), difficulty reading, diplopia in some gazes, cosmetic; affects surgical plan.

Management

  • With oblique overaction: weaken the overacting oblique (IO recession or anterior transposition for V; SO tenotomy or expander for A).
  • Without oblique overaction: vertical offset of horizontal recti insertions (MR towards the apex of the pattern, LR towards the open end; half to one tendon width), slanted recessions.
SQ 26. Congenital cranial dysinnervation disorders — Duane retraction syndrome (types, management with abnormal head posture); Brown syndrome (superior oblique sheath syndrome); double elevator palsy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Congenital cranial dysinnervation disorders (CCDDs)

  • Developmental abnormalities of cranial nerve innervation: Duane, Möbius, CFEOM, horizontal gaze palsy with progressive scoliosis, congenital ptosis (some).

Duane retraction syndrome

  • Absence of the sixth nerve nucleus with aberrant innervation of LR by the third nerve; globe retraction and palpebral fissure narrowing on adduction, upshoot or downshoot; left eye and females more common.
  • Huber types: I (limited abduction, commonest), II (limited adduction), III (both limited).
  • Management with abnormal head posture: recession of the ipsilateral MR (type I esotropic), Y-splitting of LR for upshoots, vertical rectus transposition (with or without MR recession), avoid resection.

Brown syndrome (SO sheath or tendon syndrome)

  • Limited elevation in adduction, positive forced duction, V-pattern, click; congenital or acquired (trauma, rheumatoid arthritis, sinus surgery).
  • Management: observation (some resolve), steroids for acquired inflammatory cases, SO tenotomy with silicone expander or split tendon lengthening for severe cases.

Double elevator palsy (monocular elevation deficiency)

  • Limited elevation in all gazes; hypotropia, pseudoptosis; restrictive (IR) or supranuclear.
  • Management: IR recession for restriction; Knapp procedure (MR and LR transposition to SR) for paretic type.

Revise from: Anti-Elevation Syndrome after Inferior Oblique Surgery

SQ 27. Paralytic strabismus — diagnosis, investigation and surgery; recent-onset diplopia in an adult. DNB / UNIV · outline

Paralytic strabismus

  • Incomitant deviation due to cranial nerve or muscle weakness.

Diagnosis

  • Diplopia history, head posture, cover test in nine gazes, primary vs secondary deviation, ductions and versions, Parks three-step test (vertical), Hess chart, diplopia charting, forced duction test (restriction) and force generation test (palsy), saccadic velocity.

Investigations

  • BP, blood sugar, lipids, ESR and CRP (over 50), thyroid function, acetylcholine receptor antibodies, MRI and MRA or CTA (pupil-involving third nerve, young patients, multiple palsies, other neurological signs, no recovery in 3 months).

Recent-onset diplopia in an adult

  • Monocular (optical) vs binocular; urgent cases: pupil-involving third nerve palsy (aneurysm), GCA, papilloedema, cavernous sinus disease, myasthenic crisis.
  • Temporising: occlusion, Fresnel prisms, botulinum toxin.

Surgery

  • After 6–12 months of stability; principles: weaken the antagonist, strengthen the palsied muscle, weaken the yoke, transposition for complete palsies; adjustable sutures; aim for single vision in primary position and downgaze.

Revise from: Strabismus Surgery Basics for Residents

SQ 46. Sagging eye syndrome and heavy eye syndrome. NEW · outline

Sagging eye syndrome (SES)

  • Age-related degeneration of the connective tissue bands (LR–SR band) between the lateral and superior rectus pulleys, letting the LR pulley sag inferiorly.
  • Features: older adults; distance esotropia (divergence insufficiency pattern) and/or small cyclovertical deviation (hypotropia on the side of greater sag); comitant; associated signs of ageing adnexa (high lid crease, ptosis, deep superior sulcus).
  • Diagnosis: clinical; MRI shows LR pulley sag and thinned or ruptured LR–SR band.
  • Management: prisms (base-out for distance), LR resection or plication (small amounts), medial rectus recession, vertical rectus recession for hypotropia.

Heavy eye syndrome (myopic strabismus fixus)

  • High axial myopia: the enlarged globe herniates superotemporally through the muscle cone between the SR and LR, displacing the LR inferiorly and the SR nasally.
  • Features: progressive esotropia and hypotropia, limited abduction and elevation, eye may become fixed in esotropia and hypotropia.
  • Diagnosis: MRI or CT (globe prolapse between SR and LR).
  • Management: Yokoyama procedure (loop myopexy: union of the SR and LR muscle bellies to reposition the globe into the muscle cone), with MR recession; prisms for mild cases.
SQ 47. Möbius syndrome and congenital fibrosis of the extraocular muscles (CFEOM). NEW · outline

Möbius syndrome

  • Congenital, non-progressive facial palsy (bilateral or unilateral) with limited abduction (sixth nerve or horizontal gaze palsy); a congenital cranial dysinnervation disorder; sporadic mostly (vascular disruption: maternal misoprostol use, PLXND1 and REV3L genes).
  • Features: mask-like face, inability to smile, incomplete eyelid closure (exposure keratopathy), esotropia, horizontal gaze palsy with preserved vertical movements and convergence, limb and chest anomalies (Poland syndrome, clubfoot), tongue and swallowing problems.
  • Management: lubrication and lid protection, strabismus surgery (MR recession; transpositions), speech and feeding therapy, facial reanimation (smile surgery), multidisciplinary care.

Congenital fibrosis of the extraocular muscles (CFEOM)

  • Group of CCDDs with abnormal development of the oculomotor (and trochlear) nerves; secondary muscle fibrosis.
  • CFEOM1 (KIF21A, autosomal dominant): bilateral ptosis, eyes fixed in infraduction (hypotropia), inability to elevate above midline, chin-up head posture.
  • CFEOM2 (PHOX2A, recessive): bilateral ptosis, exotropia; CFEOM3 (TUBB3, KIF21A): variable, may have other neurological findings.
  • Diagnosis: clinical, forced duction test (restriction), MRI (hypoplastic oculomotor nerve), genetic testing.
  • Management: treat amblyopia, strabismus surgery (large recessions of tight muscles; disappointing results), ptosis surgery with caution (frontalis sling with poor Bell phenomenon: exposure risk).

Revise from: Third, Fourth & Sixth Nerve Palsies

Amblyopia & Paediatric Vision

SQ 28. Amblyopia — definition, causes, pathogenesis, classification, critical period and recent trends in management (including anisometropic amblyopia and penalisation). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Reduced best-corrected visual acuity (usually two lines difference) in one or both eyes without structural cause, from abnormal visual experience in the critical period.

Causes and classification

  • Strabismic, anisometropic, isoametropic (bilateral high refractive error), meridional (astigmatism), deprivation (cataract, ptosis, corneal opacity: most severe), occlusion (reverse).

Pathogenesis

  • Competition at the lateral geniculate body and visual cortex; loss of binocular cells; abnormal spatial and temporal processing (Hubel and Wiesel).

Critical period

  • Most sensitive until 2–3 years, plasticity until about 7–8 years; older children may still respond (PEDIG).

Management

  • Correct refractive error first (spectacles alone improve many: refractive adaptation, PEDIG).
  • Patching: 2 hours daily for moderate, 6 hours for severe (PEDIG); penalisation with atropine 1% (weekend atropine as effective).
  • Recent trends: binocular dichoptic treatments (Luminopia, CureSight), video games, levodopa (adjunct, limited), perceptual learning in older children.
  • Anisometropic amblyopia: refractive correction (contact lenses, refractive surgery in selected cases), patching or penalisation; good prognosis.
  • Maintain to prevent recurrence; taper therapy.

Revise from: Amblyopia Treatment Updates: Glasses, Patching Doses and Digital Therapy

SQ 29. Vision assessment in a child; fixation reflexes. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Vision assessment in a child

  • Infants: fixation and following (central, steady, maintained: CSM), preferential looking (Teller and Cardiff cards), VEP, OKN, objection to occlusion.
  • Toddlers: Cardiff, Kay pictures, Lea symbols.
  • Over 3 years: HOTV, Sheridan–Gardiner, Lea, logMAR crowded charts; over 5 years: Snellen or ETDRS.

Fixation reflexes

  • Fixation develops by 6 weeks, following by 3 months; fixation reflex (holding the image on the fovea), refixation reflex, fusion reflex.
  • Fixation preference test: 10 prism dioptre fixation test in non-strabismic children.
  • Visuscope: eccentric fixation.
SQ 30. Differential diagnosis of poor vision in a child and how you would manage such a child. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of poor vision in a child

  • Refractive: high refractive error, amblyopia.
  • Anterior segment: congenital cataract, corneal opacity, congenital glaucoma, aniridia.
  • Posterior: ROP, retinoblastoma, coloboma, LCA and other dystrophies, toxoplasmosis scars, albinism.
  • Optic nerve: hypoplasia (septo-optic dysplasia), atrophy, glioma.
  • Cortical (cerebral) visual impairment: hypoxia, prematurity; delayed visual maturation.
  • Nystagmus as a clue.

Evaluation

  • Birth and family history, fixation behaviour, pupils, red reflex, cycloplegic refraction, fundus, ERG and VEP, MRI brain, genetics.

Management

  • Treat the cause (cataract surgery, glaucoma surgery, ROP care), refraction, amblyopia therapy, low-vision aids, early intervention services, education support, genetic counselling.
SQ 31. Dichoptic and binocular digital therapy for amblyopia (PEDIG studies). · outline

Dichoptic and binocular treatments

  • Present different images to each eye (higher contrast or only key elements to the amblyopic eye) to reduce suppression and encourage binocular use; devices: tablet games with red-green glasses, virtual reality headsets, dichoptic movies (Luminopia, FDA approved), CureSight (eye-tracking based), Falling Blocks game.

PEDIG studies

  • ATS18: binocular iPad game was not as good as 2 hours patching in 5–12-year-olds (poor adherence).
  • ATS20: dichoptic Dig Rush game not better than continued glasses in older children.
  • Dichoptic movie studies (Luminopia trial: 1 hour daily of dichoptic TV improved acuity more than glasses alone).

Practice

  • Option for children who do not tolerate patching or have residual amblyopia; adherence and supervision are key; combine with spectacles; long-term benefit and stereopsis gains still being studied.

Revise from: Amblyopia Treatment Updates: Glasses, Patching Doses and Digital Therapy

Strabismus Surgery

SQ 32. Lateral rectus resection; hang-back recession; inferior oblique recession. · outline

Lateral rectus resection

  • Strengthening procedure: isolate the LR, place sutures at the planned resection length (e.g. 4–9 mm), resect the tendon and reattach to the original insertion; for exotropia combined with MR recession or for esotropia due to sixth nerve palsy (with MR weakening).
  • Complications: slipped muscle, lost muscle, conjunctival scarring, anterior segment ischaemia (with multiple recti), narrowing of palpebral fissure.

Hang-back recession

  • Muscle sutured to the original insertion with an adjustable length of suture (“hung back”), instead of suturing directly to the sclera further back; useful when sclera is thin or exposure difficult; can be adjustable; risk of central sag and less predictability.

Inferior oblique recession

  • Isolate IO in the inferotemporal quadrant, detach and suture to the sclera at a point 3 mm posterior and 2 mm lateral to the lateral end of the IR insertion (8 mm recession) or more anteriorly (anterior transposition at IR insertion for DVD and V patterns).

Revise from: Anti-Elevation Syndrome after Inferior Oblique Surgery

SQ 33. Faden operation (posterior fixation suture). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Faden operation (posterior fixation suture)

  • A non-absorbable suture fixing the muscle belly to the sclera 11–14 mm behind the insertion; reduces the muscle’s action in its field of action without affecting the primary position much.

Indications

  • High AC/A esotropia (MR), nystagmus blockage syndrome, DVD (SR), incomitant deviations, Duane syndrome, to weaken the yoke of a paretic muscle.

Complications

  • Scleral perforation, retinal detachment, limited effect, scarring; often combined with recession; pulley fixation as an alternative.

Revise from: Strabismus Surgery Basics for Residents

SQ 34. Adjustable sutures in strabismus surgery. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Adjustable sutures

  • Muscle reattached with a sliding noose or bow-tie so that position can be adjusted after surgery (hours to a day) with the patient awake.

Indications

  • Adults, re-operations, restrictive squints (thyroid eye disease, blow-out fractures), unpredictable deviations, paralytic strabismus.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • More precise alignment and fewer re-operations; needs co-operative patient (some children under sedation), additional discomfort, cost, slipped muscle risk.

Revise from: Strabismus Surgery Basics for Residents

SQ 35. Evaluate the various transposition (muscle transplantation) procedures in strabismus. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Transposition procedures

  • Move the insertions of normal muscles to supply force in the field of a paralysed muscle.

Types

  • Full tendon transposition (Hummelsheim: half tendons, Jensen: splitting and union of halves), full vertical rectus transposition (VRT), superior rectus transposition (SRT) with or without MR recession, Knapp (horizontal recti to SR for monocular elevation deficiency), inverse Knapp, Nishida procedure (without disinsertion), augmented with Foster sutures.

Indications

  • Complete sixth nerve palsy, Duane syndrome, third nerve palsy (partial), monocular elevation deficiency, lost muscles.

Evaluation

  • Restore alignment in primary position and some field of single vision; botulinum toxin to the antagonist; risk of anterior segment ischaemia (avoid disinserting three recti); vertical deviations and torsion after surgery.

Revise from: Strabismus Surgery Basics for Residents

SQ 36. Surgical options for nystagmus and their outcome; Kestenbaum-Anderson procedure. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Surgical options for nystagmus

  • Kestenbaum–Anderson procedure: moves the null point to primary position (recess and resect yoke muscles) to correct abnormal head posture; Parks “5-6-7-8” rule (amounts of surgery).
  • Artificial divergence surgery: bimedial recession with exotropia induced, using convergence to dampen nystagmus.
  • Large recession of all four horizontal recti: reduces nystagmus amplitude.
  • Tenotomy and reattachment: improves foveation (NAFX scores), modest improvement in acuity.

Outcome

  • Good correction of head posture (about 70–90%); modest improvement in vision and amplitude; recurrences of head turn possible.

Revise from: Nystagmus: A Classification Approach

SQ 37. Botulinum toxin in strabismus. · outline

Botulinum toxin in strabismus

  • Clostridium botulinum toxin A blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing temporary paralysis (onset 2–4 days, lasts 2–3 months); injected under EMG guidance or direct visualisation (1.25–5 units).

Indications

  • Acute sixth nerve palsy (MR injection to prevent contracture), infantile esotropia, acute acquired comitant esotropia, small-moderate deviations in adults, post-operative residual deviation, thyroid eye disease (active phase), nystagmus, diagnostic (predict post-operative diplopia), blepharospasm and hemifacial spasm.

Side effects

  • Ptosis (commonest), vertical deviation (spread to adjacent muscles), over-correction, scleral perforation, retrobulbar haemorrhage; need for repeat injections.

Revise from: Botulinum Toxin in Ophthalmology: Reconstitution, Units per Site for Blepharospasm, Strabismus Doses and Side Effects

SQ 38. Recent advances in surgical and non-surgical management of squint. DNB / UNIV · outline

Surgical advances

  • Adjustable sutures (including in children), minimally invasive strabismus surgery (MISS: small incisions), pulley-based surgery (posterior fixation alternative), transposition variants (superior rectus transposition, Nishida), botulinum augmentation, plication instead of resection (preserves anterior ciliary vessels), 3D visualisation, intraoperative imaging.

Non-surgical advances

  • Botulinum toxin, bupivacaine injections (strengthening), Fresnel prisms, dichoptic and digital amblyopia treatment, PEDIG evidence-based protocols (overminus for intermittent exotropia, observation), virtual reality orthoptic therapy, eye-tracking based diagnostics (automated cover test), AI-assisted photography for screening.

Paediatric Ophthalmology

SQ 39. Acquired eye diseases in children — types, causes and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Acquired eye diseases in children

  • Infections: conjunctivitis, ophthalmia neonatorum, keratitis (VAD-related), orbital cellulitis, endophthalmitis (rare).
  • Inflammation: JIA uveitis, VKC, pars planitis.
  • Trauma: injuries (commonest cause of unilateral blindness), non-accidental injury.
  • Nutritional: xerophthalmia.
  • Refractive errors and amblyopia; myopia (rising).
  • Tumours: retinoblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, optic glioma, leukaemic infiltration.
  • Systemic: diabetes (cataract, retinopathy in adolescents), NF1, IIH, ROP sequelae.
  • Acquired cataract (trauma, steroids), acquired glaucoma (steroid-induced, uveitic).

Management principles

  • Early detection (school screening, red reflex), age-appropriate examination (EUA), treat the cause, amblyopia management, family counselling, prevention (immunisation, vitamin A, injury prevention).
SQ 40. Ophthalmic findings in inborn errors of metabolism; mucopolysaccharidoses. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ophthalmic findings in inborn errors of metabolism

  • Carbohydrate: galactosaemia (oil-droplet cataract), diabetes.
  • Amino acids: homocystinuria (lens subluxation inferonasally), albinism, alkaptonuria (scleral pigmentation), cystinosis (corneal crystals; cysteamine drops), tyrosinaemia type II (pseudodendritic keratitis).
  • Lipid storage: Tay–Sachs, Niemann–Pick, Sandhoff, GM1 (cherry-red spot), Fabry (cornea verticillata, tortuous vessels, spoke cataract), Gaucher (eye movement abnormalities), Refsum (RP-like).
  • Mucopolysaccharidoses, mucolipidoses.
  • Peroxisomal (Zellweger: RP, cataract), Wilson disease (KF ring, sunflower cataract), mitochondrial (CPEO, LHON), gyrate atrophy (ornithine), abetalipoproteinaemia (RP; vitamin E).

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS)

  • Lysosomal enzyme defects with accumulation of glycosaminoglycans.
  • Corneal clouding (Hurler MPS I, Scheie, Morquio, Maroteaux–Lamy; absent in Hunter MPS II and Sanfilippo III), pigmentary retinopathy (Hurler, Hunter, Sanfilippo), optic atrophy and disc swelling, glaucoma, ptosis.
  • Management: enzyme replacement therapy, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, keratoplasty for corneal clouding.
SQ 41. Craniofacial syndromes — Crouzon, Apert and Goldenhar. DNB / UNIV · outline

Crouzon syndrome

  • Autosomal dominant (FGFR2); premature fusion of coronal and other sutures (craniosynostosis), midface hypoplasia.
  • Eye: shallow orbits with proptosis (exposure keratopathy, globe luxation), hypertelorism, V-pattern exotropia (excyclorotation of orbits), papilloedema and optic atrophy (raised ICP), amblyopia.

Apert syndrome

  • FGFR2; craniosynostosis with syndactyly of hands and feet; similar ocular findings, more severe, intellectual disability, keratoconus.

Goldenhar syndrome (oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum)

  • First and second branchial arch anomalies; hemifacial microsomia, preauricular skin tags, vertebral anomalies.
  • Eye: epibulbar (limbal) dermoids and lipodermoids, upper lid coloboma, Duane syndrome, microphthalmos.

Management

  • Multidisciplinary craniofacial team; protect cornea, treat amblyopia and strabismus, monitor optic nerve (raised ICP), orbital and craniofacial surgery.
SQ 42. Peters anomaly and other anterior segment dysgeneses. DNB / UNIV · outline

Anterior segment dysgenesis

  • Disorders of neural crest migration and differentiation in the anterior segment; often with glaucoma (about 50%).

Peters anomaly

  • Central corneal opacity with defects in posterior stroma, Descemet and endothelium; type 1 (iridocorneal adhesions), type 2 (lens-corneal adhesion or cataract); bilateral in 60%; PAX6, PITX2, FOXC1, CYP1B1; Peters plus syndrome (short stature, cleft lip, developmental delay).
  • Management: optical iridectomy, keratoplasty (high failure in infants), glaucoma surgery, amblyopia treatment.

Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome

  • Posterior embryotoxon (prominent Schwalbe line) with iris strands (Axenfeld), plus iris hypoplasia, corectopia, polycoria (Rieger), plus dental and facial anomalies, umbilical skin (syndrome); PITX2 and FOXC1; glaucoma in 50%.

Others

  • Aniridia, primary congenital glaucoma (trabeculodysgenesis), sclerocornea, congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy, iridogoniodysgenesis, posterior keratoconus.
SQ 43. Oculocutaneous albinism. DNB / UNIV · outline

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA)

  • Autosomal recessive reduction or absence of melanin in the skin, hair and eyes; types: OCA1 (tyrosinase; OCA1A no pigment), OCA2 (P gene; commonest in Africa), OCA3, OCA4; ocular albinism (X-linked, GPR143: eyes only).

Ocular features

  • Reduced vision (6/24–6/60), nystagmus, photophobia, iris transillumination, foveal hypoplasia (OCT), hypopigmented fundus, refractive errors (high astigmatism), strabismus, abnormal decussation at the chiasm (excess crossing: asymmetric VEP), reduced stereopsis.

Systemic

  • Skin cancer risk; Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome (bleeding, pulmonary fibrosis), Chédiak–Higashi syndrome (immunodeficiency): check before surgery.

Management

  • Refractive correction, tinted glasses or contact lenses, low-vision aids, sun protection, genetic counselling, educational support; strabismus and nystagmus surgery as needed.
SQ 44. Congenital infections — rubella, toxoplasmosis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Congenital rubella

  • First trimester infection: cataract (nuclear pearly), salt-and-pepper retinopathy, microphthalmos, glaucoma, corneal clouding; deafness, heart defects, microcephaly; prevention with vaccination.

Congenital toxoplasmosis

  • Transplacental infection; triad: chorioretinitis (macular scars; bilateral), hydrocephalus, intracranial calcification.
  • Other eye findings: microphthalmos, nystagmus, strabismus, cataract, optic atrophy.
  • Treatment: pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine and folinic acid for the first year; steroids for vision-threatening lesions; lifelong risk of reactivation.

Other TORCH infections

  • CMV (chorioretinitis, optic atrophy), herpes simplex (keratitis, retinitis, cataract), syphilis (interstitial keratitis later, salt-and-pepper retinopathy), varicella (cataract, chorioretinal scars), Zika (macular pigment mottling, chorioretinal atrophy, optic nerve hypoplasia, microcephaly), LCMV.

Evaluation

  • TORCH serology, neonatal eye examination, neuroimaging, hearing assessment.
SQ 45. Paediatric ocular screening and vision-screening devices. · outline

Paediatric ocular screening

  • Newborn: red reflex test (cataract, retinoblastoma, glaucoma), ROP screening in preterm babies.
  • Infants and toddlers: fixation and following, corneal light reflex (Hirschberg), cover test, red reflex, family history.
  • Preschool (3–5 years): visual acuity with age-appropriate charts (Lea, HOTV), stereoacuity, cover test; detect amblyopia risk factors.
  • School: acuity charts (refractive errors), colour vision.

Vision-screening devices

  • Photoscreeners (Plusoptix, Spot Vision Screener, iScreen): detect refractive errors, anisometropia, strabismus and media opacity from a photograph of the pupil reflexes; quick, for non-verbal children.
  • Autorefractors (handheld: Retinomax, SureSight), smartphone-based apps (GoCheck Kids, Peek Acuity), Brückner test (simultaneous red reflex), random-dot stereo tests.

Programme considerations

  • Referral criteria by age (AAPOS guidelines), trained staff, follow-up of referrals, cost-effectiveness, integration with RBSK in India.
SQ 48. Cerebral (cortical) visual impairment in children. NEW · outline

Cerebral (cortical) visual impairment (CVI)

  • Visual impairment due to damage or malfunction of the retrochiasmal visual pathways and brain visual processing areas, not explained by eye disease; the commonest cause of visual impairment in children in high-income countries and increasingly in India.

Causes

  • Perinatal hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, periventricular leukomalacia (preterm), hydrocephalus, infections (meningitis, encephalitis, congenital infections), neonatal hypoglycaemia, trauma (abusive head trauma), metabolic disorders, seizures, genetic syndromes; often with cerebral palsy.

Features

  • Variable acuity, visual inattention, light gazing, colour preference, better vision for moving objects, difficulty with crowded scenes (visual crowding), lower field defects, poor visually guided reach, delayed visual responses; associated strabismus, nystagmus, optic atrophy and refractive errors; normal pupils and fundi (or optic atrophy).

Assessment

  • Functional vision assessment (CVI Range), preferential looking, VEP, MRI brain, refraction (accommodation weakness: near glasses), visual fields by confrontation.

Management

  • Correct refractive error, treat strabismus, early intervention programmes, environmental adaptations (simplify visual clutter, high contrast, good lighting, familiar objects, movement), special education, therapy for the whole child, counselling families; vision may improve over time.

Revise from: Children's Eye Problems for MBBS Students: White Pupil, Retinoblastoma, Congenital Cataract, ROP and Ophthalmia Neonatorum
