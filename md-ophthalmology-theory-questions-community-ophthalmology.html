MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Community Ophthalmology (29 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 29 community ophthalmology theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Epidemiology & Surveys

CM 1. WHO definitions of blindness and visual impairment; predominant causes of blindness in India from national surveys, and national initiatives for their prevention. HIGH-YIELD · outline

WHO definitions (ICD-11, presenting visual acuity in the better eye)

  • Mild visual impairment: worse than 6/12 to 6/18.
  • Moderate: worse than 6/18 to 6/60.
  • Severe: worse than 6/60 to 3/60.
  • Blindness: worse than 3/60, or visual field less than 10° around fixation.
  • Near vision impairment: worse than N6 at 40 cm.
  • India adopted the WHO definition of blindness (worse than 3/60) in 2017, replacing worse than 6/60.

Causes of blindness in India (NBVIS 2015–2019, people aged 50 and over)

  • Cataract (the leading cause), corneal opacity, cataract surgical complications, posterior segment disease, glaucoma; uncorrected refractive error is a leading cause of moderate to severe visual impairment.

National initiatives

  • NPCBVI (started 1976 as NPCB, renamed 2017): cataract surgery, school eye screening and free spectacles, eye banking and corneal transplantation, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma and ROP services, vision centres, trained human resources.
  • Ayushman Bharat health and wellness centres, eSanjeevani tele-consultation, VISION 2020 India.

CM 2. Plan a survey for cataract blindness — incidence vs prevalence, sample size; RAAB methodology. DNB / UNIV · outline

Incidence vs prevalence

  • Prevalence: proportion of a population with the disease at a point in time (existing cases); used for cataract blindness burden.
  • Incidence: new cases occurring in a population at risk over a period (needs cohort follow-up).

Planning a survey for cataract blindness

  • Define objectives (prevalence of blindness due to cataract, cataract surgical coverage, outcomes, barriers), target population (people aged 50 and over), sampling frame (census enumeration areas), cluster random sampling.
  • Sample size: n = Z²p(1 − p)/d² × design effect; e.g. expected prevalence 2%, precision 20% of p, 95% confidence, design effect about 1.5, plus non-response (10–15%).
  • Examination teams, standard definitions (WHO), training and inter-observer agreement, pilot study, data entry and analysis.

RAAB (Rapid Assessment of Avoidable Blindness)

  • Population-based survey of people aged 50 and over (where most blindness occurs); 50 people per cluster, compact segment sampling; quick, inexpensive; presenting and pinhole visual acuity, lens examination with torch and direct ophthalmoscope, cause of vision loss, cataract surgical coverage, surgical outcome, barriers to uptake.
  • Software (RAAB7) generates indicators: prevalence of blindness and visual impairment, causes, CSC, eCSC, outcomes, barriers; used for district planning and monitoring.

Revise from: Sample Size Calculator for Ophthalmology Theses: Prevalence, Two Proportions and Two Means

CM 3. National Blindness and Visual Impairment Survey (2015-19) — key findings; WHO World Report on Vision. · outline

National Blindness and Visual Impairment Survey (NBVIS 2015–2019)

  • Conducted using RAAB methodology in 31 districts among people aged 50 years and over.
  • Prevalence of blindness (presenting vision worse than 3/60) about 1.99% in people aged 50 and over (estimated about 0.36% across all ages), down from earlier surveys.
  • Causes of blindness: untreated cataract (about two-thirds), corneal opacity, cataract surgical complications, posterior segment disorders, glaucoma.
  • Causes of moderate to severe visual impairment: cataract and uncorrected refractive error.
  • Barriers: lack of awareness, cost, fear, no escort, distance.
  • Higher in rural areas, older age groups, women and illiterate people.

WHO World Report on Vision (2019)

  • At least 2.2 billion people have vision impairment or blindness, of whom at least 1 billion have a preventable or unaddressed impairment (cataract and refractive error leading).
  • Recommendations: integrated people-centred eye care (IPEC) in universal health coverage, eye care within primary health care, high-quality research, monitoring with effective coverage indicators (eCSC and eREC), engaging communities; addresses the rise of myopia, diabetes and ageing.

Revise from: 6/60, 6/18, 6/36 & 3/60 Vision Meaning: Legal Blindness in India (RPwD 2016)

National Programmes

CM 4. VISION 2020: The Right to Sight — objectives, diseases covered, strategies and organisation; its status in India. HIGH-YIELD · outline

VISION 2020: The Right to Sight

  • Launched in 1999 by WHO and IAPB to eliminate avoidable blindness by 2020.

Objectives

  • Eliminate avoidable blindness; prevent the projected doubling of global blindness.

Diseases covered

  • Cataract, refractive error and low vision, trachoma, childhood blindness (including vitamin A deficiency), onchocerciasis; later glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy were added in many countries.

Strategies

  • Disease control, human resource development, infrastructure and appropriate technology, advocacy; integration into primary health care.

Organisation

  • Global (WHO, IAPB), national VISION 2020 committees, district-level eye care programmes (planning for a population of about 1 million).

Status in India

  • India adopted VISION 2020 in 2001; major gains in cataract surgery rates and blindness reduction; successor: WHO World Report on Vision (2019), integrated people-centred eye care (IPEC), 2030 targets for effective coverage of cataract surgery (eCSC) and refractive error (eREC).

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking, Trachoma: WHO Grading, SAFE Strategy and India's Elimination

CM 5. Discuss NPCB, with special reference to combating corneal blindness. HIGH-YIELD · outline

NPCB (now NPCBVI)

  • Launched in 1976 as a 100% centrally sponsored scheme; goal to reduce blindness prevalence to 0.3% (and 0.25% target in later plans).
  • Components: cataract surgery, school eye screening, refractive services, eye banking, management of other diseases (DR, glaucoma, ROP, keratoplasty), vision centres, training, IEC, District Health Society implementation.

Combating corneal blindness

  • Causes: infective keratitis (fungal in agricultural workers), trauma, vitamin A deficiency, trachoma, chemical injuries, traditional eye medicine use.
  • Prevention: protective eyewear, early treatment of corneal abrasions (antibiotics and antifungals at primary level), vitamin A prophylaxis, health education.
  • Treatment: eye banks (grant-in-aid for collection and processing), eye donation fortnight (25 August–8 September), hospital cornea retrieval programmes (HCRP), keratoplasty grants, training corneal surgeons.
  • Rehabilitation for irreversible cases.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

CM 6. Constitution and functions of the District Health Society (District Blindness Control Society). DNB / UNIV · outline

District Health Society (formerly District Blindness Control Society)

  • Under the National Health Mission, the District Blindness Control Society (DBCS, set up from 1994–95) was merged into the District Health Society; NPCBVI activities are implemented through it.

Constitution

  • Chairperson: District Collector or Magistrate; member secretary: Chief Medical Officer or District Programme Manager; members: district ophthalmic surgeon (district programme manager for NPCBVI), representatives of health, education, social welfare, information, NGOs, private practitioners, and civil society.

Functions

  • Plan and implement district eye care activities and targets, coordinate government, NGO and private sector providers, release grants-in-aid for cataract and other surgeries, organise screening camps and school eye screening, distribute free spectacles, promote eye donation, monitor quality and outcomes, data reporting (HMIS), IEC activities, training of staff.
CM 7. Strategies to control cataract blindness in India; cataract surgical rate and visual outcome; plan to eliminate the cataract backlog in your state. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Strategies to control cataract blindness

  • Increase cataract surgical rate (CSR: surgeries per million population per year; India over 6000), improve quality (visual outcome monitoring), reduce cost (free surgery for poor), increase coverage of women and rural people, high-volume efficient surgery (MSICS, phaco), IOL for all.

Visual outcome (WHO)

  • Good: 6/18 or better (target over 80% of operated eyes with best correction, over 90% with available correction); borderline: worse than 6/18 to 6/60; poor: worse than 6/60.
  • Monitoring causes of poor outcome: selection (comorbidities), surgery (complications), spectacles (uncorrected refractive error), sequelae (PCO).

Plan to eliminate the cataract backlog in a state

  • Estimate backlog (population, prevalence surveys, RAAB), map resources (surgeons, theatres), district eye health plans, base hospitals with outreach camps and vision centres, transport for patients, public-private partnership, training, quality monitoring, counselling to address barriers (fear, cost, distance), targets with audit.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

CM 8. Eye camp approach vs reach-in approach for cataract in the community. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Eye camp approach

  • Surgical camps in schools or temporary sites (historical: outreach surgery); later screening camps with surgery at the base hospital.
  • Advantages: reach remote populations, large numbers.
  • Disadvantages: poor sterility and outcomes (infection disasters), poor follow-up; now surgery only in approved facilities.

Reach-in approach

  • Patients transported from screening camps to well-equipped base hospitals for surgery (Aravind and other models); vision centres provide primary care and follow-up.
  • Advantages: better quality, sterility, IOL implantation, follow-up; disadvantages: transport cost, need for counselling.

Current

  • Screening camps + base hospital surgery (reach-in), vision centres, tele-ophthalmology; combined approach is recommended.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

CM 9. Primary eye care — role of PHC, health assistant and NGOs; organisation and problems of eye camps. DNB / UNIV · outline

Primary eye care

  • Eye care at the first point of contact: health promotion, prevention, early detection, treatment of simple conditions and referral.

Role of PHC and health workers

  • Health and wellness centres (Ayushman Arogya Mandir), community health officers, ASHA workers and multipurpose health workers: vision testing, identification of cataract, red eye management, removal of superficial foreign bodies, vitamin A distribution, referral, follow-up of operated patients, health education, school eye screening.
  • Ophthalmic assistants and optometrists at vision centres: refraction, spectacles, screening for glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy.

Role of NGOs

  • Service delivery (base hospitals, outreach), community mobilisation, training, eye banks, research (Aravind, LV Prasad, Sankara Nethralaya models), partnerships with government (grants).

Organisation of eye camps (screening camps)

  • Pre-camp: site selection, publicity, permissions, team, equipment and transport; screening of patients, selection for surgery at base hospital (reach-in approach), counselling.
  • Post-camp: follow-up, spectacles, monitoring outcomes.

Problems

  • Poor sterility and outcomes in historical operating camps, inadequate follow-up, lack of IOL, transport and cost barriers, inadequate counselling, targets driving quality down; now only screening camps with surgery in approved facilities.
CM 10. Discuss visual aids and their relevance to the national programme for control of blindness. · outline

Visual aids relevant to the national programme

  • Spectacles: free spectacles for schoolchildren with refractive errors and for presbyopic elderly; near vision spectacles for older persons (NPCBVI component).
  • Low vision aids: optical (magnifiers, telescopes, spectacle magnifiers), non-optical (lighting, large print, contrast), electronic devices; low vision clinics at regional institutes and medical colleges.
  • Aphakic and pseudophakic correction after cataract surgery.
  • Assistive devices through the ADIP scheme (Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment): smart canes, Braille kits, talking devices.

Relevance

  • Uncorrected refractive error is a leading cause of visual impairment; spectacles are cheap and effective (eREC indicator); low vision services improve function and quality of life for people with irreversible loss.

Challenges

  • Compliance with spectacle wear, quality and fit of spectacles, availability of trained staff, low awareness of low vision services.
CM 11. NPCBVI — current structure, targets and the shift to comprehensive eye care; eREC and eCSC indicators. · outline

NPCBVI current structure

  • Centrally sponsored programme under the National Health Mission (Ministry of Health and Family Welfare); national programme officer at the Directorate General of Health Services; state programme officers, district health societies; regional institutes of ophthalmology, medical colleges, district hospitals, sub-district hospitals, vision centres at PHCs; NGOs and private partners.

Targets

  • Reduce prevalence of blindness to 0.25% (all ages) by 2025 (and beyond), with targets for cataract surgeries, spectacles for schoolchildren and elderly, corneas collected, other eye diseases treated.

Shift to comprehensive eye care

  • Beyond cataract to diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, ROP, corneal blindness (keratoplasty), childhood blindness, low vision, trauma; strengthening vision centres and health and wellness centres; teleophthalmology; human resource development; mobile ophthalmic units; National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly links.

eREC and eCSC (WHO, World Health Assembly 2021 targets for 2030)

  • Effective refractive error coverage (eREC): proportion of people with refractive error who have adequate correction leading to good vision; target: 40 percentage point increase by 2030.
  • Effective cataract surgical coverage (eCSC): proportion of people with operable cataract who have had surgery with a good visual outcome (6/12 or better); target: 30 percentage point increase by 2030.
  • Both measure access and quality; RAAB provides data.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

CM 26. Vision centres and integrated people-centred eye care (IPEC). NEW · outline

Vision centres

  • Primary eye care units serving about 50,000 people, usually staffed by an optometrist or ophthalmic assistant, often in PHCs or run by NGOs (LV Prasad, Aravind models); linked to secondary and tertiary eye hospitals by referral and teleophthalmology.
  • Services: vision testing and refraction with spectacles, screening for cataract, glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy, treatment of minor conditions, referral, follow-up, health education; NPCBVI supports vision centres at PHCs.
  • Impact: increased access and spectacle coverage, earlier cataract detection, lower costs for patients.

Integrated people-centred eye care (IPEC)

  • WHO concept from the World Report on Vision (2019): eye care services managed and delivered so that people receive a continuum of promotion, prevention, treatment and rehabilitation according to their needs throughout life, integrated into primary health care and universal health coverage.
  • Strategies: engage communities, reorient care towards primary level, coordinate services across levels and sectors, create an enabling environment (workforce, financing, data), include eye care in health information systems; monitor with eCSC and eREC.

Indian context

  • Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (health and wellness centres) adding eye care to primary health care, NPCBVI, PM-JAY coverage for surgeries.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking, Online Eye Consultations in India: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, What Can Be Prescribed and When to See the Patient

Childhood Blindness & Nutrition

CM 12. Causes of childhood blindness and strategies to decrease them; prevention of childhood eye disorders and the role of school health programmes. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of childhood blindness

  • Anatomical: whole globe (microphthalmos), cornea (vitamin A deficiency, measles, ophthalmia neonatorum), lens (congenital cataract), uvea (coloboma), retina (ROP, dystrophies, retinoblastoma), optic nerve (atrophy, hypoplasia), cerebral visual impairment, refractive error and amblyopia.
  • Aetiological: hereditary, intrauterine (rubella, toxoplasma), perinatal (ROP, ophthalmia neonatorum), childhood (VAD, measles, trauma), unknown.

Strategies

  • Primary prevention: measles immunisation, rubella vaccination, vitamin A, maternal health, safe deliveries, ROP-safe oxygen, avoiding harmful traditional practices, injury prevention.
  • Early detection: red reflex testing at birth, ROP screening, school screening.
  • Treatment: paediatric eye care centres (cataract, glaucoma surgery), spectacles, low vision services, rehabilitation and special education.

School health programmes

  • Screening for refractive error and other diseases by teachers and optometrists, free spectacles, referral, health education, eye safety, monitoring compliance with spectacles (RBSK, NPCBVI).

Revise from: Children's Eye Problems for MBBS Students: White Pupil, Retinoblastoma, Congenital Cataract, ROP and Ophthalmia Neonatorum

CM 13. How would you organise an effective eye screening programme in a semi-urban school, with special reference to refractive errors? HIGH-YIELD · outline

Planning

  • Situation analysis: number of schools and children, staff, partners (school authorities, local health departments, NGOs, optical suppliers).
  • Team: trained teachers for primary screening, optometrists and ophthalmologists for secondary screening.

Steps

  • Teacher training to use a simple vision chart (6/9 or 6/12 line, tumbling E or Snellen).
  • Primary screening by teachers; children failing referred for refraction by optometrists in school.
  • Cycloplegic refraction where needed; spectacles provided free and quickly; referral for other eye problems (strabismus, amblyopia, cataract).
  • Education of children, teachers and parents (myopia, outdoor time, screen time).

Follow-up and evaluation

  • Spectacle compliance checks (often poor), annual re-screening, records, indicators (children screened, refracted, spectacles given, worn).

Special reference to refractive errors

  • Myopia is rising in urban and semi-urban schoolchildren; ready-made spectacles for simple errors, custom spectacles for astigmatism and anisometropia; myopia control (outdoor activity, atropine, defocus lenses).
CM 14. Discuss viable strategies to reduce amblyopia at the community level. · outline

Strategies to reduce amblyopia at the community level

  • Awareness among parents, teachers, health workers and paediatricians about squint, poor vision and the importance of early treatment.
  • Early detection: red reflex testing at birth and in infancy (RBSK), preschool vision screening (anganwadis) at 3–5 years with age-appropriate charts or photoscreeners, school screening.
  • Correct refractive errors early with free spectacles and ensure compliance (school follow-up).
  • Treatment access: occlusion and atropine penalisation through primary and secondary centres, trained optometrists, affordable patches.
  • Treat cataract, ptosis and strabismus early (paediatric referral pathways).
  • Monitoring and follow-up systems (registers, reminders), tele-ophthalmology.
  • Research into digital binocular therapy that is low cost.
CM 15. Strategies for tackling major causes of visual impairment in children under NPCB. HIGH-YIELD · outline

NPCBVI strategies for children

  • School eye screening and free spectacles for children with refractive errors.
  • Paediatric ophthalmology units: congenital cataract and glaucoma surgery, ROP screening and treatment.
  • Vitamin A supplementation (with the national immunisation programme), measles and rubella vaccination.
  • Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK): screening for congenital cataract, strabismus, refractive errors.
  • Low vision services and rehabilitation; human resources (optometrists, paediatric ophthalmologists).
  • Community awareness and early detection.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

CM 16. Vitamin A deficiency — xerophthalmia, keratomalacia, management of a malnourished child with xerophthalmia, and prophylaxis programmes. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Vitamin A deficiency

  • Causes: malnutrition, measles, diarrhoea, poverty; children 6 months to 6 years.

WHO classification of xerophthalmia

  • XN night blindness, X1A conjunctival xerosis, X1B Bitot spots, X2 corneal xerosis, X3A corneal ulceration (less than one third), X3B keratomalacia (one third or more), XS corneal scar, XF xerophthalmic fundus.

Management of a malnourished child with xerophthalmia

  • Vitamin A: 200,000 IU orally on days 1, 2 and 14 (half dose 100,000 IU for infants 6–12 months; 50,000 IU under 6 months); treat corneal ulcer with antibiotics, protect the cornea; treat malnutrition, infections, diarrhoea and measles; nutritional rehabilitation; health education.

Prophylaxis programmes

  • National Vitamin A Prophylaxis Programme (India): 100,000 IU at 9 months (with measles vaccine), then 200,000 IU every 6 months until 5 years (9 doses).
  • Dietary diversification (green leafy vegetables, carrots, mango, eggs), food fortification, breastfeeding promotion.

Revise from: Community Ophthalmology for MBBS Students: Blindness Definitions, NPCBVI, VISION 2020, Eye Donation and Eye Banking

Diabetes & Screening

CM 17. Design a suitable community screening programme for diabetic retinopathy and discuss strategies to reduce ocular morbidity in diabetics. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Community screening for diabetic retinopathy

  • Target: all known diabetics (registered in NCD clinics and pharmacies), annually.
  • Method: non-mydriatic or handheld fundus cameras with trained technicians, two-field 45° images; grading by trained graders, tele-ophthalmology or AI (autonomous systems); referral pathways for referable DR (moderate NPDR or worse, maculopathy).
  • Infrastructure: vision centres, mobile vans, primary health centres, NPCBVI and NPCDCS (now NP-NCD) integration.
  • Quality assurance: grading audits, ungradable images referral.

Strategies to reduce ocular morbidity

  • Systemic control (sugar, BP, lipids), awareness campaigns, treatment centres (laser, anti-VEGF, vitrectomy), follow-up tracking, reducing costs, involvement of physicians and endocrinologists, early cataract surgery with DR management, glaucoma screening.

Revise from: Diabetic and Hypertensive Retinopathy for MBBS Students: Signs, Grading, Screening and Treatment, Online Eye Consultations in India: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, What Can Be Prescribed and When to See the Patient

Rehabilitation & Disability

CM 18. Discuss the various approaches to rehabilitation of the visually handicapped, including problems in visually handicapped children. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Approaches to rehabilitation

  • Medical: optimise vision with low vision aids (magnifiers, telescopes, electronic devices, apps), illumination, contrast.
  • Educational: Braille, large-print books, integrated education (inclusive schools), special schools.
  • Vocational: training, employment reservations (RPwD Act 2016), self-employment schemes.
  • Social: mobility training (white cane, guide dogs), activities of daily living, counselling for psychological adjustment, family support.
  • Community-based rehabilitation (CBR) involving the family and community.
  • Government benefits: disability certificate (UDID card), pensions, travel concessions, scholarships, assistive devices (ADIP scheme).

Problems in visually handicapped children

  • Delayed motor and social development, learning difficulties, stereotyped behaviours (blindisms), overprotection, school drop-out, associated disabilities (cerebral palsy, hearing loss), need for early intervention and family counselling.

Revise from: Living With Low Vision: Magnifiers, Lighting, Phone Tools and Support, Low Vision Assessment Step by Step: History, Charts, Contrast, Magnification and Rehabilitation

CM 19. Critically evaluate visual disability categories and percentages based on severity as per current norms. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Current norms (RPwD Act 2016 and DEPwD assessment guidelines)

  • Visual impairment is graded from best-corrected acuity in the better and the worse eye, and from the visual field, using a matrix that gives a percentage and a category.
  • Matrix (2018 guidelines, as on the UDID portal): category 0 (0–10%), I (20%), II one-eyed person (30%: better eye 6/6–6/18, worse eye less than 3/60 to NPL), III a–e low vision (40–80%), IV a–b blindness (90–100%).
  • Field criteria: less than 40° up to 20° (or hemianopia involving the macula) grades as 6/24–6/60; less than 20° up to 10° as less than 6/60–3/60; less than 10° as less than 3/60.
  • Benchmark disability (for benefits) needs 40% or more.
  • Act definitions: blindness is best-corrected acuity below 3/60 in the better eye or field under 10°; low vision is acuity below 6/18 up to 3/60 or field under 40° up to 10°.
  • Assessment guidelines re-notified on 12 March 2024 (S.O. 1338(E)): confirm the current table in the gazette before certifying; certificate issued by the medical authority through the UDID portal.

Critical evaluation

  • Strengths: simple, uses best-corrected acuity and fields, recognises one-eyed persons and WHO-like thresholds.
  • Limitations: near vision, contrast sensitivity, binocular function and functional vision are not assessed; difficult in young children and in people with other disabilities; field testing needs reliable perimetry; malingering needs objective tests.

Revise from: Visual Disability Certificate Calculator (India, RPwD Act)

CM 20. Recent advances in the alleviation of irreversible blindness — low vision aids, stem cells and retinal implants. · outline

Low vision aids

  • Optical (magnifiers, telescopes, prismatic spectacles), electronic (video magnifiers, head-mounted displays), smartphone apps (text and object recognition), visual rehabilitation (eccentric viewing training).

Stem cells

  • Limbal stem cells (CLET, SLET) for corneal blindness, iPSC and ESC-derived RPE patches for AMD (early trials), photoreceptor precursor transplants (research), corneal endothelial cell injection therapy.

Gene therapy

  • Voretigene neparvovec (RPE65), gene therapy trials for RPGR, choroideremia, achromatopsia and LHON; optogenetics (sonpiretigene isteparvovec: MCO-010) for advanced RP.

Retinal implants and artificial vision

  • Epiretinal (Argus II: discontinued), subretinal (Alpha IMS, PRIMA photovoltaic implant for geographic atrophy with promising trial results), suprachoroidal; cortical visual prostheses (Orion, Gennaris).

Others

  • Keratoprosthesis (Boston KPro, OOKP), neuroprotection research, complement inhibitors for GA, sensory substitution devices (BrainPort), brain-computer interfaces.

Revise from: Low Vision Aids Calculator: Kestenbaum Rule and Magnification, Low Vision Assessment Step by Step: History, Charts, Contrast, Magnification and Rehabilitation

CM 21. Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 and the current visual disability certification guidelines. · outline

Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016

  • Replaced the Persons with Disabilities Act 1995; aligned with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities; recognises 21 specified disabilities including blindness and low vision.
  • Benchmark disability: 40% or more of a specified disability.
  • Rights: equality and non-discrimination, accessibility (buildings, transport, information, digital), education (inclusive; free education for children aged 6–18 with benchmark disabilities), employment (4% reservation in government jobs; 1% for blindness and low vision), 5% reservation in higher education, social security, health care, legal capacity, protection from abuse; penalties for offences.
  • Certification: Unique Disability ID (UDID) card through medical authorities.

Visual disability certification guidelines

  • Based on best-corrected visual acuity in the better and the worse eye and visual fields, using a matrix (DEPwD guidelines 2018, re-notified 2024) giving categories and percentages from 0% to 100%: one-eyed person 30%, low vision 40–80%, blindness 90–100%.
  • Assessment by medical board (ophthalmologist); temporary certificates for children and progressive disease; review periods; check the current gazette notification before certifying.

Revise from: Visual Disability Certificate Calculator (India, RPwD Act)

Research Methodology & Biostatistics

CM 22. Study designs — observational and prospective studies; randomised controlled trials and randomisation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Observational studies

  • Descriptive: case reports, case series, cross-sectional surveys (prevalence; e.g. RAAB).
  • Analytical: case-control (start with outcome, look back for exposure; odds ratio; good for rare diseases), cohort (start with exposure, follow for outcome; prospective or retrospective; relative risk and incidence), ecological.

Prospective studies

  • Data collected forward in time from the start of the study; better data quality, can calculate incidence; costly and long; loss to follow-up.

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)

  • Experimental studies where participants are randomly allocated to intervention or control; gold standard for evaluating treatments; designs: parallel, crossover, factorial, cluster, non-inferiority, paired-eye (one eye each arm).

Randomisation

  • Purpose: removes selection bias, balances known and unknown confounders.
  • Methods: simple (computer-generated), block (equal groups), stratified (balance key factors such as baseline IOP), cluster, minimisation.
  • Allocation concealment (sealed opaque envelopes, central randomisation) prevents foreknowledge.
  • Blinding: single, double, triple; intention-to-treat analysis; CONSORT reporting guidelines; trial registration (CTRI in India).

Revise from: Biostatistics and Research Methods for Ophthalmologists

CM 23. Bias and confounding — sources and methods to avoid them (e.g. in a glaucoma drug trial). DNB / UNIV · outline

Bias

  • Systematic error leading to incorrect estimates.
  • Selection bias (non-random allocation, volunteer bias, loss to follow-up), measurement or information bias (observer bias in IOP measurement, recall bias, instrument error), confounding, publication bias, attrition bias, lead-time bias (screening), performance bias (unequal care).

Confounding

  • A third factor associated with both exposure and outcome that distorts the association (e.g. age when studying a drug’s effect on glaucoma progression; central corneal thickness affecting IOP readings).

Methods to avoid them (e.g. in a glaucoma drug trial)

  • Design: randomisation (balances confounders), allocation concealment, masking of patients and IOP measurers (masked Goldmann readings: two observers, dial hidden), identical bottles, standard measurement times (diurnal variation), washout period for previous drugs, inclusion criteria defining glaucoma clearly, stratification by baseline IOP, adequate sample size, minimising drop-outs.
  • Analysis: intention-to-treat, multivariable regression or stratified analysis for confounders, sensitivity analyses, pre-registered protocol and outcomes.

Revise from: Biostatistics and Research Methods for Ophthalmologists

CM 24. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value. DNB / UNIV · outline

Definitions (2 × 2 table of test result vs disease)

  • Sensitivity = true positives / all with disease (TP / (TP + FN)): ability to detect disease; high sensitivity rules out disease when negative (SnNout).
  • Specificity = true negatives / all without disease (TN / (TN + FP)): high specificity rules in when positive (SpPin).
  • Positive predictive value (PPV) = TP / (TP + FP): probability of disease if the test is positive.
  • Negative predictive value (NPV) = TN / (TN + FN): probability of no disease if the test is negative.

Key points

  • Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test; predictive values depend on prevalence (screening for glaucoma in a low-prevalence population gives a low PPV and many false positives).
  • Likelihood ratios (LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity)) combine both; ROC curves show trade-off at different cut-offs (area under the curve).

Example

  • IOP over 21 mmHg as a glaucoma screening test has poor sensitivity (misses normal-tension glaucoma) and poor specificity (ocular hypertension); combining tests improves performance.

CM 25. Measures of central tendency and variability; standard deviation, confidence interval and tests of significance. DNB / UNIV · outline

Measures of central tendency

  • Mean (average; affected by outliers), median (middle value; better for skewed data, e.g. endothelial cell loss), mode (most frequent value).

Measures of variability

  • Range, interquartile range (with median), variance, standard deviation (spread of individual values around the mean; in a normal distribution, mean ± 1 SD contains about 68% and ± 2 SD about 95% of values), coefficient of variation (SD/mean; used for endothelial polymegathism).

Standard error and confidence interval

  • Standard error of the mean = SD/√n (precision of the sample mean).
  • 95% confidence interval = mean ± 1.96 SE: range likely to contain the true population value; if a CI for a difference excludes 0 (or 1 for ratios), the result is significant at p < 0.05.

Tests of significance

  • Null hypothesis, p value (probability of the observed result if the null is true), type I error (alpha, false positive) and type II error (beta, false negative), power (1 − beta).
  • Parametric: Student t-test (paired for two eyes or before-after; unpaired for two groups), ANOVA (more than two groups), Pearson correlation.
  • Non-parametric: Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman correlation.
  • Categorical: chi-square test, Fisher exact test (small numbers), McNemar test (paired).
  • Survival: Kaplan–Meier, log-rank test, Cox regression; agreement: Bland–Altman plots, kappa.
  • Clinical vs statistical significance; correction for using both eyes of one patient (inter-eye correlation).

CM 27. Clinical audit in ophthalmology — for example, monitoring cataract surgical outcomes. NEW · outline

Clinical audit

  • A quality improvement cycle: measure current practice against agreed standards, implement change, and re-measure.

Steps (audit cycle)

  • Choose topic (important, common, costly, high-risk), set standards (e.g. RCOphth or WHO benchmarks), collect data (prospective or retrospective), compare with the standard, identify reasons for gaps, implement changes (training, protocols), re-audit to close the loop.

Example: cataract surgical outcomes

  • Standards: WHO targets (good outcome 6/18 or better in over 80% with presenting vision and over 90% with best correction), posterior capsule rupture rate (RCOphth benchmark about 2% or less, adjusted for case complexity), endophthalmitis rate, refractive outcome (within 0.5 D of target in about 55% and within 1 D in about 85%).
  • Tools: cataract surgery record forms, software (EPOC or national audit tools), monitoring curves.
  • Action: identify causes of poor outcomes (selection, surgery, spectacles, sequelae), retrain surgeons, improve biometry.

Audit vs research

  • Audit checks whether we do what we should; research finds out what we should do; audit usually does not need ethics approval but must protect confidentiality.

CM 28. Evidence-based ophthalmology — levels of evidence, systematic reviews and meta-analysis. NEW · outline

Evidence-based ophthalmology

  • Integration of best research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values (Sackett); steps: ask a focused question (PICO), search, appraise, apply, evaluate.

Levels of evidence (Oxford CEBM)

  • Level 1: systematic reviews of RCTs; level 2: individual RCTs; level 3: cohort studies; level 4: case-control studies; level 5: case series and expert opinion; GRADE system rates quality of evidence (high to very low) and strength of recommendation.

Systematic reviews

  • Clearly defined question, comprehensive search, predefined inclusion criteria, risk-of-bias assessment, synthesis; Cochrane Eyes and Vision group; PRISMA reporting; registration (PROSPERO).

Meta-analysis

  • Statistical pooling of results from several studies to obtain a combined estimate (forest plot); fixed-effect vs random-effects models; heterogeneity (I² statistic); publication bias (funnel plot); sensitivity analysis.

Limitations

  • Garbage in, garbage out; heterogeneity; publication bias; surrogate outcomes.

Examples in ophthalmology

  • Cochrane reviews on anti-VEGF for AMD, intracameral antibiotics, blue-light filtering spectacles, atropine for myopia.

CM 29. Health economics in eye care — cost-effectiveness, QALYs and DALYs. NEW · outline

Health economics in eye care

  • Resources are limited; economic evaluation compares costs and outcomes to choose interventions giving the most health gain.

Types of economic evaluation

  • Cost-minimisation (equal outcomes), cost-effectiveness analysis (cost per natural unit, e.g. per case of blindness prevented), cost-utility analysis (cost per QALY or DALY), cost-benefit analysis (outcomes in money).
  • Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) = difference in cost / difference in effect; thresholds (WHO: under 1–3 times GDP per capita per DALY averted; NICE: £20,000–30,000 per QALY).

QALY and DALY

  • QALY (quality-adjusted life year): years of life weighted by quality (utility 0–1); vision loss reduces utility values (time trade-off, standard gamble).
  • DALY (disability-adjusted life year): years of life lost plus years lived with disability; used in the Global Burden of Disease studies.

Examples

  • Cataract surgery and spectacles are among the most cost-effective health interventions; SLT first-line (LiGHT) more cost-effective than drops; bevacizumab vs ranibizumab; DR screening with AI; productivity losses from vision impairment (estimated in hundreds of billions of dollars globally).
