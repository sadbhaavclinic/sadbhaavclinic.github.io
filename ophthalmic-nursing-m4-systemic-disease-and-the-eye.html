Ophthalmic Nursing Module 4: Systemic Disease and the Eye

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Diabetes: yearly retinal check for everyone, from diagnosis in type 2 and from 5 years after diagnosis (or puberty) in type 1; more often in pregnancy.
  • Thyroid eye disease: smoking makes it worse; reduced vision or colour vision means optic nerve compression, an emergency.
  • Hydroxychloroquine users need baseline and then yearly retinal screening after about 5 years (earlier if at high risk).
  • High blood pressure can damage the retina; a very high reading with swollen discs is a medical emergency.

Why systemic disease matters to the eye nurse

Many patients in eye clinics have diabetes, high blood pressure or other long-term conditions. Their eye problems are often the first sign that the general condition is poorly controlled, and their medicines can affect eye treatment and surgery. The nurse is often the person who asks the right question and checks blood pressure or blood sugar.

Diabetes

Eye effectWhat to know
Diabetic retinopathyDamage to small retinal vessels: microaneurysms, haemorrhages, exudates; later new vessels (proliferative) that bleed. Silent until late.
Diabetic macular oedemaSwelling of the central retina; the commonest cause of vision loss in diabetes. Treated with anti-VEGF injections, steroids or laser.
CataractEarlier and more often; surgery risks (macular oedema, infection) are higher.
Refraction changesHigh sugar makes vision fluctuate; glasses should be prescribed when sugar is stable.
Nerve palsiesSudden double vision from third, fourth or sixth nerve palsy; often recovers within months, but other causes must be excluded.
Neovascular glaucomaNew vessels block drainage; painful, high IOP.
  • Screening: type 2 diabetes at diagnosis, then yearly if normal; type 1 within 5 years of diagnosis (or by puberty), then yearly; pregnant women with pre-existing diabetes early in pregnancy and each trimester.
  • Risk control: blood sugar (HbA1c), blood pressure, cholesterol, stopping smoking. Rapid tightening of sugar control can worsen retinopathy briefly, so eye checks are needed when treatment intensifies.
  • Before surgery: follow the unit protocol on acceptable sugar levels, fasting and insulin adjustments; hypoglycaemia during fasting is a real risk.

Patient tools: diabetes eye action plan (English, Gujarati, Hindi).

High blood pressure

  • Hypertensive retinopathy: narrowed arteries, haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots; in very high blood pressure, swollen optic discs (needs emergency medical care).
  • Blood pressure is a risk factor for retinal vein and artery occlusions and for ischaemic optic neuropathy.
  • Check BP in every patient with a vein occlusion, subconjunctival haemorrhage that keeps recurring, or before surgery as per protocol. Very high readings before surgery are reported to the surgeon and anaesthetist.

Thyroid eye disease (Graves’ orbitopathy)

  • Signs: lid retraction (staring look), bulging eyes (proptosis), red swollen lids and conjunctiva, double vision, dry gritty eyes.
  • Danger: compression of the optic nerve (falling vision, faded colours) or a cornea that cannot be covered. Both need urgent treatment.
  • Nursing: encourage stopping smoking (it worsens the disease and treatment response), lubricants, sleeping with the head raised, sunglasses; monitor during intravenous steroid courses (blood sugar, blood pressure, liver tests as ordered).

Neurological conditions

  • Multiple sclerosis: optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia (double vision on side gaze), nystagmus.
  • Myasthenia gravis: drooping lids and double vision that vary and get worse with tiredness. The ice-pack test (lid improves after cooling) supports the diagnosis. Watch for breathing or swallowing difficulty (myasthenic crisis).
  • Stroke: field loss (hemianopia), double vision, eye movement problems. Sudden vision loss can be a warning stroke (amaurosis fugax).
  • Pituitary tumours: bitemporal field loss; may need urgent care if sudden headache and vision loss (pituitary apoplexy).
  • Raised intracranial pressure: papilloedema; idiopathic intracranial hypertension often affects young women with weight gain.

Rheumatological and immune conditions

ConditionEye effects
Rheumatoid arthritisDry eye, scleritis, corneal melting
Sjögren syndromeSevere dry eye and dry mouth
Systemic lupus erythematosusDry eye, retinal vasculitis; hydroxychloroquine toxicity risk
Ankylosing spondylitis (HLA-B27)Recurrent acute anterior uveitis
Giant cell arteritisSudden vision loss from optic nerve ischaemia; urgent steroids
Behçet disease, sarcoidosisUveitis, retinal vasculitis
Juvenile idiopathic arthritisSilent anterior uveitis in children; regular screening

Infections

  • HIV: cytomegalovirus retinitis at low CD4 counts (now less common with antiretroviral therapy), herpes zoster in young adults, ocular surface squamous neoplasia, Kaposi sarcoma.
  • Tuberculosis: uveitis and choroiditis; the TB drug ethambutol can damage the optic nerve, so patients should report blurred vision or colour changes.
  • Herpes zoster ophthalmicus: shingles on the forehead; oral antivirals within 72 hours; eye review if the nose tip is involved or the eye is red.
  • Syphilis: can cause any type of uveitis; blood tests are part of uveitis work-up.
  • Toxoplasmosis: retinochoroiditis, often recurrent.

Nutrition and the eye

  • Vitamin A deficiency: night blindness, conjunctival and corneal dryness, Bitot spots, keratomalacia. India’s programme gives vitamin A doses to young children.
  • AREDS2 supplements: reduce progression only in intermediate or advanced AMD; not for everyone.
  • Tobacco and alcohol: toxic optic neuropathy (with poor nutrition), cataract and AMD risk from smoking.

Pregnancy

  • Diabetic retinopathy may worsen; check each trimester if diabetic.
  • Headache with blurred vision or flashing lights may be pre-eclampsia: check blood pressure and urine protein urgently.
  • Many eye drops are used with care; always tell the doctor about pregnancy or breastfeeding. See pregnancy and your eyes.

Go deeper: thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis, hypertensive retinopathy, diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy, TB, sarcoid and syphilis uveitis.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

When should a person with type 2 diabetes have the first eye check?

At diagnosis, then at least yearly if no retinopathy.

Which prostate medicine must be recorded before cataract surgery and why?

Tamsulosin (and similar alpha-blockers): it causes floppy iris syndrome during surgery.

A thyroid eye disease patient says colours look faded. What does it mean?

Possible optic nerve compression: an emergency needing same-day review.

What eye problem can ethambutol cause?

Optic neuropathy: reduced vision and colour vision; patients should report changes.

A pregnant woman has headache and flashing lights. What do you check?

Blood pressure and urine protein urgently for pre-eclampsia, and inform the doctor.

Show answer

Answer: Retina. Screening needed.

Show answer

Answer: HLA-B27. Ankylosing spondylitis.

Show answer

Answer: Myasthenia gravis. Fatigable ptosis.

6. Topiramate can cause:

Show answer

Answer: Bilateral acute angle closure. Ciliary body swelling.

7. Cytomegalovirus retinitis is seen in:

Show answer

Answer: Advanced HIV with low CD4. Less common with ART.

Show answer

Answer: Tuberculosis. Optic neuropathy risk.
