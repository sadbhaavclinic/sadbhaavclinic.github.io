Eye Drop Packaging: Bottles, Cap Colours, Preservatives and Novelia

Last updated: October 2026

An eye drop is a drug and a device. The bottle decides how big each drop is, whether a preservative is needed, how long the drop stays sterile after opening, and whether an elderly patient can tell one drug from another. This page reviews the packaging of eye drops, from the ordinary dropper bottle and cap colours to unit-dose vials, Novelia and other preservative-free multidose bottles, and the newer devices that replace the drop altogether.

Why packaging matters: five problems a bottle must solve

1. Drop sizeA standard bottle delivers about 40 µL per drop, but the tear film holds only about 7–10 µL. Most of every drop overflows onto the cheek or drains into the nose, where it is absorbed into the blood.
2. Sterility after openingA multidose bottle is opened every day for weeks. Either a preservative or the bottle design must stop germs from growing inside.
3. Ocular surface toxicityThe commonest preservative, benzalkonium chloride (BAK), damages epithelium and goblet cells with long-term use. This matters most in glaucoma and dry eye, where drops are used for years.
4. IdentificationPatients with poor vision or literacy pick their drops by cap colour and bottle shape. Similar-looking bottles cause mix-ups, including with glue tubes.
5. Handling and adherenceArthritis, tremor and poor vision make squeezing and aiming hard. Stiff bottles and large drops waste medicine, so bottles run out before the refill is due.

The standard dropper bottle

Most eye drops in India still come in a 5 mL or 10 mL low-density polyethylene (LDPE) squeeze bottle. It has three parts: the soft bottle, a nozzle insert (the dropper tip) pressed into its neck, and a screw cap. A tamper-evident ring or shrink band shows the bottle has not been opened. The bottle is filled and sealed sterile, but once opened, every use lets air and possibly germs in, so the solution needs a preservative.

What sets drop sizeEffect
Tip orifice and outer diameterThe main factor. A wider tip makes a bigger drop; this is fixed by the manufacturer.
Angle of the bottleHolding the bottle vertical gives the most consistent drop; tilting it changes the size.
Squeeze force and speedA hard or fast squeeze can release a stream or a double drop.
Viscosity and surface tensionGels, suspensions and solutions with surfactants (including BAK) form different-sized drops.
VolumeApproximate figure
Drop from a standard bottleAbout 40 µL on average (39.8 µL across nine medications in one 2023 study); published products range from about 25 µL to over 70 µL
Volume the tear film can usefully holdRoughly 10–20 µL; drops above about 20 µL overflow without adding effect
Drops in a 2.5 mL bottleAbout 70 with a standard tip, about 184 with a drop-reducing adaptor
Clinical point: one drop is always enough. A second drop in the same eye only washes out the first. If two different drops are prescribed, wait about 5 minutes between them, and ask the patient to close the eyes or press on the inner corner for a minute to reduce systemic absorption (important for timolol in asthma or heart block).

A preserved multidose bottle is usually discarded 4 weeks after opening; always follow the label. Soft contact lenses absorb BAK, so lens wearers should remove lenses before preserved drops and wait about 15 minutes before putting them back.

Cap colours: the AAO colour code

Since 1983 the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) has recommended a standard cap and label colour for each class of topical eye drug, so that patients and staff can spot a wrong bottle. The latest revision (2015) has 13 colours, each defined by a Pantone shade. The system is voluntary: it was developed with the FDA and US industry, and most US manufacturers follow it, but no law requires it. Indian manufacturers are not bound by it, so cap colours vary between brands.

Remember the combinations: any fixed combination containing timolol has a dark blue cap, not the colour of the other drug. A combination of an adrenergic agonist without a beta-blocker (brinzolamide-brimonidine) is light green. Netarsudil and latanoprost-netarsudil have no AAO colour class of their own; check the label. The most dangerous mix-up is tan (antibiotic) for pink (steroid), because a steroid on an undiagnosed infective keratitis can be disastrous. See also our AAO colour codes page.

Unit-dose vials

Single-dose units are small LDPE ampoules (about 0.3–0.5 mL) made by blow-fill-seal: the plastic is moulded, filled and sealed in one sterile step. They contain no preservative, because each vial is used once and thrown away. They are used for lubricants in severe dry eye, for preservative-free glaucoma drops (for example tafluprost), after corneal surgery, and in patients allergic to preservatives.

AdvantagesDrawbacks
No preservative; sterile until openedCostly per dose
Easy to carry; no in-use period to rememberTwist-off tops are hard to open with arthritis, and a sharp edge can scratch the eye
Good for occasional or short-term useEach vial holds several drops, so most is wasted, and the plastic adds up
Patients often keep and reuse an opened vial, which defeats the purpose

Preservative-free multidose bottles: Novelia, OSD, ABAK and COMOD

These bottles give the convenience of one bottle with the safety of no preservative. All of them must solve the same two problems: stop liquid that has touched the eye from flowing back into the bottle, and replace the volume of each drop with air that cannot carry germs in. They do it in two main ways: by filtering, or with a one-way valve at the tip.

System (maker)How it worksNotes
Novelia (Nemera)A one-way silicone valve at the tip lets a drop out and closes, so no liquid flows back. Air re-enters through a separate solid silicone membrane (PureFlow) with no pores. Some versions add silver to the cap or tip.Introduced 2010 (by Rexam, now Nemera). The maker reports over 550 products in more than 55 countries, including Systane Hydration PF in the US. Five valve sizes set the drop size. Labels warn people allergic to silver where silver is used.
OSD, Ophthalmic Squeeze Dispenser (Aptar)A spring-loaded tip seal opens only above a set squeeze pressure and closes straight after the drop. Returning air passes a 0.2 µm sterile air filter; the liquid itself is never filtered and touches no metal.Launched 2011; the device in Restasis MultiDose (FDA approved 2016). Works with solutions, emulsions and suspensions.
ABAK (Laboratoires Théa)A filter membrane in the tip that both drops and returning air must pass. Early versions removed the preservative from a preserved solution; current versions hold preservative-free solutions.Kept sterile for up to 3 months after opening (per the maker). Suits thin solutions only; the bottle needs a fairly firm squeeze.
COMOD (Ursapharm, AeroPump technology)An airless pump: the solution sits in a bag that collapses as it empties, so no air enters. A silver coating or wire near the outlet protects the tip.Used for Hylo-Comod lubricants; up to 6 months after opening. Works as a pump rather than a soft squeeze bottle, so patients need to be shown how to use it.
Practical points. These bottles cost more than standard bottles but usually less per dose than unit-dose vials. Many need priming or a firmer squeeze, so show the patient once. The in-use period (from 1 to 6 months) is set for each product: read it on the label rather than assuming 4 weeks. Tip hygiene still matters, because a tip valve protects the bottle, not a tip that has touched the lashes.

Safety lessons from packaging

  • Contaminated artificial tears (US, 2023). EzriCare Artificial Tears, made by Global Pharma Healthcare, were linked to a carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa outbreak. CDC counted 81 patients, with 4 deaths, about 14 with vision loss and 4 enucleations; even unopened bottles were contaminated. The lesson: manufacturing quality matters, and patients with an unusual infection should be asked about every drop they use, including over-the-counter tears.
  • Glue mix-ups. Small tubes of nail glue and superglue look like single-dose eye drops. Case reports describe glue instilled in error, sealing the lids shut. Advise patients to store eye drops away from glues and other small bottles (ear drops, nail products).
  • Look-alike bottles. The same company often uses identical bottles for different drugs. Teach patients to read the name, not just the cap colour, and use the timetable to separate drops.
  • Tip contact. Touching the lashes or lid with the tip contaminates the bottle. This is a common cause of contaminated in-use bottles, whatever the packaging.

Which pack for whom: a quick guide

PatientBest packaging choice
Short course after surgery or for infectionStandard preserved bottle is fine; discard at the end of the course
Glaucoma on 2–3 drugs for years, or with ocular surface diseasePreservative-free (unit-dose or multidose PF bottle), BAK-free (Polyquad, Purite, SofZia) or a fixed combination to cut the BAK load; consider SLT to reduce drops
Moderate to severe dry eye using tears more than 4 times a dayPreservative-free tears: multidose PF bottle for daily use, unit-dose for travel
Contact lens wearerPreservative-free drops, or remove lenses before preserved drops
Arthritis, tremor or poor visionEasy-squeeze bottle plus a drop guide or squeezer; avoid twist-off vials; consider SLT or an implant in glaucoma
Allergy to a preservative (BAK, thiomersal)Preservative-free; check for silver in some PF bottles if allergic to silver

Where residents slip

  • Assuming every preservative-free bottle lasts 4 weeks: in-use periods range from single use to 6 months; read the label.
  • Giving a timolol combination the colour of its partner drug: all beta-blocker combinations are dark blue.
  • Counselling by cap colour alone: the AAO code is voluntary, and Indian brands often differ.
  • Saying "preservative-free" means "sterile forever": a PF bottle protects its contents, not a tip touched to the lashes.
  • Prescribing two drops "together": the second washes out the first; wait 5 minutes.
