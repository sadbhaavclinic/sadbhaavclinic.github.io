Applied Anatomy & Physiology

RE 1. Blood supply of the retina and optic nerve. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Retina

  • Inner two-thirds: central retinal artery (branch of ophthalmic artery) → four branch arterioles; end-arteries; superficial and deep capillary plexuses; foveal avascular zone.
  • Outer third (photoreceptors, RPE): choriocapillaris from short posterior ciliary arteries (diffusion).
  • Cilioretinal artery (about 15–30% of eyes) from the choroidal circulation: may spare central vision in CRAO.
  • Venous drainage: central retinal vein → superior ophthalmic vein, cavernous sinus.

Optic nerve

  • Surface nerve fibre layer: CRA branches.
  • Prelaminar and laminar: short posterior ciliary arteries, circle of Zinn–Haller.
  • Retrolaminar: pial plexus and CRA branches. Intracanalicular and intracranial: ophthalmic artery, internal carotid, anterior cerebral and superior hypophyseal arteries.

Clinical relevance

  • CRAO vs NAION and AAION (posterior ciliary arteries), watershed zones, glaucoma and perfusion pressure.

RE 2. RPE and its significance. · outline

Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)

  • Single layer of hexagonal cells from the neuroectoderm (outer layer of the optic cup); apical microvilli interdigitate with photoreceptor outer segments; tight junctions form the outer blood–retinal barrier; basement membrane forms the inner layer of Bruch’s membrane.

Functions

  • Phagocytosis of shed outer segment discs (each cell serves 30–40 photoreceptors), visual cycle (regeneration of 11-cis retinal: RPE65), absorption of stray light (melanin), transport of nutrients and ions, removal of water (keeps retina attached), secretion of growth factors (VEGF towards choroid, PEDF towards retina), immune privilege, antioxidant protection.

Significance in disease

  • AMD (lipofuscin, drusen, GA, PED), RPE65 dystrophy (gene therapy), Best disease (bestrophin), Stargardt (A2E accumulation), CSCR (RPE leak), CHRPE, RPE tears after anti-VEGF, PVR (RPE cells migrate), toxic retinopathies (hydroxychloroquine).
RE 3. Posterior vitreous detachment; photopsia and floaters; asteroid hyalosis and synchysis scintillans. DNB / UNIV · outline

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)

  • Separation of the posterior vitreous cortex from the ILM; age-related liquefaction (synchysis) and collapse (syneresis); earlier in myopia, trauma, inflammation, after cataract surgery.

Symptoms

  • Photopsia (traction on the retina), floaters (Weiss ring: glial tissue from the disc margin), cobwebs.

Significance

  • Retinal tears in 10–15% of symptomatic PVD (more if vitreous haemorrhage or pigment in vitreous: Shafer sign).
  • Examine with dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy and indentation; repeat at 4–6 weeks; advise on RD symptoms.

Asteroid hyalosis

  • Calcium-phospholipid bodies suspended in a normal vitreous; unilateral mostly; older people, diabetes; usually asymptomatic; may hinder fundus view and biometry (vitrectomy rarely).

Synchysis scintillans

  • Cholesterol crystals settling in a liquefied vitreous; after vitreous haemorrhage or trauma; golden-brown, shower on eye movement; usually no treatment.
RE 4. Cherry-red spot — causes. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Cherry-red spot

  • Thin fovea (no ganglion cells) shows the normal choroid while the surrounding retina is opaque (oedema or storage).

Causes

  • Vascular: CRAO, commotio retinae (Berlin oedema), quinine toxicity.
  • Lipid storage disorders: Tay–Sachs (GM2, hexosaminidase A), Sandhoff, Niemann–Pick (types A and B), GM1 gangliosidosis, Farber, sialidosis (cherry-red spot myoclonus syndrome), metachromatic leukodystrophy (some).
  • Others: Gaucher (some), methanol poisoning (oedema), dapsone.

Age-related Macular Degeneration

RE 5. Pathology, clinical features, FFA picture, differential diagnosis and management of ARMD (dry and wet); critically analyse the changing scenario and recent advances in its management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathology

  • Drusen (between RPE basal lamina and Bruch’s membrane), RPE dysfunction, complement dysregulation (CFH, ARMS2), oxidative stress; geographic atrophy (dry); choroidal neovascularisation (wet: type 1 sub-RPE, type 2 subretinal, type 3 retinal angiomatous proliferation).

Clinical features

  • Distortion, central scotoma, drusen, pigment changes, GA, subretinal fluid, haemorrhage, PED, disciform scar.

FFA

  • Classic CNV: early lacy hyperfluorescence with late leakage; occult: stippled late leakage, fibrovascular PED; GA: window defect. ICGA for PCV.

Differential diagnosis

  • PCV, CSCR, myopic CNV, pattern dystrophies, adult vitelliform dystrophy, choroidal tumours.

Management and changing scenario

  • Dry: AREDS2 supplements (intermediate AMD), Amsler grid, low-vision aids; GA: complement inhibitors (pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol; modest slowing).
  • Wet: from laser (MPS) and PDT (TAP) to anti-VEGF (ranibizumab: MARINA, ANCHOR; bevacizumab: CATT, IVAN; aflibercept: VIEW), then longer-acting agents (faricimab, brolucizumab, aflibercept 8 mg), treat-and-extend, biosimilars, port delivery; gene therapy under study.

Revise from: AMD: The Current Anti-VEGF Landscape, Geographic Atrophy Treatment: Pegcetacoplan and Avacincaptad Pegol Explained

RE 6. Choroidal neovascular membrane — causes and management (including intrafoveal CNVM). DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes of CNV

  • Degenerative: AMD (commonest in older people), pathological myopia (commonest in young people), angioid streaks.
  • Inflammatory: POHS, multifocal choroiditis, punctate inner choroidopathy, serpiginous, VKH, toxoplasmosis.
  • Others: choroidal rupture, laser burns, optic disc drusen, pattern dystrophies, Best disease, CSCR (chronic), idiopathic.

Types (location)

  • Type 1 (sub-RPE, occult), type 2 (subretinal, classic), type 3 (intraretinal: RAP); subfoveal, juxtafoveal, extrafoveal.

Diagnosis

  • OCT (fluid, hyper-reflective lesion), FFA (classic: early lacy, late leakage; occult: late stippled), ICGA (PCV), OCTA.

Management

  • Anti-VEGF agents for all locations (first line: intrafoveal CNV); regimens: monthly loading then treat-and-extend or PRN.
  • PDT for PCV and some myopic CNV; laser photocoagulation for extrafoveal CNV (historical: MPS).
  • Treat inflammation in inflammatory CNV (steroids, immunosuppression with anti-VEGF).
  • Surgery (submacular surgery, macular translocation, displacement of submacular haemorrhage with tPA and gas) for selected cases.
RE 7. Verteporfin and photodynamic therapy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Verteporfin

  • Photosensitiser (benzoporphyrin derivative); infused IV at 6 mg/m² over 10 minutes; binds LDL receptors on proliferating endothelium.

PDT

  • Non-thermal 689 nm laser at 15 minutes after infusion start (50 J/cm², 83 s) causes free-radical damage and closure of abnormal vessels.

Indications today

  • Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (with anti-VEGF: EVEREST II), chronic CSCR (half-dose or half-fluence), choroidal haemangioma, some myopic CNV; historically classic AMD CNV (TAP).

Side effects

  • Photosensitivity for 48 hours (avoid sunlight), back pain during infusion, visual loss (rare), RPE atrophy.

Revise from: AMD: The Current Anti-VEGF Landscape

RE 8. Geographic atrophy — complement inhibitors (pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol). · outline

Geographic atrophy (GA)

  • Advanced dry AMD: sharply demarcated areas of RPE, photoreceptor and choriocapillaris loss; grows about 1.5–2 mm² per year; foveal sparing at first.

Imaging

  • FAF (hypoautofluorescent areas; junctional hyperautofluorescence patterns predict growth), OCT (cRORA: complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy, hypertransmission).

Complement inhibitors

  • Pegcetacoplan (C3 inhibitor, Syfovre; OAKS and DERBY): monthly or every other month intravitreal injection; slows lesion growth by about 20% (more in later years).
  • Avacincaptad pegol (C5 inhibitor, Izervay; GATHER1 and GATHER2): monthly; slows growth by about 14–18%.
  • No proven improvement in visual acuity; risks: endophthalmitis, conversion to wet AMD (exudation), intraocular inflammation, occlusive retinal vasculitis (pegcetacoplan rare); treatment burden and cost; not approved in Europe for pegcetacoplan.

Other management

  • AREDS2 supplements for fellow eyes, low-vision aids, monitor for CNV; trials: photobiomodulation, gene therapy, oral agents.

Revise from: Geographic Atrophy Treatment: Pegcetacoplan and Avacincaptad Pegol Explained

RE 9. Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy — features, diagnosis and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV)

  • Type 1 CNV variant with branching vascular network and aneurysmal polypoidal dilatations; common in Asians (up to 50% of “wet AMD” in Asia); pachychoroid association.

Features

  • Orange-red subretinal nodules, serosanguinous PEDs, massive submacular haemorrhage, recurrent haemorrhagic PED, breakthrough vitreous haemorrhage.

Diagnosis

  • ICGA (gold standard: early hyperfluorescent polyps, branching network), OCT (sharp peaked PED, notch, double-layer sign, thumb-like polyp, thick choroid), OCTA.

Management

  • Anti-VEGF monotherapy (aflibercept: PLANET trial) or combination with PDT (EVEREST II: better polyp closure and vision with ranibizumab plus PDT), faricimab; thermal laser for extrafoveal polyps; pneumatic displacement with tPA for large submacular haemorrhage.

Revise from: ICG Angiography Indications: PCV, CSCR and Choroiditis

RE 72. Submacular haemorrhage — management options. NEW · outline

Submacular haemorrhage (SMH)

  • Blood between the neurosensory retina and RPE (or under the RPE) at the macula; iron toxicity, fibrin contraction and diffusion barrier damage photoreceptors within days.

Causes

  • Neovascular AMD and PCV (commonest), retinal arterial macroaneurysm, trauma (choroidal rupture), myopic CNV, anticoagulants (risk factor), Valsalva.

Evaluation

  • Size (disc areas), thickness (OCT, B-scan), duration, location (sub-RPE vs subretinal), underlying cause (ICGA for PCV).

Management options

  • Small, thin SMH: anti-VEGF monotherapy.
  • Thick, large, fresh (within about 7–14 days): pneumatic displacement with intravitreal gas (SF6 or C3F8) with or without intravitreal tPA (25–50 µg) and face-down positioning, plus anti-VEGF.
  • Vitrectomy with subretinal tPA injection and gas tamponade; randomised evidence (TIGER trial) has not shown a clear visual advantage of surgery over less invasive options.
  • Massive SMH with breakthrough vitreous haemorrhage: vitrectomy.
  • Treat underlying disease; review anticoagulation with physician (do not stop without advice).

Prognosis

  • Better with small, thin, recent haemorrhage and good presenting vision.

Revise from: AMD: The Current Anti-VEGF Landscape

Diabetic Retinopathy & Macular Oedema

RE 10. Classification and current concepts in the pathology of diabetic retinopathy and their relevance to management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • ETDRS (international clinical scale): no DR, mild, moderate and severe NPDR (4-2-1 rule: haemorrhages in 4 quadrants, venous beading in 2, IRMA in 1), PDR (NVD, NVE, vitreous haemorrhage); high-risk PDR (DRS criteria).
  • DME: centre-involving vs non-centre-involving (OCT); clinically significant macular oedema (ETDRS).

Current concepts in pathology

  • Hyperglycaemia → polyol pathway, advanced glycation end products, protein kinase C activation, oxidative stress → pericyte loss, basement membrane thickening, microaneurysms, capillary closure, ischaemia → VEGF → leakage and neovascularisation; inflammation and neurodegeneration occur early.

Relevance to management

  • Glycaemic, BP and lipid control (DCCT, UKPDS, FIELD fenofibrate), anti-VEGF (blocks VEGF), steroids (inflammation), PRP (reduces ischaemic drive); screening intervals by grade.

Revise from: Diabetic Retinopathy: Classification and Management for Residents

RE 11. Discuss the pathogenesis, features, investigations and management of diffuse diabetic macular oedema (CSME), with recent developments; management of recalcitrant DME. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathogenesis

  • Breakdown of the inner blood–retinal barrier (VEGF, inflammatory cytokines), leakage from microaneurysms and capillaries, Müller cell dysfunction.

Features

  • Diffuse thickening, cysts, hard exudates; CSME (ETDRS definitions); centre-involving DME on OCT.

Investigations

  • OCT (thickness, cysts, subretinal fluid, DRIL, hyper-reflective foci, vitreomacular interface), FFA (leakage, ischaemia), OCTA.

Management

  • Systemic control; anti-VEGF first line for centre-involving DME with vision loss (Protocol T: aflibercept better when vision 6/15 or worse); faricimab and aflibercept 8 mg for longer intervals.
  • Steroids (dexamethasone implant, fluocinolone) for pseudophakic eyes, chronic or recalcitrant DME, patients who cannot attend often.
  • Focal or grid laser for non-centre-involving; Protocol V: observation acceptable if vision good.

Recalcitrant DME

  • Switch anti-VEGF, add or switch to steroid implant, vitrectomy with ILM peel for traction, control systemic factors (BP, renal function, anaemia).

Revise from: OCT Biomarkers in Diabetic Macular Oedema, Landmark Trials in Diabetic Retinopathy and DME: DRS, ETDRS, DRCR Protocols and Anti-VEGF

RE 12. Proliferative diabetic retinopathy — features and management; panretinal photocoagulation; vitrectomy in diabetic retinopathy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Features

  • NVD, NVE, vitreous and preretinal haemorrhage, fibrovascular proliferation, tractional RD; high-risk characteristics (DRS).

PRP

  • 1200–1600 burns of 200–500 µm spots, one burn-width apart, outside the arcades, in 1–3 sessions (or PASCAL patterns); reduces severe visual loss by 50% or more (DRS).
  • Complications: field loss, night vision, DME worsening, choroidal effusion, angle closure.

Anti-VEGF

  • Protocol S: ranibizumab non-inferior to PRP at 2 years (better fields, less DME) but needs adherence; CLARITY: aflibercept.

Vitrectomy

  • Non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage, tractional RD involving or threatening the macula, combined tractional-rhegmatogenous RD, dense premacular haemorrhage, progressive fibrovascular proliferation; pre-op anti-VEGF reduces bleeding; DRVS: early vitrectomy in type 1 diabetics with severe haemorrhage.

Revise from: Diabetic Retinopathy Treatment Ahmedabad

RE 13. ETDRS — classification, findings and significance. · outline

ETDRS (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study)

  • 3711 patients with mild to severe NPDR or early PDR, with or without macular oedema.

Classification

  • ETDRS severity scale (levels 10–85) from 7-field stereo photographs; definition of clinically significant macular oedema (CSME): thickening within 500 µm of the centre; hard exudates within 500 µm with adjacent thickening; thickening of 1 disc area or more within 1 disc diameter of the centre.
  • 4-2-1 rule for severe NPDR.

Findings

  • Focal or grid laser for CSME reduced moderate visual loss by about 50%.
  • Early PRP not recommended for mild to moderate NPDR; consider for severe NPDR or early PDR, especially in type 2 diabetes.
  • Aspirin (650 mg daily) did not affect retinopathy progression or vitreous haemorrhage.

Significance

  • ETDRS chart (logMAR), grading scales and CSME definition are still standards; laser largely replaced by anti-VEGF for centre-involving DME.
RE 14. Role of HbA1c, blood pressure and lipids in diabetic retinopathy; pathogenesis of cotton-wool spots and hard exudates. DNB / UNIV · outline

HbA1c

  • DCCT (type 1) and UKPDS (type 2): intensive control (HbA1c about 7%) reduces onset and progression of retinopathy (by about 25–76%); metabolic memory (EDIC); early worsening with rapid improvement in control (insulin pumps, GLP-1 agonists: monitor).

Blood pressure

  • UKPDS: tight BP control (under 150/85) reduced retinopathy progression by about a third; ACE inhibitors and ARBs (DIRECT).

Lipids

  • Fenofibrate reduces progression and need for laser (FIELD, ACCORD Eye); dyslipidaemia associated with hard exudates.

Pathogenesis of cotton-wool spots

  • Focal retinal ischaemia in the nerve fibre layer: arteriolar occlusion blocks axoplasmic flow; swollen axon ends (cytoid bodies); indicates capillary non-perfusion.

Pathogenesis of hard exudates

  • Lipoprotein deposits in the outer plexiform layer from leaking microaneurysms and capillaries; circinate rings around leaking points; macular star; resorb slowly after leak is treated.

Revise from: Diabetic Retinopathy: Classification and Management for Residents

RE 15. Landmark trials in retina — DRS, ETDRS, DRVS, MARINA/ANCHOR, CATT, SCORE, BRAVO/CRUISE, CVOS/BVOS. DNB / UNIV · outline

Diabetic retinopathy

  • DRS: PRP halves severe visual loss in PDR; high-risk characteristics defined.
  • ETDRS: focal laser for CSME; no early PRP for mild NPDR; aspirin safe.
  • DRVS: early vitrectomy for severe vitreous haemorrhage benefited type 1 diabetics.

AMD

  • MARINA and ANCHOR: monthly ranibizumab improves vision (vs sham and PDT).
  • CATT and IVAN: bevacizumab non-inferior to ranibizumab; PRN similar to monthly.

Retinal vein occlusion

  • BVOS: grid laser for macular oedema (vision 6/12 or worse) and scatter laser for neovascularisation.
  • CVOS: grid laser not useful for CRVO macular oedema; PRP when NVI appears (not prophylactic).
  • SCORE: triamcinolone 1 mg better than observation for CRVO macular oedema; in BRVO, laser remained standard.
  • BRAVO and CRUISE: ranibizumab improves vision in BRVO and CRVO macular oedema.

Revise from: Landmark Trials in Retinal Vein Occlusion: CVOS, BVOS, SCORE, BRAVO, CRUISE and Beyond

RE 16. Indications for ophthalmic referral in juvenile diabetes. DNB / UNIV · outline

Indications for referral in juvenile (type 1) diabetes

  • Screening: start at puberty or 3–5 years after diagnosis (ISPAD: from age 11 years or 2–5 years after diagnosis), then annually (every 2 years if no retinopathy and good control).
  • Refer urgently: any retinopathy beyond mild NPDR, macular oedema, PDR, unexplained drop in vision.
  • Others: cataract (snowflake), refractive fluctuation, pregnancy (more frequent examination: each trimester), rapid improvement in control (early worsening), poor control, hypertension, nephropathy.
  • Also: cranial nerve palsy, papillopathy (diabetic papillopathy), rubeosis.
RE 17. DRCR Retina Network protocols (T, V, S, AA) and their impact on practice. · outline

Protocol T

  • Aflibercept vs bevacizumab vs ranibizumab for centre-involving DME: all improved vision; aflibercept better at 1 year when baseline vision 6/15 or worse; differences narrowed by 2 years.

Protocol V

  • Centre-involving DME with good vision (6/7.5 or better): initial observation, laser or aflibercept gave similar 2-year vision; observation with close follow-up acceptable.

Protocol S

  • PDR: ranibizumab non-inferior to PRP at 2 and 5 years; less DME and field loss with anti-VEGF; loss to follow-up risky (rapid progression).

Protocol AA

  • Ultra-widefield imaging: predominantly peripheral lesions increase risk of progression.

Others

  • Protocol I (ranibizumab with prompt or deferred laser superior to laser alone), Protocol W (anti-VEGF in severe NPDR reduces progression but no visual benefit at 4 years), Protocol AC (bevacizumab first then switch is cost-effective).

Impact

  • Anti-VEGF first-line for DME with vision loss, observation for good vision, anti-VEGF option for PDR with reliable follow-up, cost-effective step therapy.

Revise from: Landmark Trials in Diabetic Retinopathy and DME: DRS, ETDRS, DRCR Protocols and Anti-VEGF

RE 73. OCT biomarkers in diabetic macular oedema and neovascular AMD (DRIL, hyper-reflective foci, fluid compartments). NEW · outline

OCT biomarkers in DME

  • Central subfield thickness (CST), intraretinal cysts, subretinal fluid (inflammatory component; responds to steroids), disorganisation of retinal inner layers (DRIL: predicts poor vision; correlates with ischaemia), hyper-reflective foci (activated microglia, lipid; inflammation; may favour steroids), integrity of the ellipsoid zone and external limiting membrane (predict visual potential), vitreomacular interface (traction, ERM), macular ischaemia on OCTA.

OCT biomarkers in neovascular AMD

  • Intraretinal fluid (worse prognosis), subretinal fluid (tolerated in some regimens, may protect against atrophy), sub-RPE fluid and PED, subretinal hyper-reflective material (SHRM: fibrosis risk), outer retinal atrophy (cRORA), hyper-reflective foci, shallow irregular PED (double-layer sign: type 1 CNV), polyp signs (PCV).

Clinical use

  • Treatment decisions (fluid-guided treat-and-extend), predicting response, choosing steroids vs anti-VEGF, trial end-points; AI fluid quantification is emerging.

Revise from: OCT Biomarkers in Diabetic Macular Oedema

Retinal Vascular Disease

RE 18. Branch retinal vein occlusion — differential diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Branch retinal vein occlusion

  • At arteriovenous crossings (usually superotemporal); risk: hypertension, age, diabetes, glaucoma.

Differential diagnosis

  • Diabetic retinopathy, hypertensive retinopathy, radiation retinopathy, retinal vasculitis, macroaneurysm, ocular ischaemic syndrome.

Management

  • Systemic risk control.
  • Macular oedema: anti-VEGF first line (BRAVO, VIBRANT), dexamethasone implant (GENEVA), grid laser (BVOS) for persistent oedema with good perfusion.
  • Neovascularisation: sector scatter laser when NV develops (not prophylactically, BVOS); vitreous haemorrhage: vitrectomy.

Revise from: Landmark Trials in Retinal Vein Occlusion: CVOS, BVOS, SCORE, BRAVO, CRUISE and Beyond, Retinal Vein Occlusion: BRVO and CRVO Made Practical

RE 19. Central retinal vein occlusion — aetiopathogenesis, features, newer modalities of management, surgical intervention and the CVO trials. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiopathogenesis

  • Thrombosis of the central retinal vein at or behind the lamina cribrosa; risk: age, hypertension, diabetes, glaucoma, hyperviscosity, thrombophilia (young), dehydration, oral contraceptives.

Features

  • Non-ischaemic (perfused): mild, “blood and thunder” less; ischaemic: RAPD, poor vision, extensive haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots, more than 10 disc areas of non-perfusion; risk of rubeosis (90-day glaucoma).

Management

  • Systemic workup; IOP control.
  • Macular oedema: anti-VEGF (CRUISE, COPERNICUS, GALILEO, SCORE2), dexamethasone implant; grid laser not effective (CVOS).
  • Ischaemia: monthly gonioscopy and watch for NVI; PRP when NVI or NVA appear (CVOS: no prophylactic PRP).

Surgical interventions (largely abandoned)

  • Radial optic neurotomy, chorioretinal anastomosis, sheathotomy.

CVO study

  • Early PRP for NVI, no benefit from grid laser for vision, perfusion status predicts risk.

Revise from: Landmark Trials in Retinal Vein Occlusion: CVOS, BVOS, SCORE, BRAVO, CRUISE and Beyond

RE 20. Central retinal artery occlusion — fundus picture and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)

  • Sudden painless profound vision loss (counting fingers or worse), RAPD.

Fundus picture

  • Early: arteriolar attenuation, segmentation of blood columns (box-carring), retinal whitening (oedema of inner retina), cherry-red spot; cilioretinal sparing (central island); emboli (cholesterol: Hollenhorst, platelet-fibrin, calcific).
  • Late (weeks): whitening resolves, optic atrophy, attenuated vessels, NVI in about 20% (watch for NVG).

Management

  • An ocular stroke: urgent referral to stroke unit (risk of cerebral stroke); thrombolysis within 4.5 hours under trial or protocol in some centres.
  • Conservative measures (unproven): ocular massage, AC paracentesis, IOP lowering, carbogen inhalation, hyperbaric oxygen.
  • Workup: ESR and CRP (exclude GCA in older patients: urgent steroids), carotid Doppler, echocardiography, ECG (atrial fibrillation), BP, lipids, glucose; thrombophilia in young.
  • Follow-up for rubeosis (PRP or anti-VEGF if NVI).

Revise from: Retinal Artery Occlusion: A Stroke of the Eye

RE 21. Ocular changes in hypertension and their management, with special reference to pregnancy-induced hypertension. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Hypertensive retinopathy

  • Keith–Wagener–Barker (grades 1–4) and Wong–Mitchell classification: arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping, copper and silver wiring, haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates (macular star), disc oedema (malignant).
  • Hypertensive choroidopathy: Elschnig spots, Siegrist streaks, serous RD; optic neuropathy.

Management

  • Control BP gradually (avoid rapid drops: optic nerve ischaemia), treat complications (BRVO, macroaneurysm).

Pregnancy-induced hypertension (pre-eclampsia, eclampsia)

  • Focal arteriolar spasm, retinal oedema, serous RD (usually bullous, resolves after delivery), cortical blindness (PRES), HELLP-related haemorrhages.
  • Management: obstetric and BP control; prognosis good; serous RD resolves after delivery.

Revise from: Hypertensive Retinopathy: Grading and Hypertensive Emergencies

RE 22. Haematological disorders and the retina (anaemia, leukaemia, sickle cell disease). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Anaemia

  • Flame and dot haemorrhages, Roth spots, cotton-wool spots, venous tortuosity; worse with thrombocytopenia.

Leukaemia

  • Haemorrhages (Roth spots), cotton-wool spots, leukaemic infiltrates (retina, choroid, optic nerve: emergency radiotherapy), hypopyon, orbital mass (chloroma), opportunistic infection.

Sickle cell disease

  • Non-proliferative: salmon-patch haemorrhages, iridescent spots, black sunbursts, angioid streaks.
  • Proliferative (HbSC most): Goldberg stages I–V (arteriolar occlusion, AV anastomosis, sea-fan neovascularisation, vitreous haemorrhage, RD); laser for sea fans; avoid hyperviscosity and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors in hyphaema.

Others

  • Hyperviscosity (Waldenström, myeloma): “sausage” veins, CRVO-like picture.

Revise from: Sickle Cell Retinopathy: Goldberg Stages and Management

RE 23. Retinal vasculitis and Eales disease; describe granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Retinal vasculitis

  • Inflammation of retinal vessels (sheathing, leakage, occlusion); veins (periphlebitis) or arteries.
  • Causes: infectious (TB, syphilis, herpes, toxoplasma), systemic (Behçet, sarcoid, SLE, GPA, polyarteritis nodosa, MS), ocular (Eales, birdshot, IRVAN), drug-induced (pegcetacoplan, brolucizumab).

Eales disease

  • Idiopathic occlusive periphlebitis in young Indian men; hypersensitivity to tuberculoprotein suspected.
  • Stages: inflammation, ischaemia, neovascularisation; recurrent vitreous haemorrhage.
  • Treatment: steroids in active stage, ATT if TB evidence, scatter laser to ischaemic areas, vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage or TRD.

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener)

  • Necrotising granulomatous vasculitis of airways and kidneys; c-ANCA (PR3).
  • Eye: orbital inflammation (pseudotumour, proptosis), scleritis (necrotising), PUK, nasolacrimal obstruction, retinal vasculitis, optic neuropathy.
  • Treatment: steroids with cyclophosphamide or rituximab.

RE 24. Amaurosis fugax. · outline

Amaurosis fugax

  • Transient monocular vision loss (seconds to minutes), often like a curtain; due to temporary retinal ischaemia.

Causes

  • Emboli from the carotid artery (atheroma) or heart (AF, valvular disease), giant cell arteritis, hypoperfusion (carotid stenosis, ocular ischaemic syndrome), vasospasm (retinal migraine), hyperviscosity, raised ICP (transient visual obscurations: seconds, bilateral), optic disc drusen, angle closure, uveitis-glaucoma-hyphaema.

Evaluation

  • Treat as a TIA: urgent stroke assessment; fundus (emboli), ESR and CRP, carotid Doppler, ECG and Holter, echocardiography, lipids, blood sugar, BP, neuroimaging.

Management

  • Antiplatelets, statins, BP and diabetes control, anticoagulation for AF, carotid endarterectomy for symptomatic stenosis over 70% (NASCET), steroids for GCA.
RE 25. Angioid streaks. DNB / UNIV · outline

Angioid streaks

  • Breaks in a thickened, calcified, brittle Bruch’s membrane: dark red-brown irregular lines radiating from the disc.

Associations (PEPSI)

  • Pseudoxanthoma elasticum (commonest; ABCC6; skin plucked-chicken appearance), Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, Paget disease of bone, sickle cell disease and other haemoglobinopathies, idiopathic.

Other features

  • Peau d’orange, comet-tail lesions, optic disc drusen, salmon spots.

Complications

  • CNV (main threat to vision), choroidal rupture after minor trauma.

Management

  • Protective eyewear (avoid contact sports), Amsler monitoring, anti-VEGF for CNV, systemic evaluation (dermatology, cardiovascular for PXE).
RE 26. Coats disease; macular telangiectasia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Coats disease

  • Idiopathic unilateral retinal telangiectasia with exudation; young boys; NDP gene somatic mutation in some.
  • Features: leukocoria or xanthocoria, telangiectatic vessels with light bulb aneurysms, massive subretinal and intraretinal exudation, exudative RD.
  • Shields staging 1–5 (telangiectasia, exudation, RD, glaucoma, end-stage).
  • Differential: retinoblastoma (calcification on ultrasound absent in Coats), ROP, FEVR.
  • Management: laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy to telangiectasia, anti-VEGF adjunct, drainage of subretinal fluid with vitrectomy for advanced disease.

Macular telangiectasia type 2 (MacTel)

  • Bilateral; middle age; loss of macular pigment, greyish parafoveal sheen, right-angled venules, crystalline deposits, pigment plaques, inner and outer retinal cavitation on OCT, subretinal neovascularisation late.
  • Management: CNTF implant (revakinagene taroretcel: slows photoreceptor loss; approved 2025), anti-VEGF for neovascularisation.

MacTel type 1

  • Unilateral, men; aneurysmal telangiectasia with exudation (Coats spectrum); laser or anti-VEGF.
RE 27. Ocular ischaemic syndrome. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ocular ischaemic syndrome (OIS)

  • Chronic hypoperfusion of the eye from severe carotid artery stenosis (over 90%) or occlusion; older men; diabetes, hypertension, smoking.

Features

  • Gradual vision loss, ocular pain (ischaemic), prolonged recovery after light; anterior segment: rubeosis, NVG (IOP may be low due to ciliary hypoperfusion), anterior uveitis; posterior: mid-peripheral dot-blot haemorrhages, dilated non-tortuous veins, narrowed arteries, cotton-wool spots, spontaneous arterial pulsation, NVD.

Investigations

  • FFA (delayed arm-retina and choroidal filling, prolonged AV transit, vessel wall staining), carotid Doppler, CT or MR angiography.

Management

  • Carotid endarterectomy or stenting, PRP and anti-VEGF for neovascularisation, IOP control, systemic risk management (high mortality from cardiovascular disease).
RE 28. Retinal changes at high altitude. DNB / UNIV · outline

Retinal changes at high altitude (high-altitude retinopathy)

  • Due to hypoxia: vascular dilatation and tortuosity, retinal haemorrhages (flame, dot), cotton-wool spots, disc hyperaemia and oedema, vitreous haemorrhage rarely; usually asymptomatic unless the macula is involved.

Associations

  • Acute mountain sickness, high-altitude cerebral oedema (papilloedema); rapid ascent, altitudes over 4000–5000 m.

Other ocular effects

  • UV keratitis (snow blindness), dry eye, refractive shift after radial keratotomy (hyperopic shift), gas bubble expansion after vitreoretinal surgery (contraindicated), changes in IOP.

Management

  • Gradual ascent, acclimatisation, descent if symptomatic, acetazolamide prophylaxis; haemorrhages usually resolve.

RE 74. Paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM) and acute macular neuroretinopathy (AMN). NEW · outline

Paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM)

  • Ischaemia of the intermediate and deep capillary plexuses: hyper-reflective band-like lesions at the level of the inner nuclear layer on OCT, later INL thinning; paracentral scotomas.
  • Associations: retinal vein and artery occlusions, diabetic and sickle cell retinopathy, Purtscher, migraine, vasopressors, caffeine, COVID-19, hypovolaemia; in older patients may herald CRAO or carotid disease.
  • Evaluate: OCT, OCTA (deep plexus flow deficit), near-infrared reflectance (grey lesions), systemic vascular workup (carotid Doppler, BP, cardiac).

Acute macular neuroretinopathy (AMN)

  • Damage to the outer retina (deep capillary plexus and choriocapillaris): hyper-reflective lesions in the outer plexiform and outer nuclear layers, later ellipsoid zone disruption; wedge or petal-shaped dark lesions on near-infrared imaging pointing to the fovea.
  • Young women; associations: oral contraceptives, viral illness (including COVID-19), adrenaline, caffeine, trauma, hypotension.

Management

  • No specific treatment; address systemic causes; scotomas may persist.
RE 75. Radiation retinopathy. NEW · outline

Radiation retinopathy

  • Occlusive microangiopathy after radiation (plaque brachytherapy, proton beam, external beam for head and neck tumours); usually appears 6 months to 3 years after treatment; dose-dependent (risk with doses over 45 Gy; lower with diabetes, chemotherapy).

Features

  • Microaneurysms, telangiectasia, haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, macular oedema (radiation maculopathy), capillary non-perfusion, neovascularisation, vitreous haemorrhage, NVG; radiation optic neuropathy.

Diagnosis

  • History of radiation; OCT (macular oedema), FFA and OCTA (non-perfusion); differentiate from diabetic retinopathy and tumour recurrence.

Management

  • Anti-VEGF (also prophylactic schedules after plaque therapy reduce maculopathy), intravitreal steroids (dexamethasone implant), sector or panretinal photocoagulation for ischaemia and neovascularisation, vitrectomy for vitreous haemorrhage, IOP control for NVG; radiation optic neuropathy: anti-VEGF or steroids (limited success), hyperbaric oxygen.
RE 76. Retinal arterial macroaneurysm. NEW · outline

Retinal arterial macroaneurysm (RAM)

  • Acquired fusiform or saccular dilatation of a retinal arteriole (often at a bifurcation or AV crossing in the temporal arcades); elderly women; hypertension (main association), atherosclerosis.

Presentations

  • Asymptomatic (incidental), exudative (macular oedema, hard exudate rings near the fovea), haemorrhagic (multilevel haemorrhage: subretinal, intraretinal, preretinal and vitreous: hourglass appearance; can mimic melanoma or AMD).

Investigations

  • FFA (early filling of the aneurysm), ICGA (through haemorrhage), OCT, BP measurement.

Management

  • Observation (many thrombose and involute), treat hypertension.
  • Exudative with macular involvement: anti-VEGF, direct or indirect laser to the macroaneurysm (risk of arteriolar occlusion), micropulse laser.
  • Haemorrhagic: Nd:YAG hyaloidotomy for premacular haemorrhage, pneumatic displacement with tPA for submacular blood, vitrectomy for non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage.

RE 29. Macular hole — definition, classification, pathophysiology, OCT features in relation to management, prognostic factors and recent advances. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Full-thickness defect of the neurosensory retina at the fovea.

Classification

  • Gass stages 1–4 (impending, small hole, full-thickness with operculum, with PVD).
  • IVTS (OCT based): vitreomacular adhesion, traction, small (≤ 250 µm), medium (250–400 µm) and large (> 400 µm) holes, with or without VMT.

Pathophysiology

  • Anteroposterior and tangential vitreofoveal traction with perifoveal PVD; Müller cell cone dehiscence.

OCT features and relation to management

  • Minimum linear diameter guides prognosis and technique: small holes close with standard ILM peel, ocriplasmin or pneumatic vitreolysis; large holes need inverted ILM flap; chronic holes do worse.

Prognostic factors

  • Hole size and duration, MHI, presence of ERM, myopia.

Recent advances

  • Inverted ILM flap, autologous retinal transplant and amniotic membrane for refractory holes, face-down positioning shortened or skipped for small holes.

RE 30. Central serous chorioretinopathy — aetiology, clinical features and current trends in management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiology

  • Choroidal hyperpermeability and thickening (pachychoroid); risk: corticosteroids (any route), type A personality, stress, pregnancy, Cushing, Helicobacter pylori, sildenafil.

Clinical features

  • Young or middle-aged men; blurred vision, metamorphopsia, micropsia; serous detachment at the macula, PED; hyperopic shift.
  • OCT: subretinal fluid, thick choroid with dilated Haller vessels; FFA: ink-blot or smoke-stack leak; ICGA: choroidal hyperpermeability.

Current trends in management

  • Stop steroids; observe for 3 months (most resolve).
  • Chronic or recurrent: half-dose or half-fluence PDT (most effective), micropulse laser, focal laser for extrafoveal leak; eplerenone failed (VICI trial).
  • CNV in chronic disease: anti-VEGF.

RE 31. Cystoid macular oedema — causes, features and treatment; pseudophakic CME. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes

  • Pseudophakic (Irvine–Gass), diabetes, vein occlusion, uveitis, RP, drugs (prostaglandin analogues, niacin, taxanes), epiretinal traction, CNV.

Features

  • Blurred central vision; petaloid pattern on FFA (late), cysts on OCT; flower-petal appearance at the fovea.

Pseudophakic CME

  • Peak 4–12 weeks after surgery; risk: posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, diabetes, uveitis, ERM.

Treatment

RE 32. Toxic retinopathies — hydroxychloroquine and others: screening. DNB / UNIV · outline

Hydroxychloroquine retinopathy

  • Bull’s eye maculopathy; parafoveal (Caucasians) or pericentral (Asians: extramacular) photoreceptor loss; irreversible and may progress after stopping.
  • Risk factors: dose over 5 mg/kg real body weight per day, duration over 5 years, renal disease, tamoxifen use, pre-existing macular disease.
  • Screening (AAO 2016 and RCOphth 2020): baseline examination within the first year; annual screening after 5 years (sooner with risk factors): SD-OCT and automated fields (10-2 for Caucasians, 24-2 or 30-2 for Asians), FAF, multifocal ERG.

  • Chloroquine (higher risk), thioridazine and chlorpromazine (pigmentary retinopathy), tamoxifen (crystalline maculopathy), canthaxanthine (crystals), talc (emboli), ethambutol (optic neuropathy), MEK inhibitors (serous retinopathy), pentosan polysulfate (pattern maculopathy: screen long-term users), vigabatrin (field constriction), interferon (cotton-wool spots), checkpoint inhibitors (uveitis), sildenafil (blue tint).

RE 33. Epiretinal membrane — causes and management; ILM peeling. DNB / UNIV · outline

Epiretinal membrane (ERM)

  • Fibrocellular proliferation (glial cells, RPE, hyalocytes, myofibroblasts) on the inner retinal surface; contracts causing distortion.

Causes

  • Idiopathic (associated with PVD; commonest), secondary: retinal tears, RD surgery, retinal vascular disease (diabetes, RVO), uveitis, trauma, laser or cryotherapy, intraocular tumours.

Features

  • Metamorphopsia, reduced vision, cellophane maculopathy, macular pucker, retinal striae, vascular tortuosity, pseudohole, lamellar hole; OCT staging (Govetto stages 1–4 with ectopic inner foveal layers).

Management

  • Observation if mild; vitrectomy with membrane peeling for symptomatic ERM (vision loss, distortion); outcomes depend on preoperative vision and EIFL stage.

ILM peeling

  • Using dyes (brilliant blue G, ICG: toxicity concerns, trypan blue, triamcinolone) to remove the ILM reduces ERM recurrence; ILM peel essential in macular hole surgery; risks: dissociated optic nerve fibre layer (DONFL), retinal damage, paracentral scotomas.

RE 34. Vitreomacular traction and the International Vitreomacular Traction Study classification. · outline

Vitreomacular traction (VMT)

  • Abnormal persistent attachment of the vitreous to the macula with anatomical distortion, as the surrounding vitreous separates.

IVTS classification (OCT based, 2013)

  • Vitreomacular adhesion (VMA): perifoveal vitreous separation with remaining attachment within 3 mm without retinal changes.
  • VMT: attachment with retinal distortion (cysts, elevation); focal (1500 µm or less) or broad (over 1500 µm); isolated or concurrent (with AMD, DME, RVO).
  • Full-thickness macular hole: small (≤ 250 µm), medium (over 250 to 400 µm), large (over 400 µm); with or without VMT; primary or secondary.
  • Lamellar hole and pseudohole (updated definitions in 2020).

Management

  • Observation (spontaneous release in about 30–40%), pneumatic vitreolysis (intravitreal C3F8 or SF6), ocriplasmin (limited, withdrawn in many markets), vitrectomy for symptomatic persistent VMT.
RE 35. Pachychoroid spectrum disease. · outline

Pachychoroid spectrum

  • Diseases characterised by a thick choroid with dilated outer choroidal vessels (pachyvessels) and attenuated choriocapillaris above them; choroidal congestion and hyperpermeability.

Spectrum

  • Uncomplicated pachychoroid, pachychoroid pigment epitheliopathy (PPE), central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR), pachychoroid neovasculopathy (PNV: type 1 CNV), polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (aneurysmal type 1 neovascularisation), focal choroidal excavation, peripapillary pachychoroid syndrome.

Imaging

  • EDI-OCT or swept-source OCT (subfoveal choroidal thickness often over 300 µm, pachyvessels), ICGA (dilated vessels, hyperpermeability), autofluorescence (gravitational tracts).

Management

  • Depends on disease: observation, half-dose PDT, anti-VEGF for neovascularisation, avoid corticosteroids.

RE 36. Anti-VEGF agents and their role in ocular pathologies; intravitreal injections — technique and complications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Anti-VEGF agents

  • Pegaptanib (historical), bevacizumab 1.25 mg (off-label), ranibizumab 0.5 mg, aflibercept 2 mg and 8 mg, brolucizumab 6 mg (vasculitis risk), faricimab 6 mg (VEGF-A and Ang-2); biosimilars.

RE 39. Retinitis pigmentosa — clinical features, genetics, associated systemic syndromes (Usher, Laurence-Moon-Bardet-Biedl), atypical RP and recent advances. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Clinical features

  • Night blindness, progressive peripheral field loss (ring scotoma), later central loss; triad: bone-spicule pigmentation, arteriolar attenuation, waxy disc pallor; posterior subcapsular cataract, CME, myopia.

  • Autosomal dominant (RHO commonest), recessive, X-linked (RPGR, worst), digenic, mitochondrial; over 80 genes.

  • Sector, unilateral, RP sine pigmento, retinitis punctata albescens, pericentral.

  • Voretigene neparvovec (RPE65), other gene therapies in trials, optogenetics, retinal prostheses (Argus II), stem cells; vitamin A palmitate (controversial); treat CME with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

RE 41. Peripheral retinal degenerations — lattice degeneration; prophylaxis of retinal breaks. HIGH-YIELD · outline

RE 42. Retinoschisis — features and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Retinoschisis

  • Splitting of the neurosensory retina.

Degenerative (senile) retinoschisis

  • Older people; bilateral, inferotemporal periphery; smooth dome-shaped elevation, absolute scotoma, split in the outer plexiform layer; snowflakes on inner wall, sclerosed vessels.
  • Differentiate from RD: no shifting fluid, absolute (not relative) scotoma, laser burns whiten the outer leaf, no pigment or tobacco dust, OCT shows split.
  • Usually observation; treat only if progressive RD develops (outer wall breaks: vitrectomy or buckle).

X-linked juvenile retinoschisis

  • RS1 gene; boys; foveal schisis (spoke-wheel pattern) in almost all, peripheral schisis in about half; negative ERG (reduced b-wave); complications: vitreous haemorrhage, RD.
  • Management: carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (topical dorzolamide or oral acetazolamide) for foveal schisis, refractive correction, amblyopia therapy, vitrectomy for complications; gene therapy trials.

Others

  • Myopic foveoschisis, optic pit maculopathy, tractional schisis (diabetes).
RE 43. Night blindness — causes; congenital stationary night blindness and fundus albipunctatus. DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes of night blindness

  • Vitamin A deficiency (xerophthalmia), retinitis pigmentosa and other rod-cone dystrophies, congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB), fundus albipunctatus, Oguchi disease, gyrate atrophy, choroideremia, high myopia, advanced glaucoma, cataract (some), drugs (quinine, phenothiazines), PRP-treated eyes.

Congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB)

  • Non-progressive night blindness from birth; normal fundus (most) or abnormal fundus (fundus albipunctatus, Oguchi).
  • Complete (CSNB1: NYX, GRM6 — ON bipolar defect) vs incomplete (CSNB2: CACNA1F); myopia, nystagmus, strabismus.
  • ERG: Schubert–Bornschein type (negative waveform: b-wave smaller than a-wave), Riggs type (reduced rod function).

Fundus albipunctatus

  • RDH5 gene (11-cis retinol dehydrogenase); multiple small white-yellow dots in the mid-periphery sparing the macula; slow dark adaptation (rod recovery takes hours); ERG normalises after prolonged dark adaptation; some cases have cone dystrophy.

Oguchi disease

  • Golden-yellow sheen in light adaptation that disappears after dark adaptation (Mizuo–Nakamura phenomenon); SAG or GRK1 gene.

RE 44. Discuss the changing concepts in the management of pathological high myopia. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathological myopia

  • Myopia with axial elongation and degenerative changes (META-PM: category 2 or more or plus lesions).

Lesions

  • Tessellated fundus, diffuse and patchy atrophy, lacquer cracks, Fuchs spot, myopic CNV, posterior staphyloma, myopic traction maculopathy (foveoschisis, macular hole), dome-shaped macula, peripapillary changes, glaucoma, RD, cataract.

Changing concepts in management

  • Myopia control in childhood (atropine, defocus spectacles and contact lenses, outdoor time) to prevent high myopia.
  • Myopic CNV: anti-VEGF (RADIANCE, MYRROR) on a PRN basis; PDT historical.
  • Traction maculopathy: vitrectomy with fovea-sparing ILM peel, macular buckles; macular hole RD: inverted flap.
  • Refractive options: phakic IOL; avoid unnecessary clear lens extraction (RD risk).
  • Posterior scleral reinforcement (limited evidence); genetics and AI risk assessment.

RE 45. Stargardt disease and Best disease — genetics and emerging therapies. · outline

Stargardt disease

  • Commonest inherited macular dystrophy; autosomal recessive ABCA4 gene (rim protein of photoreceptor discs); accumulation of A2E and lipofuscin in RPE.
  • Features: childhood or young adult onset, bilateral central vision loss, beaten-bronze macula, yellow pisciform flecks, bull’s eye maculopathy; dark choroid on FFA; FAF (hypo- and hyperautofluorescent flecks); peripapillary sparing.
  • ERG: normal or reduced (Lois groups); classification by FAF.

Best vitelliform macular dystrophy

  • Autosomal dominant BEST1 gene (bestrophin-1, chloride channel of RPE).
  • Stages: previtelliform, vitelliform (egg-yolk lesion), pseudohypopyon, vitelliruptive (scrambled egg), atrophic or fibrotic, CNV.
  • EOG: Arden ratio under 1.5 with normal ERG (diagnostic).

Emerging therapies

  • Stargardt: avoid vitamin A supplements, UV and blue light protection; deuterated vitamin A (ALK-001), visual cycle modulators (emixustat, tinlarebant), complement inhibitors, gene therapy (large ABCA4 gene: dual AAV, lentivirus), stem cell RPE.
  • Best: anti-VEGF for CNV; gene therapy research (canine models).

RE 77. Leber congenital amaurosis and gene therapy. NEW · outline

Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA)

  • Group of severe inherited retinal dystrophies presenting at birth or in early infancy; autosomal recessive; over 25 genes (CEP290 commonest in many populations, GUCY2D, CRB1, RPE65, AIPL1).

Features

  • Severe visual impairment from birth, nystagmus, poor pupil reactions, oculodigital sign (eye poking), high hyperopia, keratoconus, cataract, fundus normal early then pigmentary changes; extinguished or severely reduced ERG.
  • Associations: intellectual disability, renal disease (Senior–Løken: nephronophthisis), Joubert syndrome (CEP290).

Diagnosis

  • ERG, OCT, genetic testing (panels or exome sequencing).

Gene therapy

  • Voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna): subretinal AAV2-hRPE65v2 for biallelic RPE65 mutations with viable retinal cells; improved light sensitivity and navigation in dim light (MLMT) in the phase 3 trial; long-term durability being studied; perifoveal atrophy reported.
  • Others: CEP290 RNA therapy (sepofarsen: trial did not meet endpoint), CRISPR editing (EDIT-101), GUCY2D and AIPL1 gene therapies in trials.

Management

  • Low-vision support, refractive correction, early intervention and education, genetic counselling, screening of systemic associations.

Retinopathy of Prematurity & Paediatric Retina

RE 46. Retinopathy of prematurity — risk factors, classification, screening protocol in India, guidelines for intervention, treatment modalities and prevention; plus disease. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Risk factors

  • Low birth weight and gestational age, oxygen therapy, sepsis, transfusions, poor weight gain; in India larger and older babies also get ROP.

Classification (ICROP3, 2021)

  • Zones I–III (with posterior zone II), stages 1–5 (5A, 5B, 5C), plus and pre-plus disease, aggressive ROP (A-ROP replacing AP-ROP), regression and reactivation terms.

Screening protocol in India

  • Birth weight under 2000 g or gestational age under 34 weeks (and larger babies with risk factors); first screen by day 20–30 of life (earlier, about 2–3 weeks, for very small babies); ROP screening by trained ophthalmologists or telemedicine (KIDROP).

Treatment criteria

  • Type 1 ROP (ETROP): zone I any stage with plus, zone I stage 3, zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus: treat within 48–72 hours.
  • Type 2: watch closely.

Treatment

  • Laser photocoagulation (gold standard), anti-VEGF (bevacizumab or ranibizumab 0.2 mg: BEAT-ROP, RAINBOW) especially zone I and A-ROP, with long follow-up for reactivation; vitrectomy or lens-sparing surgery for stage 4–5.

Prevention

  • Controlled oxygen saturation (target about 90–95%), prevention of preterm birth, good neonatal care.

Plus disease

  • Venous dilatation and arterial tortuosity in at least two quadrants of the posterior pole; marks activity needing treatment.

RE 47. Persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (persistent fetal vasculature). · outline

Persistent fetal vasculature (PFV, formerly PHPV)

  • Failure of regression of the primary vitreous and hyaloid vascular system; usually unilateral; term infants.

Types

  • Anterior: retrolental fibrovascular membrane, elongated ciliary processes (pathognomonic), cataract, shallow AC, microphthalmos, glaucoma.
  • Posterior: vitreous stalk from the disc, falciform retinal fold, tractional RD, optic disc anomalies.
  • Combined (commonest).

Differential diagnosis

  • Retinoblastoma (no calcification in PFV; microphthalmos in PFV), Coats disease, ROP, toxocariasis, congenital cataract, FEVR.

Investigations

  • B-scan (stalk from disc to lens), CT or MRI, UBM.

Management

  • Mild: observation and amblyopia treatment; lens and membrane removal with vitrectomy (early, to prevent angle closure and phthisis), aphakic correction and patching; prognosis guarded.
RE 48. ICROP3 classification, anti-VEGF in ROP (RAINBOW trial) and tele-ROP screening. · outline

ICROP3 (2021) key changes

  • Posterior zone II (2 disc diameters into zone II from the zone I boundary), notch (incursion of ROP into a more posterior zone by 1–2 clock hours), sub-categorisation of stage 5 (5A: optic disc visible; 5B: retrolental tissue; 5C: with anterior segment changes), A-ROP replaces AP-ROP (can occur in larger babies), plus disease as a continuum, regression and reactivation terminology (after anti-VEGF), long-term sequelae.

Anti-VEGF in ROP

  • BEAT-ROP: bevacizumab better than laser for zone I stage 3+ (fewer recurrences), with later reactivation.
  • RAINBOW: ranibizumab 0.2 mg vs 0.1 mg vs laser: 0.2 mg had higher treatment success than laser (not statistically significant), less unfavourable structural outcomes and less myopia; approved in Europe and other countries.
  • FIREFLEYE and BUTTERFLEYE: aflibercept non-inferior to laser.
  • Concerns: late reactivation (need follow-up to 65 weeks PMA), persistent avascular retina, systemic VEGF suppression and neurodevelopment.

Tele-ROP screening

  • Wide-field imaging (RetCam, 3nethra Neo) by trained technicians with remote grading; KIDROP model (Karnataka) in rural India; AI-assisted plus disease detection (i-ROP DL); validated in e-ROP study.

RE 78. Familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, Norrie disease and Stickler syndrome. NEW · outline

Familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR)

  • Defective peripheral retinal vascularisation in full-term infants; genes in Wnt signalling (FZD4, LRP5, TSPAN12: autosomal dominant; NDP: X-linked).
  • Features: peripheral avascular retina, vessel straightening, macular dragging and falciform folds, exudation, neovascularisation, tractional and exudative RD; asymmetric; resembles ROP without prematurity.
  • Investigations: widefield FFA (examine family members: often asymptomatic), genetic testing; LRP5 associated with low bone density.
  • Management: laser to avascular retina with leakage or neovascularisation, anti-VEGF (selected), vitrectomy or scleral buckle for RD.

Norrie disease

  • X-linked (NDP gene, norrin); boys; bilateral congenital retinal dysplasia and detachment (pseudoglioma, leukocoria), later cataract, phthisis; sensorineural deafness and intellectual disability.
  • Management: genetic counselling, early laser in some at-risk babies, hearing support.

Stickler syndrome

  • Collagen disorder (COL2A1: type 1 with membranous vitreous anomaly; COL11A1: type 2); high myopia, optically empty vitreous, perivascular lattice, giant retinal tears and RD (highest risk inherited RD), cataract, glaucoma; cleft palate (Pierre Robin sequence), midface hypoplasia, deafness, arthropathy.
  • Management: prophylactic 360° cryotherapy (Cambridge protocol) or laser to reduce RD risk, regular review, genetic counselling.

Retinal Detachment & Vitreoretinal Surgery

RE 49. Classification of retinal detachment; discuss the changing concepts in the management of different types of RD. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Rhegmatogenous (break), tractional (diabetes, ROP, PVR, trauma), exudative or serous (VKH, tumours, CSCR, posterior scleritis, Coats), combined tractional-rhegmatogenous.

Changing concepts in management

  • Rhegmatogenous: from scleral buckling to primary vitrectomy in pseudophakic and complex detachments; pneumatic retinopexy for selected superior single breaks (PIVOT: better vision and less metamorphopsia than vitrectomy); small-gauge vitrectomy, wide-angle viewing, PFCL, faster recovery.
  • Tractional: anti-VEGF before vitrectomy, bimanual delamination.
  • Exudative: treat the cause (steroids, tumour therapy), not surgery.
  • Macula-on RD: urgent surgery; outcomes depend on macular status and duration.

RE 50. Rhegmatogenous RD — predisposing factors, diagnosis and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Predisposing factors

  • Lattice degeneration, high myopia, pseudophakia and aphakia (especially with PCR or YAG capsulotomy), trauma, PVD with tears, previous RD in the fellow eye, family history, Stickler and Marfan syndromes, retinal necrosis (ARN, CMV), atopic dermatitis.

Diagnosis

  • Symptoms: flashes, floaters, curtain or shadow, reduced vision if macula off.
  • Signs: tobacco dust (Shafer sign), convex mobile detachment with corrugations, retinal breaks (Lincoff rules to find the primary break), low IOP, RAPD (large RD); B-scan in opaque media; OCT for macular status.

Management

  • Urgent surgery (macula-on: within 24–48 hours).
  • Pneumatic retinopexy (single break in superior two-thirds, PIVOT), scleral buckle (young phakic, attached vitreous, dialysis, inferior breaks), pars plana vitrectomy (pseudophakic, posterior breaks, multiple breaks, vitreous haemorrhage, PVR), combined buckle-vitrectomy; tamponade with gas or silicone oil; laser or cryo retinopexy.
  • Fellow eye: examine and treat risk lesions.
RE 51. Exudative retinal detachment — causes and treatment; choroidal detachment and effusion. DNB / UNIV · outline

Exudative (serous) RD: causes

  • Inflammatory: VKH, posterior scleritis, sympathetic ophthalmia, uveal effusion syndrome, orbital cellulitis.
  • Tumours: choroidal melanoma, metastases, haemangioma, retinoblastoma (exophytic).
  • Vascular: Coats disease, malignant hypertension, pre-eclampsia, CSCR (bullous), DIC.
  • Others: nanophthalmos, renal failure, optic disc pit, PDT complications.

Features

  • Smooth, convex, shifting fluid (gravitates inferiorly), no breaks, normal or low IOP.

Treatment

  • Treat the cause (steroids and immunosuppression for VKH and scleritis, tumour treatment, BP control, PDT for haemangioma); surgery seldom needed.

Choroidal detachment and effusion

  • Accumulation of fluid (serous) or blood (haemorrhagic) in the suprachoroidal space; smooth brown elevations that do not extend to the disc (vortex vein anchorage); kissing choroidals.
  • Causes: hypotony (post-operative wound leak, overfiltration), inflammation, trauma, uveal effusion syndrome (thick sclera), drugs (topiramate: bilateral effusion with angle closure), nanophthalmos.
  • Management: treat the cause, cycloplegics and steroids, drainage (sclerotomy) if kissing choroidals, flat AC or persistent; scleral windows for uveal effusion syndrome.

RE 52. Tractional retinal detachment — causes, investigations and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes of tractional RD

  • Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (commonest), ROP, PVR, penetrating trauma, sickle cell retinopathy, FEVR, PFV, Eales disease, retinal vein occlusion, toxocariasis.

Features

  • Concave, immobile detachment with taut retina, no shifting fluid, fibrovascular membranes; may become combined tractional-rhegmatogenous (convex, mobile) when a break develops.

Investigations

  • B-scan (tented immobile retina), OCT (macular traction), FFA (activity of neovascularisation), systemic control.

Management

  • Observe extramacular TRD not progressing; vitrectomy for macula-involving or threatening TRD, combined RD, non-clearing haemorrhage.
  • Pre-op anti-VEGF (3–7 days before) reduces bleeding; PRP; surgical techniques: segmentation, delamination, en bloc dissection, bimanual surgery, endolaser, tamponade with gas or silicone oil.

RE 53. Prognostic factors in rhegmatogenous RD; retinopexy methods (laser, cryo) and SRF drainage. DNB / UNIV · outline

Prognostic factors in rhegmatogenous RD

  • Anatomical success: PVR (most important cause of failure), number and size of breaks, giant tears, aphakia or pseudophakia, choroidal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, previous surgery.
  • Visual outcome: macular status (macula-on best), duration of macula-off (better if under 1 week), height of macular detachment, age, pre-operative vision, outer retinal integrity on OCT after surgery, ERM and CME development.

Retinopexy methods

  • Laser photocoagulation: needs attached retina (or after drainage or gas tamponade), less inflammation, adhesion in 10–14 days, precise; used with indirect laser or endolaser.
  • Cryotherapy: through the sclera, works on detached retina and through media opacity; more inflammation (PVR risk), RPE dispersion; adhesion strength similar after maturation.

Subretinal fluid drainage

  • External (needle or cut-down through sclera) in buckling: for bullous RD, chronic RD, inferior detachments; complications: haemorrhage, retinal incarceration, perforation, hypotony.
  • Internal (through a retinotomy or the break during vitrectomy) with PFCL or air-fluid exchange.
  • Non-drainage techniques (Lincoff) rely on RPE pump.

RE 54. Hypotony — causes and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Hypotony

  • IOP below 6 mmHg (statistical) or low enough to cause structural or functional changes (hypotony maculopathy: chorioretinal folds, disc oedema, vascular tortuosity).

Causes

  • Reduced production: ciliary body shutdown (uveitis, ciliary detachment), cyclodestruction, ischaemia, drugs.
  • Increased outflow: wound leak, overfiltration after trabeculectomy, cyclodialysis cleft, RD (uveoscleral outflow), choroidal detachment, globe rupture, systemic (dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis).

Evaluation

  • Seidel test, gonioscopy (cleft), UBM (ciliary body detachment), B-scan (RD, choroidal effusion), OCT (folds).

Management

  • Treat cause: bandage contact lens or resuturing of wound leaks, bleb revision (autologous blood, compression sutures, revision surgery), cyclodialysis cleft closure (atropine, laser, cryo, direct suturing), control uveitis with steroids, RD surgery, silicone oil for chronic hypotony; viscoelastic in AC temporarily.
RE 55. Retinal detachment surgery — principles of conventional reattachment surgery; scleral buckling vs vitrectomy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principles of conventional reattachment surgery

  • Find all breaks, close them (buckle supports breaks and relieves traction), create a chorioretinal adhesion (cryo or laser), drain subretinal fluid if needed, maintain IOP.

Scleral buckling

  • Segmental (radial or circumferential) or encircling band (No. 240); explants or implants.
  • Best: young phakic patients, attached vitreous, dialysis, anterior breaks, lattice-related RDs.
  • Complications: refractive change (myopia), diplopia, extrusion and infection, choroidal detachment, anterior segment ischaemia.

Vitrectomy

  • Best: pseudophakic, posterior or multiple breaks, PVR, media opacities, giant tears.
  • Complications: cataract, raised IOP, iatrogenic breaks, need for positioning.

Comparison (SPR study)

  • Phakic: buckling had better outcomes; pseudophakic: vitrectomy preferred.

RE 56. Pneumatic retinopexy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pneumatic retinopexy

  • Office procedure: intravitreal expansile gas (SF6 0.5–0.6 mL or C3F8 0.3 mL) after AC paracentesis, with head positioning to place the bubble against the break, combined with cryotherapy (before) or laser (after flattening).

Indications

  • Single break or group of breaks within 1 clock hour in the superior two-thirds (8–4 o’clock), clear media, no PVR grade C or more, patient able to position.

Advantages

  • Office-based, cheaper, no cataract induction, faster recovery, better visual outcomes than vitrectomy in suitable cases (PIVOT: better vision at 1 year and less metamorphopsia).

Disadvantages

  • Lower single-procedure success (about 75–80%) vs vitrectomy, new breaks, gas complications (raised IOP, subretinal gas), strict positioning, no flying.
RE 57. Retinal detachment with proliferative vitreoretinopathy — classification and modern management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR)

  • Membrane formation by RPE, glial cells and fibroblasts on retinal surfaces after RD or surgery; commonest cause of surgical failure.

Classification

  • Retina Society (1983) A–D and updated (1991) grade C with posterior (CP) and anterior (CA) and clock hours, types of contraction.

Risk factors

  • Large or multiple breaks, giant tear, vitreous haemorrhage, previous surgery, cryotherapy, choroidal detachment, long duration.

Modern management

  • Vitrectomy with membrane peeling, base shaving, relaxing retinotomy or retinectomy, PFCL, encircling band, silicone oil (Silicone Study: oil better than SF6, similar to C3F8) or heavy oil.
  • Adjuncts studied: 5-FU and heparin, intravitreal methotrexate (promising), steroids.

RE 58. Giant retinal tear; retinal dialysis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Giant retinal tear

  • Tear of 90° or more (3 clock hours or more) circumferentially, with vitreous attached to its anterior edge; risk: high myopia, Stickler, Marfan, trauma, post-surgery.
  • Management: vitrectomy with PFCL to unroll the flap, endolaser, silicone oil or long-acting gas; lens removal sometimes; prophylactic 360° laser to the fellow eye in Stickler.

Retinal dialysis

  • Disinsertion at the ora serrata (inferotemporal in young people; superonasal after trauma); vitreous attached to the posterior edge, so it does not roll; slowly progressive RD with demarcation lines.
  • Management: scleral buckle with cryotherapy (excellent results).
RE 59. Secondary rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. · outline

Secondary rhegmatogenous RD

  • RD from breaks caused by a disease process or intervention rather than primary PVD-related tears.

Causes

  • Trauma (dialysis, giant tears), retinal necrosis (ARN, CMV retinitis), after vitreoretinal surgery (iatrogenic breaks, sclerotomy-related), after cataract surgery (especially with PCR), proliferative diseases (diabetes: combined tractional-rhegmatogenous), PVR, myopic macular hole (macular hole RD), ROP, Stickler syndrome, coloboma (breaks in the coloboma), toxoplasmosis, PFV.

Management

  • Vitrectomy with membrane removal, silicone oil tamponade (necrotising retinitis, macular hole RD in high myopia), prophylactic laser demarcation in selected cases, treat underlying disease.

RE 60. Vitreous haemorrhage — causes, evaluation and management (including recurrent). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes

  • PDR (commonest), PVD with retinal tear, RVO, trauma, Terson syndrome, AMD and PCV, retinal macroaneurysm, ROP, sickle cell, Eales disease, vasculitis, bleeding disorders.

Evaluation

  • History, fellow eye examination, B-scan (RD, tear, tumour, PVD), gonioscopy (NVA), FFA when clears; systemic BP, sugar, coagulation.

Management

  • Head elevation, avoid anticoagulants if possible; treat the cause (laser to tear, PRP, anti-VEGF).
  • Vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage (1–3 months; earlier in diabetics with severe disease or in RD), with endolaser.

Recurrent haemorrhage after diabetic vitrectomy

  • Causes: fibrovascular ingrowth at sclerotomies, residual NV, new NV; prevent with pre-op anti-VEGF, complete PRP; manage with anti-VEGF, vitreous lavage, PRP fill-in.

RE 61. Pars plana vitrectomy and its indications; current trends in vitreoretinal and macular surgery. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Indications for pars plana vitrectomy

  • Retinal detachment (rhegmatogenous, tractional, PVR, giant tears), non-clearing vitreous haemorrhage, macular diseases (holes, ERM, VMT, submacular haemorrhage), endophthalmitis, dropped nucleus and IOL, IOFB, trauma, diagnostic biopsy, myopic traction maculopathy, retinopathy of prematurity stage 4–5.

Current trends

  • Small-gauge (25, 27-gauge) sutureless surgery, high cut rates (up to 10,000 cpm or more), wide-angle viewing, chandelier illumination, intraoperative OCT, 3D heads-up surgery, adjuncts (dyes, PFCL, heavy silicone oil), inverted ILM flaps, subretinal gene therapy delivery, robotics research.

RE 62. Small-gauge (25-gauge) sutureless vitrectomy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Small-gauge vitrectomy

  • 23, 25 and 27-gauge trocar-cannula systems; angled (beveled) self-sealing incisions; no conjunctival peritomy.

Advantages

  • Less trauma and inflammation, faster recovery, reduced astigmatism, shorter surgery, sutureless.

Disadvantages

  • Flexible instruments, lower flow (improved with high cut rates), risk of wound leak, hypotony and endophthalmitis, cost.

Indications

  • Most macular surgery, uncomplicated RD, vitreous haemorrhage, diabetic TRD; 27-gauge for delicate membranes.
RE 63. Internal tamponades — intravitreal gases (C3F8, SF6) and silicone oil. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Gases

  • SF6 (lasts about 2 weeks, non-expansile 20%), C2F6 (4–5 weeks, 16%), C3F8 (6–8 weeks, 12–14%); pure gases expand (SF6 about 2×, C3F8 about 4×).
  • Uses: RD, macular holes, pneumatic retinopexy, submacular haemorrhage displacement.
  • Precautions: no flying, high altitude or nitrous oxide; positioning; IOP rise; cataract.

Silicone oil

  • Polydimethylsiloxane, 1000 or 5000 centistokes; lighter than water (floats); heavy oils (Densiron) for inferior tamponade.
  • Indications: PVR, giant tears, complex RD, AIDS-related RD, patients who cannot position or must fly.
  • Complications: cataract, glaucoma, emulsification, keratopathy, unexplained vision loss after removal; removal at 3–6 months.

RE 64. Perfluorocarbon liquids. · outline

Perfluorocarbon liquids (PFCL)

  • Perfluoro-n-octane (commonest), perfluorodecalin, perfluorotributylamine; clear, high specific gravity (1.76–2.03), low viscosity, immiscible with water, optically clear, different refractive index (visible interface).

Uses (intraoperative)

  • Flatten detached retina from the posterior pole outwards (pushes SRF anteriorly through breaks), unroll giant retinal tears, stabilise retina during PVR membrane dissection, float dropped nuclei and IOLs, tamponade haemorrhage, help drain suprachoroidal haemorrhage, direct PFCL-silicone oil exchange.

Complications

  • Retained PFCL (subretinal: scotoma; AC: corneal toxicity), inflammation, toxicity with long-term use (short-term postoperative tamponade for 1–2 weeks in some cases), subretinal migration through large breaks.
RE 65. Vitreous substitutes and viscoelastics. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ideal vitreous substitute

  • Clear, inert, non-toxic, appropriate refractive index and specific gravity, stays long enough, easy to inject and remove.

Types

  • Gases (air, SF6, C2F6, C3F8); silicone oils (standard and heavy); perfluorocarbon liquids (PFCL: heavier than water, intraoperative only); balanced salt solution; hydrogels and foldable capsular vitreous body (research).

Viscoelastics in vitreoretinal surgery

  • Viscodissection of membranes, sodium hyaluronate as a temporary substitute, protecting the endothelium; hyaluronic acid as a tamponade has failed.
RE 66. Terson syndrome. · outline

Terson syndrome

  • Intraocular haemorrhage (vitreous, subhyaloid, sub-ILM, intraretinal) associated with subarachnoid haemorrhage (or other intracranial haemorrhage and raised ICP).

Pathogenesis

  • Sudden rise in intracranial pressure transmitted to the optic nerve sheath obstructs venous drainage (retinochoroidal anastomoses), rupturing peripapillary and retinal capillaries.

Significance

  • Occurs in about 10–40% of SAH; marker of severe SAH and higher mortality; bilateral often.

Management

  • Observation (many clear), vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage (especially in children: amblyopia), Nd:YAG membranotomy for premacular haemorrhage; check for ERM and macular holes later.

Endophthalmitis & Infections

RE 67. Endophthalmitis — aetiology, investigations, rational management, including a critical note on therapy-induced problems. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiology

  • Exogenous: post-operative (acute, delayed), post-traumatic, post-injection, bleb-related; endogenous (Candida, Klebsiella in diabetics, liver abscess, IV drug use).

Investigations

  • Vitreous tap or biopsy (and AC tap): Gram stain, culture, PCR; B-scan; blood cultures in endogenous cases.

Rational management

  • Intravitreal antibiotics immediately: vancomycin 1 mg/0.1 mL and ceftazidime 2.25 mg/0.1 mL (or amikacin 0.4 mg); antifungal (amphotericin 5 µg or voriconazole 100 µg) if fungal.
  • Vitrectomy: EVS indication (light perception only) for post-cataract; earlier vitrectomy increasingly used; systemic antibiotics for trauma and endogenous cases.
  • Steroids: topical, and intravitreal dexamethasone in selected cases.

Therapy-induced problems

  • Aminoglycoside macular infarction, vancomycin-associated haemorrhagic occlusive retinal vasculitis (HORV), retinal toxicity from repeated injections, RD after vitrectomy, resistance.

RE 68. Postoperative (pseudophakic) endophthalmitis — diagnosis, treatment and prevention. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Diagnosis

  • Usually 2–7 days after surgery: pain, reduced vision, lid oedema, hypopyon, vitritis, loss of red reflex; B-scan for vitreous involvement.

Treatment (EVS)

  • Vitreous tap and injection of vancomycin and ceftazidime for vision of hand movements or better; immediate vitrectomy if light perception only (diabetics may benefit from vitrectomy at hand movements).
  • Systemic antibiotics did not add benefit in EVS; topical fortified antibiotics and steroids.

Prevention

  • Povidone-iodine 5% to conjunctival sac (the only proven measure), lid hygiene, treat blepharitis, sterile technique, well-constructed wounds, intracameral cefuroxime (ESCRS study: about fivefold reduction) or moxifloxacin, avoid vitreous loss.

RE 69. Ocular cysticercosis — diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular cysticercosis

  • Larval stage of Taenia solium; common in India; subconjunctival, orbital (muscle cysts), intraocular (subretinal or vitreous).

Diagnosis

  • Cyst with a scolex (white dot), undulating movements on examination; B-scan or USG (cyst with scolex), CT or MRI (orbital and brain), ELISA.

Management

  • Intraocular: surgical removal (vitrectomy or trans-scleral); avoid antihelminthics alone (dying cyst causes severe inflammation).
  • Orbital and muscle cysts: oral albendazole with steroids (after ruling out intraocular and checking brain imaging).
  • Subconjunctival: excision.
RE 70. Endogenous endophthalmitis; lessons from EVS and intracameral antibiotic prophylaxis (ESCRS). · outline

Endogenous endophthalmitis

  • Haematogenous spread of infection from a distant source; 2–8% of endophthalmitis.

Risk factors

  • Diabetes, immunosuppression, IV drug use, indwelling catheters, recent surgery, liver abscess (Klebsiella in East Asia), endocarditis, total parenteral nutrition, COVID-19 (fungal).

Organisms

  • Fungal (Candida commonest; Aspergillus), bacterial (Klebsiella, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus).

Diagnosis

  • Blood and urine cultures, echocardiography, abdominal imaging; vitreous tap or vitrectomy (culture, PCR).

Management

  • Systemic antimicrobials (IV), intravitreal antibiotics or antifungals (voriconazole 100 µg, amphotericin 5 µg), early vitrectomy for severe vitritis, treat source with physicians; poor prognosis.

Lessons from EVS

  • Vitrectomy only for light perception vision; tap and inject for hand movements or better; systemic antibiotics not helpful (in post-cataract endophthalmitis).

Intracameral antibiotic prophylaxis (ESCRS study)

  • Intracameral cefuroxime 1 mg/0.1 mL at the end of cataract surgery reduced endophthalmitis about fivefold; moxifloxacin intracameral (Aravind data) also effective; dosing errors cause toxicity (HORV with vancomycin; macular oedema with high-dose cefuroxime).

Suprachoroidal Haemorrhage

RE 71. Suprachoroidal and expulsive haemorrhage — pathophysiology, recognition and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathophysiology

  • Rupture of a posterior ciliary artery during sudden hypotony; blood accumulates in the suprachoroidal space; expulsive if intraocular contents extrude through the wound.
  • Risk factors: older age, hypertension, glaucoma, high myopia, aphakia, anticoagulants, tachycardia, Valsalva, long surgery.

Recognition

  • Intra-operative: sudden pain, shallowing of AC, hard eye, loss of red reflex, dark mound behind the pupil, iris and lens prolapse.
  • Post-operative: severe pain, shallow AC, dark elevated choroid (kissing), B-scan.

Management

  • Immediate wound closure (finger pressure, sutures), raise head, IV mannitol, posterior sclerotomies only if needed.
  • Later: secondary drainage at 7–14 days (when clot liquefies on B-scan) with vitrectomy, especially kissing choroidals, RD, or vitreous incarceration.
