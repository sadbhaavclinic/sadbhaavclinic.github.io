Neuro-Ophthalmology OSCE Stations: 10 Spot Stations on Fields, Pupils, Hess Charts, Papilloedema and GCA

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 10 spot stations, 40 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 10 original stations cover the neuro-ophthalmology topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. Bitemporal hemianopia, 2. Relative afferent pupillary defect, 3. Horner syndrome, 4. Hess chart, 5. Fourth nerve palsy, 6. Papilloedema, 7. Ocular myasthenia, 8. Colour vision testing, 9. Optic atrophy, 10. Giant cell arteritis

Station 1: Bitemporal hemianopia

The examiner shows you: A pair of visual field printouts: loss of the temporal half of each field, denser superiorly, respecting the vertical midline.

  1. Where is the lesion?
    The optic chiasm.
  2. Why is it denser superiorly?
    Inferior crossing fibres (serving the superior temporal fields) are compressed first by a mass from below, such as a pituitary adenoma.
  3. Name causes.
    Pituitary adenoma (commonest), craniopharyngioma (often inferior first), meningioma, aneurysm, and inflammatory or infiltrative lesions.
  4. What investigations do you request?
    MRI of the brain and pituitary with contrast, pituitary hormone profile, and baseline OCT of the RNFL and ganglion cells.

Station 2: Relative afferent pupillary defect

The examiner shows you: A swinging torch test: when the light moves from the right to the left eye, both pupils dilate.

  1. What does this show?
    A left relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn pupil).
  2. Name causes.
    Asymmetric optic nerve disease (optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, compression, glaucoma), and extensive retinal disease (large detachment, CRVO, CRAO).
  3. Does cataract cause an RAPD?
    No: dense media opacity does not cause an RAPD (if anything, a dense cataract may cause a slight RAPD in the other eye).
  4. How do you quantify it?
    With neutral density filters in front of the normal eye until the response is balanced, recorded in log units.

Station 3: Horner syndrome

The examiner shows you: A patient with mild right ptosis, a smaller right pupil that dilates slowly in the dark (dilation lag) and a raised right lower lid.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Right Horner syndrome (oculosympathetic paresis).
  2. Which drops confirm it?
    Apraclonidine 0.5–1%: reversal of anisocoria (the Horner pupil dilates). Cocaine 4–10%: the normal pupil dilates, the Horner pupil does not.
  3. How do you localise the lesion?
    Hydroxyamphetamine 1%: a third-order (postganglionic) lesion fails to dilate; first- and second-order lesions dilate. Look for associated signs (brainstem, chest, neck).
  4. Which cause must not be missed in acute painful Horner syndrome?
    Carotid artery dissection: arrange urgent CT or MR angiography of the head and neck.

Station 4: Hess chart

The examiner shows you: A Hess chart: the right field is smaller, with underaction on looking to the right and overaction of the left medial rectus in the left field.

  1. Which eye and muscle are affected?
    Right lateral rectus underaction: a right sixth nerve palsy. The smaller field belongs to the affected eye.
  2. Explain the overaction in the other eye.
    Hering's law: the yoke muscle (left medial rectus) receives equal increased innervation and overacts.
  3. Name causes of a sixth nerve palsy.
    Microvascular (diabetes, hypertension), raised intracranial pressure (false localising sign), trauma, tumours, and in children post-viral or tumour.
  4. When do you image urgently?
    Any palsy with other neurological signs, papilloedema, a young patient, or one that does not improve within about 3 months.

Station 5: Fourth nerve palsy

The examiner shows you: A patient with vertical diplopia, worse on looking down and left, and a head tilt to the left. The right eye is higher.

  1. Use the three-step test.
    Right hypertropia; worse on left gaze; worse on right head tilt: right superior oblique (fourth nerve) palsy.
  2. Why the head tilt to the left?
    Tilting away from the affected side reduces the need for the weak superior oblique to intort the eye.
  3. How do you tell congenital from acquired?
    Old photographs showing a head tilt, a large vertical fusional range (over 4–6 prism dioptres) and facial asymmetry suggest a long-standing (congenital, decompensated) palsy.
  4. Name the commonest cause of acquired fourth nerve palsy.
    Head trauma; also microvascular disease.

Station 6: Papilloedema

The examiner shows you: Fundus photographs of both eyes: swollen, elevated discs with blurred margins, obscured vessels at the disc margin and peripapillary haemorrhages; spontaneous venous pulsation absent.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Bilateral optic disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure (papilloedema) until proven otherwise.
  2. How is it graded?
    The modified Frisén scale (grade 0–5).
  3. What is your plan?
    Check blood pressure (exclude malignant hypertension), urgent neuroimaging (MRI with venography) to exclude a mass and venous sinus thrombosis, then lumbar puncture with opening pressure if imaging is normal.
  4. How is IIH treated?
    Weight loss and acetazolamide (IIHTT); optic nerve sheath fenestration, CSF shunting or venous stenting for threatened vision.

Station 7: Ocular myasthenia

The examiner shows you: A woman with variable ptosis and diplopia, worse in the evening. Sustained upgaze for a minute makes the ptosis worse; an ice pack on the lid improves it.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Ocular myasthenia gravis.
  2. Name bedside tests.
    Fatigability on sustained upgaze, Cogan lid twitch, and the ice-pack test (improvement of 2 mm or more in ptosis).
  3. Which investigations confirm it?
    Acetylcholine receptor and MuSK antibodies, single-fibre EMG, and CT of the chest for thymoma.
  4. Why refer to neurology?
    About half of patients with ocular myasthenia go on to develop generalised disease, usually within 2 years.

Station 8: Colour vision testing

The examiner shows you: An Ishihara plate book and a patient who reads the test plate but misses most numbers with one eye.

  1. What does the test plate do?
    The first (demonstration) plate can be read by everyone, including people with colour deficiency; failing it suggests poor vision or malingering.
  2. What does monocular colour loss suggest?
    Acquired optic nerve disease such as optic neuritis or compression.
  3. What does Ishihara miss?
    Blue-yellow (tritan) defects; use the Hardy–Rand–Rittler plates or the Farnsworth D-15 for these.
  4. How is inherited red-green deficiency inherited?
    X-linked recessive, affecting about 8% of men and 0.5% of women.

Station 9: Optic atrophy

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph: a pale disc with sharp margins, temporal pallor more marked than nasal, in a young man with bilateral central visual loss.

  1. What do you see?
    Bilateral temporal disc pallor: optic atrophy affecting the papillomacular bundle.
  2. Name causes of temporal pallor.
    Toxic or nutritional optic neuropathy (ethambutol, methanol, tobacco-alcohol, vitamin B12 deficiency), Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, dominant optic atrophy, and previous optic neuritis.
  3. What history do you take?
    Drugs (ethambutol, amiodarone), alcohol and tobacco, diet, family history (maternal line for LHON), and occupational exposures.
  4. Which tests help?
    Visual fields (central or caecocentral scotomas), OCT of RNFL and ganglion cells, B12 and folate, and mitochondrial DNA testing for LHON.

Station 10: Giant cell arteritis

The examiner shows you: A 76-year-old with sudden visual loss, scalp tenderness and jaw claudication. ESR 90 mm/h, CRP 60 mg/L. The disc is chalky-white and swollen.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy from giant cell arteritis.
  2. What is your immediate treatment?
    High-dose steroids the same day, without waiting for a biopsy: IV methylprednisolone (for example 500 mg–1 g daily for 3 days) when vision is affected, then oral prednisolone about 1 mg/kg.
  3. How is the diagnosis confirmed?
    Temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign) or biopsy within 1–2 weeks of starting steroids; a negative biopsy does not exclude GCA (TABUL).
  4. What else protects the other eye and the patient?
    Rheumatology referral, a steroid-sparing drug such as tocilizumab (GiACTA), bone protection, and gastric protection.
