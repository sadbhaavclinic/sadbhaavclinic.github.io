Glaucoma OSCE Stations: 10 Spot Stations on Discs, Fields, OCT, Gonioscopy, Blebs and Drainage Devices

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 10 spot stations, 40 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 10 original stations cover the glaucoma topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. The glaucomatous disc, 2. Humphrey 24-2 field, 3. RNFL OCT printout, 4. Gonioscopy, 5. Central corneal thickness, 6. Trabeculectomy bleb, 7. Glaucoma drainage devices, 8. Acute angle closure, 9. Laser for glaucoma, 10. Primary congenital glaucoma

Station 1: The glaucomatous disc

The examiner shows you: A disc photograph: vertical cup-disc ratio 0.8, inferior rim thinner than superior, a flame haemorrhage at the inferotemporal disc margin and bayoneting of a vessel.

  1. Describe the findings.
    Large vertical cup with inferior rim thinning (the ISNT pattern is broken), a disc haemorrhage at the inferotemporal margin, and bayoneting of vessels where they cross the rim edge.
  2. What is the ISNT rule?
    In a normal disc the neuroretinal rim is usually thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally and Temporally thinnest.
  3. What does the disc haemorrhage mean?
    A sign of progression risk, especially in normal-tension glaucoma; it predicts field loss in the corresponding area.
  4. Why does disc size matter?
    A large disc normally has a large cup; a small disc with a moderate cup may be glaucomatous. Grade with disc size in mind, for example with the DDLS.

Station 2: Humphrey 24-2 field

The examiner shows you: A printout: reliable indices, MD −8 dB, PSD 9 dB, GHT outside normal limits and a superior arcuate defect respecting the horizontal midline.

  1. Is the test reliable?
    Check fixation losses (under 20%), false positives (under about 15%) and false negatives.
  2. Describe the defect.
    A superior arcuate (Bjerrum) defect that respects the horizontal midline: typical of glaucoma, from inferior rim damage.
  3. What do MD and PSD mean?
    Mean deviation is the average loss compared with age-matched normals (overall depression); pattern standard deviation measures localised, irregular loss.
  4. When would you use a 10-2 test?
    When there is, or you suspect, central involvement or advanced glaucoma, since the 24-2 grid can miss paracentral defects.

Station 3: RNFL OCT printout

The examiner shows you: An OCT printout: average RNFL thickness 72 µm, inferior and superior sectors in red, and a matching ganglion cell layer map showing an arcuate thinning.

  1. Interpret the scan.
    Thinning of the inferior and superior RNFL beyond the 1st percentile, with matching ganglion cell loss: consistent with glaucomatous damage.
  2. What is "red disease"?
    A false-positive abnormal result, often from a normal eye that differs from the database: high myopia, tilted discs, unusual disc size or segmentation errors.
  3. What else causes RNFL thinning?
    Previous optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, compressive lesions, and other optic neuropathies; correlate with the disc and field.
  4. How is OCT used to monitor glaucoma?
    Trend analysis of RNFL and ganglion cell thickness over time on the same machine, with attention to signal strength and the floor effect in advanced disease.

Station 4: Gonioscopy

The examiner shows you: Through a four-mirror lens in dim light: inferiorly only Schwalbe's line is visible without indentation; with indentation the ciliary body band appears. No peripheral anterior synechiae.

  1. What do you conclude?
    The angle is appositionally closed (it opens on indentation), not synechially closed: primary angle closure suspect or angle closure depending on IOP and other findings.
  2. Name the angle structures from back to front.
    Ciliary body band, scleral spur, trabecular meshwork (pigmented and non-pigmented parts), Schwalbe's line.
  3. Why use a four-mirror lens for indentation?
    Its small contact area lets you push aqueous into the angle and open it, separating apposition from synechiae.
  4. Name a grading system.
    Shaffer (grade 0–4 by angle width), Scheie (by structures seen) or Spaeth (insertion, angle and iris configuration).

Station 5: Central corneal thickness

The examiner shows you: A pachymetry printout: CCT 490 µm in a patient with IOP 20 mmHg and a suspicious disc.

  1. How does thin CCT affect the IOP reading?
    Goldmann reads falsely low on a thin cornea, so the true IOP is likely higher.
  2. Why else is CCT important?
    In OHTS, thin CCT was an independent risk factor for converting from ocular hypertension to glaucoma.
  3. Should you correct the IOP with a formula?
    No validated formula exists; use CCT to judge risk rather than adjusting the number.
  4. Name situations where CCT changes.
    After LASIK, PRK or SMILE (thinner), in corneal oedema (thicker), in keratoconus, and in Fuchs dystrophy.

Station 6: Trabeculectomy bleb

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph three years after trabeculectomy: a thin, avascular, cystic bleb; with fluorescein in cobalt light a stream of dye flows from it. The eye is red and painful today, with cells in the anterior chamber.

  1. What does the dye test show?
    A positive Seidel test: aqueous leaking through the thin bleb wall.
  2. What is the current problem?
    Blebitis, with risk of progression to bleb-related endophthalmitis.
  3. How do you manage it?
    Urgent, intensive topical antibiotics (for example fortified or fluoroquinolone drops) with close review; vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics if the vitreous is involved.
  4. Name risk factors for bleb leaks and infection.
    Antimetabolites (MMC, 5-FU) producing thin avascular blebs, inferior blebs, contact lens wear, blepharitis and chronic leaks.

Station 7: Glaucoma drainage devices

The examiner shows you: Two devices on a tray: one with a valve housing and a smaller plate, one larger plate with no valve and a ligature around its tube.

  1. Identify the devices.
    A valved device (for example Ahmed) and a non-valved device (for example Baerveldt).
  2. Why is the non-valved tube tied?
    Until a fibrous capsule forms around the plate (about 4–6 weeks), there is no resistance to flow; the ligature or ripcord prevents early hypotony.
  3. What did the ABC and AVB studies show?
    The Baerveldt gave slightly lower IOP with fewer failures; the Ahmed had fewer hypotony-related complications and lowered IOP from day one.
  4. Name complications of tubes.
    Hypotony, tube exposure, tube–cornea touch with endothelial loss, diplopia from the plate, encapsulation (hypertensive phase), and infection.

Station 8: Acute angle closure

The examiner shows you: A 62-year-old hyperopic woman with a red, painful eye, haloes and vomiting. Vision is counting fingers, the cornea is hazy, the pupil is mid-dilated and fixed, and IOP is 58 mmHg.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Acute primary angle closure.
  2. What is your immediate treatment?
    Lie the patient supine; IV or oral acetazolamide (if no contraindication), topical beta-blocker, alpha-agonist and steroid; pilocarpine once IOP starts to fall; analgesia and antiemetic; IV mannitol if IOP stays high.
  3. What is the definitive treatment?
    Laser peripheral iridotomy in both eyes once the cornea clears, or lens extraction (supported by the EAGLE trial in suitable eyes).
  4. Name the glaukomflecken and why they matter.
    Grey-white anterior subcapsular lens opacities from ischaemic necrosis of lens epithelium: evidence of a previous acute attack.

Station 9: Laser for glaucoma

The examiner shows you: A laser lens with a single angled mirror and a frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser with a fixed 400 µm spot.

  1. Identify the procedure.
    Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT) with a gonio-laser lens.
  2. How does SLT work?
    It targets pigmented trabecular meshwork cells with short pulses that spare surrounding tissue, increasing outflow through a biological response.
  3. What did the LiGHT trial show?
    SLT first was as good as drops for quality of life, and about three-quarters of eyes were drop-free at 3 years.
  4. How does laser iridotomy differ?
    It uses Nd:YAG (sometimes with argon pre-treatment) through an iridotomy lens (for example Abraham or Wise) to make a full-thickness hole in the peripheral iris, relieving pupil block in angle closure.

Station 10: Primary congenital glaucoma

The examiner shows you: A 4-month-old with tearing, photophobia and large, hazy corneas. Under anaesthesia the corneal diameter is 13 mm with curvilinear breaks in Descemet membrane.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Primary congenital glaucoma.
  2. What are the breaks called, and how are they oriented?
    Haab striae: horizontal or curvilinear breaks in Descemet membrane caused by corneal stretching (unlike the vertical breaks of birth trauma).
  3. What do you measure at examination under anaesthesia?
    IOP (anaesthetic agents affect it), corneal diameter, CCT, gonioscopy, disc cupping and axial length; refraction.
  4. What is the treatment?
    Surgery is first line: goniotomy or trabeculotomy (including 360° catheter trabeculotomy), then trabeculectomy or tubes if needed. Medicines are a bridge. Lifelong follow-up and amblyopia management.
