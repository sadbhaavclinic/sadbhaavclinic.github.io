Ophthalmic Nursing Module 9: Post-Operative Care, Intravitreal Injections and Complications

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Teach the patient and the carer: drops, shield at night, hygiene, activity limits and the warning signs (increasing pain, falling vision, more redness, discharge).
  • Endophthalmitis after cataract surgery or injection is the complication not to miss: same-day review for pain and falling vision.
  • Patients with gas must not fly, travel to high altitude or have nitrous oxide; they posture as instructed.
  • After intravitreal injection, check perfusion (at least hand movements or as per protocol) before the patient leaves.

Immediate post-operative care

  • Check vital signs as per protocol (more often after general anaesthesia or sedation).
  • Check the dressing or shield: dry, intact, not too tight.
  • Pain: mild discomfort and grittiness are expected; severe pain, nausea and headache may mean raised IOP. Check IOP as ordered (often within hours after surgery with viscoelastic, after YAG laser, or in glaucoma patients).
  • Positioning: as instructed by the surgeon (face down, side, or flat).
  • Safe mobility: the patient may have poor vision in both eyes for a few hours; help with walking and toileting.
  • Diabetic patients: check glucose before discharge, especially if they were fasting or had steroid injections.

Discharge teaching

TopicWhat to teach (adapt to the surgeon’s instructions)
DropsWhich drops, how often, how long, how to taper; wash hands; do not touch the eye with the bottle; 5 minutes apart; written chart in the patient’s language; teach-back.
ShieldWear at night (often for the first week) to stop rubbing during sleep.
HygieneClean around the eye with boiled and cooled water and sterile cotton from inner to outer corner; avoid soap and water entering the eye for the period advised; no eye make-up.
ActivitiesNormal light activity, reading and TV are fine; avoid heavy lifting, bending with head below waist, swimming and dusty places for the period advised; no rubbing.
MedicinesContinue usual medicines unless told otherwise (blood pressure, diabetes, blood thinners).
Follow-upDates for review; when glasses can be changed (often after about a month after cataract surgery).
Warning signsIncreasing pain, falling vision, increasing redness, discharge, flashes or a curtain, nausea and vomiting with eye pain: come back the same day, day or night.

Patient and carer resources: after cataract surgery care, caring for a parent after eye surgery (English, Gujarati and Hindi), printable leaflet PDFs on the patient leaflets page.

Eye dressings and wound care

  • Eye pad: sterile pad taped from forehead to cheek, with the eye closed; used after some operations or for corneal epithelial defects (not after chemical injury before irrigation, and not in infected eyes).
  • Pressure dressing: two pads, firmly taped; used after enucleation or some lid surgery; check that it is not too tight.
  • Shield: rigid plastic or metal; protects without pressing; used after most intraocular surgery and for open globe injuries.
  • Lid and skin wounds: keep clean and dry; antibiotic ointment as prescribed; cold compresses for swelling for the first 48 hours, then warm as advised; suture removal dates.
  • Bandage contact lens: after PRK or for epithelial defects; do not remove at home; report pain or discharge.

Intravitreal injections

  1. Check identity, eye, drug and dose, consent, and that the eye is not infected (lids, conjunctiva). Ask about recent stroke or heart attack and pregnancy as per protocol.
  2. Topical anaesthetic; povidone-iodine 5% onto the eye surface (and 10% on the lashes and lids), allowed to act for the time set by the protocol (commonly at least 30 seconds to 2 minutes).
  3. Lid speculum to keep lashes away; mask for staff and patient, or a no-talking policy.
  4. Injection by the doctor, usually 3.5–4 mm behind the limbus (depending on whether the eye has its natural lens).
  5. After injection: check that the optic nerve is perfused, for example by the patient counting fingers or seeing hand movements, or by ophthalmoscopy, as per protocol; check IOP if required.
  6. Teach warning signs of infection and retinal detachment; most patients have a little grittiness and sometimes a small red patch (harmless).

See the intravitreal injection protocol and patient guide.

Gas and silicone oil

  • Common gases: air (absorbed in days), SF6 (about 2 weeks), C3F8 (about 6–8 weeks). Silicone oil stays until removed.
  • With gas in the eye: no flying, no travel over mountain passes, no nitrous oxide anaesthesia; wear a gas wristband; vision is very poor until the gas reduces; a moving “level” is seen.
  • Posturing: face down or on one side for the hours advised (often 45–50 minutes every hour for several days). Teach pressure-area care and offer support aids.
  • Silicone oil can raise IOP and cause cataract; it needs removal surgery later in most cases.

Go deeper: blurred vision after cataract surgery, endophthalmitis, YAG capsulotomy, treat-and-extend anti-VEGF.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

List the warning signs every post-operative patient should know.

Increasing pain, falling vision, increasing redness, discharge, flashes or curtain, nausea or vomiting with eye pain.

How do you tell TASS from endophthalmitis?

TASS: within 12–24 hours, corneal haze from edge to edge, little pain, sterile. Endophthalmitis: usually days 2–7, pain, falling vision, hypopyon, vitritis, infective.

What checks follow an intravitreal injection?

Optic nerve perfusion (finger counting or hand movements, or ophthalmoscopy) and IOP if required by protocol, plus teaching of warning signs.

How long does SF6 gas last?

About two weeks (C3F8 about 6–8 weeks).

What is a positive Seidel test?

Fluorescein streaming from a wound under blue light, showing aqueous leaking.

Self-test: 8 MCQs

Answer all, then press “Check my score”. Option order is mixed.

1. The eye shield after cataract surgery is mainly worn:

Show answer

Answer: At night to prevent rubbing. Usually for the first week.

2. Endophthalmitis after surgery usually appears:

Show answer

Answer: In 2–7 days. Earlier for virulent organisms.

3. C3F8 gas lasts about:

Show answer

Answer: 6–8 weeks. Longest of the common gases.

4. After intravitreal injection, perfusion can be checked by:

Show answer

Answer: Counting fingers or hand movements. Or ophthalmoscopy.

5. Posterior capsule opacification is treated with:

Show answer

Answer: Nd:YAG capsulotomy. Outpatient laser.

6. A positive Seidel test shows:

Show answer

Answer: Wound leak. Fluorescein streaming.

7. Patients with gas in the eye must avoid:

Show answer

Answer: Air travel. Gas expands.

8. Cystoid macular oedema after cataract surgery is treated with:

Show answer

Answer: NSAID and steroid drops. Usually responds.
