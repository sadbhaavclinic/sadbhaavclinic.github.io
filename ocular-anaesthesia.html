Ocular Anaesthesia: Blocks, Complications and Exam Essentials

Last updated: October 2026

Choosing the safest anaesthesia for eye surgery, recognising block complications early, and the anaesthetic facts examiners love: drug doses, the oculocardiac reflex, malignant hyperthermia and gas bubbles.

Techniques

TechniqueBest forAdvantagesRisks
Topical ± intracameral lidocainePhaco in cooperative patientsNo needle, quick visual recoveryPatient movement; not for long or complex surgery
Sub-Tenon (blunt cannula)Phaco, vitrectomy, trabeculectomyGood akinesia and analgesia, very low risk of globe perforationChemosis, subconjunctival haemorrhage
Peribulbar (needle outside the cone)Most intraocular surgeryGood akinesiaGlobe perforation, retrobulbar haemorrhage (lower than retrobulbar)
Retrobulbar (needle inside the cone)Rapid deep blockFast, small volumeBrainstem anaesthesia, optic nerve injury, retrobulbar haemorrhage, globe perforation
General anaesthesiaChildren, open globe, very anxious or uncooperative patients, long surgeryFull controlSystemic risks; postoperative nausea

Block complications to recognise

  • Globe perforation: higher in axial lengths over 26 mm, staphyloma and previous buckle; prefer sub-Tenon or topical in such eyes.
  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage: tense proptosis and raised IOP; decompress with lateral canthotomy if vision is threatened.
  • Brainstem anaesthesia: within minutes: confusion, contralateral amaurosis, cranial nerve palsies, apnoea. Support the airway and circulation until it wears off; monitor every patient after a needle block.
  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity: perioral tingling, seizures, arrhythmias; give 20% lipid emulsion (1.5 mL/kg bolus) and follow resuscitation guidelines.

Drugs and doses

DrugUseMaximum dose (adult)
Lidocaine 2%Fast onset, about 1–2 hours3 mg/kg without adrenaline; 7 mg/kg with adrenaline
Bupivacaine 0.5%Slow onset, long action2 mg/kg
HyaluronidaseSpeeds spread of the blockRare allergy can mimic orbital cellulitis

Physiology and exam favourites

  • Oculocardiac reflex: traction on muscles (especially the medial rectus) or pressure on the globe causes bradycardia; afferent trigeminal, efferent vagus. Release traction; give atropine if it persists. Commoner in children.
  • Effect on IOP: suxamethonium, ketamine, coughing and straining raise IOP; propofol, thiopentone and volatile agents lower it. Avoid suxamethonium in open globe injury if possible.
  • Malignant hyperthermia: triggered by suxamethonium and volatile agents; rising end-tidal CO2, rigidity and fever; treat with dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg. More common in patients with strabismus and some myopathies.
  • Intraocular gas: nitrous oxide diffuses into SF6 or C3F8 bubbles and can raise IOP dangerously; avoid it for the life of the bubble. No flying either.
  • Anticoagulants: usually continued for topical, intracameral and sub-Tenon anaesthesia; check INR for warfarin; avoid sharp-needle blocks where possible.
