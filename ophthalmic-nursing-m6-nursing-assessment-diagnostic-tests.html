Ophthalmic Nursing Module 6: Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Tests

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Always measure vision first (before drops, lights or touching the eye), each eye separately, with glasses, then with pinhole if below 6/6.
  • Record vision exactly: 6/12 (or 20/40), N6 for near, CF at 1 m, HM, PL with projection, or NPL.
  • Before dilating, check the angle risk and ask about driving; before applanation, use anaesthetic and fluorescein and disinfect the prism.
  • Before fluorescein angiography: consent, allergy history, cannula, emergency drugs and a doctor nearby.

Exam map: III Nursing assessment (history, basic and special diagnostic tests, triage) – about 20% of the CRNO exam.

Taking an eye history

  • Presenting complaint: which eye, when it started (sudden or gradual), pain, redness, discharge, flashes, floaters, double vision, distortion.
  • Past eye history: glasses or contact lenses, previous surgery or laser, injuries, lazy eye in childhood, glaucoma.
  • Medical history: diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, asthma, kidney disease, thyroid disease, arthritis, pregnancy.
  • Medicines: all drops and tablets, especially steroids, blood thinners, tamsulosin, hydroxychloroquine, diabetic medicines.
  • Allergies: drugs (sulfonamides, penicillin), iodine, latex, adhesive tape.
  • Family history: glaucoma, retinal detachment, inherited disease, squint.
  • Social: occupation, driving, smoking, who helps at home (important for post-operative drops).
  • Vital signs and tests: blood pressure, pulse, blood sugar where needed; laboratory results such as HbA1c, INR, ESR/CRP as ordered.

Visual acuity

TestHowRecord
Snellen or logMAR chartAt 6 m (or 3 m with a reversed chart in a mirror). Cover one eye with an occluder, not the fingers. Test with current glasses.6/6, 6/9, 6/12 … (20/20 in feet; 1.0 decimal; 0.0 logMAR). Add “+2” or “−1” for partly read lines.
PinholeIf worse than 6/6, repeat with a pinhole occluder.Improvement suggests a refractive cause; no improvement suggests disease.
Low visionIf the top letter cannot be read, walk forward (record 3/60, 1/60). Then counting fingers, hand movements, light perception with direction (projection).CF 1 m, HM, PL+ with accurate projection, NPL.
Near visionN-series or Jaeger chart at about 33–40 cm with reading glasses.N6, N8 (or J1, J2).
ChildrenPicture charts, Lea symbols, HOTV or tumbling E; preferential looking cards in babies; fixing and following.Note the test used.
Illiterate adultsTumbling E or Landolt C chart.As Snellen.

Do vision before dilating drops or bright lights, and always test the right eye first unless policy says otherwise, so records are consistent.

Other basic tests

  • Colour vision: Ishihara plates (red-green), held in good daylight at about 75 cm; important in optic neuritis, ethambutol and hydroxychloroquine checks. See colour vision testing.
  • Amsler grid: one eye at a time at reading distance, with reading glasses; patient looks at the central dot and reports wavy or missing lines (macular disease).
  • Stereopsis: Titmus fly, TNO or Lang tests for depth perception, mostly in children and squint.
  • Visual field: confrontation at the bedside; automated perimetry (for example 24-2 or 10-2 programmes) in glaucoma and neurological disease. Make the patient comfortable, correct the near lens, cover the other eye, explain fixation and breaks. See field test.
  • Eye movements and cover test: follow a target in the nine directions; ask about double vision.
  • Pupils: size in light and dark, shape, direct and consensual reaction, swinging torch test for RAPD.
  • Keratometry: measures corneal curvature; needed for IOL power and contact lens fitting.
  • Pachymetry: corneal thickness; used in glaucoma and before refractive surgery.

Measuring eye pressure (tonometry)

MethodNotes
Goldmann applanation (gold standard)Topical anaesthetic plus fluorescein; cobalt blue light; align the two semicircles. Disinfect or replace the prism after each patient (adenovirus, prions).
Non-contact (air puff)No anaesthetic; good for screening; less accurate at high readings.
Rebound tonometerSmall disposable probe, no anaesthetic; useful for children and home monitoring.
Schiotz (indentation)Patient lying down; weight on the cornea; conversion table; less used now.
Hand-held applanationFor patients lying down or in theatre.

Do not squeeze the lids or press on the eye when holding lids open: this falsely raises the reading. Record time of day and the method. See tonometers compared.

Dilating the pupil

  • Check that dilation has been ordered, and ask about previous angle closure and allergy. Narrow angles do not stop dilation when the doctor needs it, but the patient should know the warning symptoms.
  • Tell the patient that vision will be blurred and light-sensitive for about 4–6 hours (tropicamide) and they should not drive until it clears.
  • Common combination: tropicamide 1% with phenylephrine 2.5%; dilation takes about 20–30 minutes. Dark irises may need longer or repeat drops.

Complications of tests to watch for

  • Fluorescein: nausea, vomiting, fainting, skin rash, rarely anaphylaxis.
  • Applanation: corneal abrasion; spread of infection if not disinfected.
  • Dilation: angle closure (rare), fall risk from blurred vision.
  • Gonioscopy: corneal abrasion.

Go deeper: slit-lamp technique, FFA and ICG patterns, B-scan, biometry, contrast sensitivity, exophthalmometry, imaging devices compared.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

A patient cannot read the top letter at 6 m. How do you continue?

Move them closer (or move the chart) and record the distance, e.g. 3/60; if still unable, test counting fingers, hand movements and light perception with projection.

What does improvement with pinhole mean?

The reduced vision is mostly due to refractive error.

List the preparation for fluorescein angiography.

Consent; allergy, asthma, pregnancy and kidney history; IV cannula; emergency drugs and doctor available; warn about nausea, yellow skin and orange urine.

Why disinfect the tonometer prism between patients?

To prevent transmission of adenovirus and other infections; follow the manufacturer’s method or use disposable tips.

What should patients be told after dilation?

Blur and glare for several hours; do not drive until vision returns; wear sunglasses.

Self-test: 8 MCQs

Answer all, then press “Check my score”. Option order is mixed.

1. Visual acuity should be measured:

Show answer

Answer: Before drops or examination, each eye separately. Standard practice.

2. 6/60 means the patient sees at 6 m what a normal eye sees at:

Show answer

Answer: 60 m. Very reduced vision.

3. The gold standard tonometer is:

Show answer

Answer: Goldmann applanation. Needs anaesthetic and fluorescein.

4. After fluorescein angiography, urine may be:

Show answer

Answer: Yellow-orange. For about a day.

5. Schirmer test measures:

Show answer

Answer: Tear production. Wetting in mm over 5 minutes.

6. The Ishihara test detects:

Show answer

Answer: Red-green colour deficiency. Also used in optic nerve disease.

7. Pressing on the globe while holding the lids open during tonometry will:

Show answer

Answer: Raise IOP falsely. Avoid pressure on the globe.

8. Before ICG angiography ask specifically about allergy to:

Show answer

Answer: Iodine. ICG contains iodide.
