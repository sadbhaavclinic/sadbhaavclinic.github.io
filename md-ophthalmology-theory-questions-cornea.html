Applied Anatomy & Physiology

CO 1. Discuss in detail the anatomy of the corneoscleral (surgical) limbus and its importance. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Surgical limbus

  • Transition zone between cornea and sclera, about 1–2 mm wide; anterior limbal border (end of Bowman’s layer) and posterior border (scleral spur level).
  • Surgically: blue zone (clear cornea overlapping sclera, about 1 mm) and white zone (sclera over the trabecular meshwork, about 1 mm).

Contents

  • Palisades of Vogt with limbal epithelial stem cells; marginal arcades of the anterior ciliary arteries; Schlemm’s canal and trabecular meshwork deep to it.

Importance

  • Incision sites for cataract and glaucoma surgery (scleral tunnel, trabeculectomy); landmark for injections; stem cell reservoir (LSCD, SLET); site of perilimbal flush, phlyctens, Vogt limbal girdle, limbal dermoid, OSSN.
CO 2. Factors involved in maintaining corneal transparency. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Factors maintaining transparency

  • Anatomical: regular stromal collagen fibrils of uniform diameter (about 25–35 nm) and spacing (lattice theory of Maurice; less than half the wavelength of light), non-keratinised smooth epithelium, absence of blood vessels and myelinated nerves.
  • Physiological: relative deturgescence (stromal water about 78%) kept by the endothelial pump and barrier, epithelial barrier, evaporation from the tear film.
  • Keratocyte crystallins reduce light scatter; normal IOP.

Loss of transparency

  • Oedema (endothelial failure, raised IOP), scarring, vascularisation, deposits, dystrophies.
CO 3. Endothelial pump and corneal endothelium — physiology, and changing concepts in the management of endothelial dysfunction. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Physiology

  • Single layer of hexagonal cells (about 2500–3000 cells/mm² in adults); does not regenerate in humans; cells enlarge and spread with age and injury.
  • Barrier (leaky tight junctions) and pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, bicarbonate transport, carbonic anhydrase) keep the stroma dehydrated (pump-leak theory).
  • Decompensation below about 500–700 cells/mm²; specular microscopy: cell density, coefficient of variation, hexagonality.

Changing concepts in managing endothelial dysfunction

  • Hypertonic saline, IOP control, bandage contact lens (symptomatic).
  • From penetrating keratoplasty to endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK, DMEK, PDEK): faster recovery, less astigmatism, lower rejection.
  • Descemet stripping only (DSO) with ROCK inhibitors (ripasudil) in Fuchs dystrophy; cultured endothelial cell injection with ROCK inhibitor (Kinoshita); artificial endothelial layers (research).

Revise from: Fuchs Endothelial Corneal Dystrophy

CO 4. Describe tear formation and the precorneal tear film (with diagram and neuronal reflex arc). Discuss tear-film dynamics in disease, with medications and in iatrogenic situations, and their evaluation. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Tear formation and film

  • Lipid layer (meibomian glands; reduces evaporation), aqueous layer (main and accessory lacrimal glands of Krause and Wolfring), mucin layer (goblet cells and glycocalyx); now seen as an aqueous–mucin gel with a lipid surface.

Reflex arc

  • Afferent: trigeminal (ophthalmic) nerve from cornea and conjunctiva → superior salivatory nucleus → nervus intermedius (facial) → greater petrosal nerve → pterygopalatine ganglion → zygomatic and lacrimal nerves → lacrimal gland.

Dynamics in disease and medications

  • Evaporative (meibomian gland dysfunction, lagophthalmos) or aqueous-deficient (Sjögren, ageing) dry eye; hyperosmolarity and inflammation.
  • Drugs: antihistamines, antidepressants, beta-blockers, isotretinoin reduce tears; preserved drops (BAK) damage the surface.
  • Iatrogenic: LASIK and SMILE (nerve cutting), cataract surgery, blepharoplasty, radiotherapy.

Evaluation

  • Tear meniscus height, TBUT and non-invasive break-up time, Schirmer I and II, fluorescein and lissamine staining, osmolarity, meibography, interferometry, MMP-9 test, impression cytology, questionnaires (OSDI).

CO 5. Corneal anatomy and its implications for refractive surgery. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneal anatomy

  • Horizontal diameter about 11.5–12 mm, vertical 10.5–11 mm; central thickness about 540 µm (thicker peripherally); anterior radius 7.8 mm, posterior 6.5 mm; power about +43 D (anterior +48, posterior −6).
  • Layers: epithelium (50 µm), Bowman layer (acellular, does not regenerate), stroma (90%: lamellae, keratocytes; anterior stroma stiffer), pre-Descemet (Dua) layer, Descemet membrane, endothelium.

Implications for refractive surgery

  • Thickness limits ablation depth: residual stromal bed of at least 250–300 µm and percentage tissue altered under 40% to avoid ectasia.
  • Anterior stroma gives most biomechanical strength (flap cuts weaken it more than SMILE caps).
  • Epithelial remodelling causes regression; epithelial maps detect early keratoconus.
  • Corneal nerves cut by flaps: dry eye; Bowman layer removal in PRK: haze risk (MMC).
  • Posterior corneal astigmatism and asphericity (Q value) influence results.
CO 6. Corneal metabolism and wound healing. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneal metabolism

  • Glucose from the aqueous (main source), oxygen from the tear film (open eye) and palpebral conjunctiva (closed eye); epithelium uses glycolysis and hexose monophosphate shunt; lactate removed via aqueous; hypoxia (contact lenses) causes lactate build-up and oedema.
  • Deturgescence maintained by endothelial Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump and barrier, epithelial barrier, evaporation, IOP.

Wound healing

  • Epithelium: cell migration (hours), proliferation and adhesion (hemidesmosomes over weeks); limbal stem cells supply new cells (X, Y, Z hypothesis).
  • Stroma: keratocyte apoptosis, activation into fibroblasts and myofibroblasts (haze), collagen deposition and remodelling over months; scars are weaker.
  • Descemet and endothelium: endothelial cells enlarge and migrate (little mitosis in humans); Descemet regenerates slowly.
  • Factors: growth factors (EGF, TGF-beta), MMPs; delayed by diabetes, steroids, NSAIDs, preservatives, neurotrophic state.
CO 7. Corneal oedema and decompensation — causes and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes of corneal oedema

  • Endothelial dysfunction: Fuchs dystrophy, PBK and ABK, surgical trauma, graft failure, endotheliitis (HSV, CMV), ICE syndrome, PPCD and CHED, Descemet detachment, toxic (TASS).
  • Raised IOP: acute angle closure.
  • Epithelial: hypoxia (contact lenses), trauma.
  • Inflammation: keratitis, uveitis.

Management

  • Treat the cause (IOP lowering, antivirals, steroids for endotheliitis, reattach Descemet with gas).
  • Symptomatic: hypertonic saline 5% drops and ointment, bandage contact lens for bullae, hair dryer in the morning, rho-kinase inhibitors (ripasudil, netarsudil) in some cases.
  • Definitive: endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK, DSAEK), PK for scarred corneas; DSO in selected Fuchs.
  • Palliative for poor visual potential: anterior stromal puncture, PTK, amniotic membrane, conjunctival flap.
CO 8. Ocular surface — concept and components. · outline

Ocular surface concept

  • An integrated functional unit (Thoft and Friend; later the lacrimal functional unit) consisting of the corneal and conjunctival epithelia, limbal stem cells, tear film, the lacrimal and meibomian glands, eyelids (blink), and their sensory and autonomic innervation, all linked by nerves, immune system and endocrine factors.

Components

  • Epithelia (corneal, conjunctival with goblet cells), limbus (stem cell niche: palisades of Vogt), tear film (lipid, aqueous, mucin layers), lacrimal glands, meibomian glands, lids and blink, nerves (corneal sensation drives reflex tearing), immune system (CALT).

Clinical relevance

  • Dry eye, limbal stem cell deficiency, neurotrophic keratopathy, chemical injuries, cicatrising diseases; surgical success (keratoplasty, refractive surgery) depends on a healthy surface.
CO 9. Vital stains in corneal evaluation. · outline

Fluorescein

  • Stains areas of epithelial loss (pools in defects), assesses tear break-up time, Seidel test (leak), applanation tonometry, contact lens fitting, Jones dye test; cobalt blue light with yellow filter.

Rose bengal

  • Stains devitalised and unprotected epithelial cells lacking mucin; stings; toxic to epithelium; dry eye, SLK, filaments.

Lissamine green

  • Stains similar to rose bengal but less irritating; best for conjunctival staining in dry eye (Oxford and NEI scores).

Others

  • Trypan blue (capsule, endothelial staining in eye banking, DMEK graft), alcian blue (mucus), methylene blue, indocyanine green; staining scores: Oxford, NEI, van Bijsterveld.
CO 10. Method of clinical examination of the cornea. DNB / UNIV · outline

Clinical examination of the cornea

  • History: pain, redness, reduced vision, photophobia, trauma, contact lens use, surgery, systemic disease.
  • External: torch light and loupe; size (horizontal diameter), shape, surface (lustre), transparency, sensation (cotton wisp, Cochet–Bonnet aesthesiometer), Placido disc.
  • Slit lamp: diffuse illumination, direct focal (optical section: depth of opacities), indirect illumination, retroillumination (direct and indirect: oedema, vacuoles, KPs), specular reflection (endothelium), sclerotic scatter (subtle opacities); fluorescein and other stains; measure size of ulcer and infiltrate.
  • Investigations: keratometry, topography and tomography, pachymetry, specular microscopy, AS-OCT, confocal microscopy, corneal scraping.

Ocular Surface Disease & Dry Eye

CO 11. Discuss the aetiology, classification, investigations (Schirmer and others) and treatment of dry eye, with recent trends in diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition (TFOS DEWS III, 2025)

  • A multifactorial, symptomatic disease with loss of homeostasis of the tear film and/or ocular surface; tear instability and hyperosmolarity, inflammation, surface damage and neurosensory abnormalities drive it.

Aetiology and classification

  • DEWS II (2017) split aqueous-deficient (Sjögren and non-Sjögren) from evaporative (meibomian gland dysfunction, lids, contact lenses, vitamin A deficiency).
  • DEWS III: almost all dry eye has an evaporative component, so patients are subtyped by their drivers (tear film layers, eyelids and meibomian glands, ocular surface) rather than two boxes.

Investigations

  • DEWS III sequence: symptoms (OSDI-6 score of 4 or more), then non-invasive tear break-up time under 10 s or osmolarity 308 mOsm/L or more, then ocular surface staining (fluorescein for cornea, lissamine green for conjunctiva and lid margin).
  • Optional: Schirmer I without anaesthesia (5 mm or less in 5 minutes is severe), meibography, interferometry, MMP-9, serology for Sjögren (anti-Ro, anti-La).

Treatment (stepwise, matched to drivers)

  • Education, environment, lid hygiene and warm compresses, preservative-free lubricants.
  • Anti-inflammatory: topical cyclosporine, lifitegrast, short steroid courses, oral doxycycline or azithromycin.
  • Punctal plugs, autologous serum drops, scleral lenses, moisture goggles; severe: tarsorrhaphy, amniotic membrane, minor salivary gland transplantation.

Recent trends

  • Perfluorohexyloctane (evaporative dry eye), lotilaner 0.25% for Demodex blepharitis, varenicline nasal spray, thermal pulsation and intense pulsed light for meibomian gland dysfunction.

Revise from: TFOS DEWS III: What Changed in Dry Eye Diagnosis and Treatment

CO 12. Classification of ocular lubricants and tear substitutes; role of preservatives. DNB / UNIV · outline

Classification of lubricants

  • By viscosity: drops (low), gels (medium), ointments (high, night use).
  • By active agent: cellulose derivatives (CMC, HPMC), polyvinyl alcohol, povidone, hyaluronic acid (viscoelastic, healing), polyethylene glycol and propylene glycol, carbomers (gels), lipid-containing emulsions and sprays (evaporative dry eye), trehalose, osmoprotectants (glycerine, L-carnitine), perfluorohexyloctane (NovaTears / Miebo).
  • Biological: autologous serum, platelet-rich plasma, amniotic membrane extract.

Role of preservatives

  • BAK is toxic with frequent use (epithelial damage, inflammation); soft preservatives (sodium perborate, Purite, Polyquad) dissipate on the eye; preservative-free unit doses or multi-dose PF bottles are preferred for use more than 4–6 times daily, moderate-severe dry eye, glaucoma patients on multiple drops.

CO 13. Sjögren syndrome — features, investigations and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Sjögren syndrome

  • Autoimmune exocrinopathy with lymphocytic infiltration of lacrimal and salivary glands; primary or secondary (with RA, SLE, scleroderma); women 40–60 years.

Features

  • Severe aqueous-deficient dry eye, filamentary keratitis, dry mouth, parotid swelling, arthralgia, vasculitis, neuropathy, renal and lung involvement; lymphoma risk (MALT).

Investigations

  • Schirmer (under 5 mm in 5 minutes), ocular staining score, TBUT, anti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB) antibodies, ANA, RF, minor salivary gland biopsy (focus score 1 or more), salivary flow; ACR/EULAR 2016 criteria.

Management

  • Ocular: preservative-free lubricants, gels, punctal plugs, cyclosporine or lifitegrast, short course steroids, autologous serum, moisture goggles, scleral lenses, treat filaments.
  • Systemic: pilocarpine or cevimeline for dryness, hydroxychloroquine, immunosuppression for extraglandular disease; rheumatology follow-up.
CO 14. Ocular surface disorders and their management, including severe ocular surface disease (unilateral and bilateral). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular surface disorders

  • Dry eye, exposure, neurotrophic keratopathy, chemical injury, SJS and pemphigoid, LSCD, xerophthalmia, OSSN.

Principles of management

  • Stabilise the tear film and lids first (lubrication, lid correction, punctal occlusion), control inflammation, promote epithelial healing (bandage lens, amniotic membrane, autologous serum), restore stem cells, then visual rehabilitation.

Severe disease

  • Unilateral: conjunctival-limbal autograft, SLET from the healthy eye.
  • Bilateral: living-related or cadaveric keratolimbal allograft (with systemic immunosuppression), cultivated oral mucosal epithelial transplantation, keratoprosthesis (Boston type I or II, osteo-odonto) as last resort.
CO 15. Stevens-Johnson syndrome and ocular cicatricial pemphigoid — acute and chronic ocular management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Stevens–Johnson syndrome: acute phase

  • Drug-induced (sulphonamides, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs, allopurinol) mucocutaneous reaction.
  • Daily examination, lubrication, topical steroids, sweeping fornices; early amniotic membrane transplantation over lids and ocular surface (within days) reduces chronic sequelae.

Chronic phase

  • Dry eye, trichiasis and lid margin keratinisation, symblepharon, LSCD, corneal vascularisation.
  • Scleral lenses (PROSE), mucous membrane grafting to lid margins, autologous serum, punctal occlusion, keratoprosthesis.

Ocular cicatricial pemphigoid

  • Autoimmune (type II) against basement membrane; progressive subepithelial fibrosis, fornix shortening (Foster and Mondino staging); conjunctival biopsy with immunofluorescence.
  • Systemic immunosuppression (dapsone, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, IVIG); avoid surgery during active disease.

Revise from: SJS, Ocular Cicatricial Pemphigoid and Neurotrophic Keratopathy

CO 16. What are limbal stem cells? Clinical and applied significance; classification and management of limbal stem cell deficiency. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Limbal stem cells

  • Located in the palisades of Vogt (basal epithelium); slow-cycling, maintain the corneal epithelium; barrier to conjunctival ingrowth.

Significance of deficiency

  • Conjunctivalisation (late fluorescein staining, goblet cells on cornea on impression cytology), vascularisation, persistent epithelial defects, poor vision; failure of keratoplasty alone.

Classification

  • Primary (aniridia, ectodermal dysplasia) vs secondary (chemical burns, SJS, contact lenses, surgery, radiation); partial vs total; unilateral vs bilateral.

Management

  • Partial: sequential sector conjunctival epitheliectomy, amniotic membrane.
  • Total unilateral: CLAU or SLET (from the fellow eye; 2 × 2 mm limbal biopsy).
  • Bilateral: KLAL or living-related CLAL with immunosuppression, CLET from relatives, COMET; keratoplasty later; Boston KPro.
CO 17. Amniotic membrane grafting — techniques and applications in ocular disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Amniotic membrane

  • Innermost layer of the placenta; basement membrane and stroma with anti-inflammatory, anti-scarring, anti-angiogenic factors; promotes epithelialisation.

Techniques

  • Graft (inlay, basement membrane up): becomes incorporated as a substrate.
  • Patch (overlay, basement membrane down): biological bandage, falls off.
  • Sandwich, multilayer for deep ulcers; sutureless (ProKera, glue).

Applications

  • Acute chemical injury and SJS, persistent epithelial defects, neurotrophic ulcers, small perforations (multilayer), pterygium and conjunctival reconstruction, bullous keratopathy, band keratopathy after chelation, OSSN excision, symblepharon release.

Revise from: Amniotic Membrane in Eye Surgery: Which Side Is Up, Dry vs Wet Membrane, Inlay, Onlay and Fixation

CO 18. Xerophthalmia. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Xerophthalmia

  • Ocular signs of vitamin A deficiency; still important in preschool children in poor nutrition, measles and diarrhoea.

WHO classification

  • XN night blindness; X1A conjunctival xerosis; X1B Bitot spots; X2 corneal xerosis; X3A ulceration or keratomalacia (< 1/3 cornea); X3B (≥ 1/3); XS corneal scar; XF xerophthalmic fundus.

Treatment (WHO)

  • Vitamin A orally on day 1, day 2 and after 2 weeks: 200,000 IU (over 1 year), 100,000 IU (6–12 months), 50,000 IU (under 6 months).
  • Treat corneal ulcers (antibiotics), malnutrition and infection.

Prevention

  • Six-monthly prophylactic doses (India: 9 months to 5 years), food fortification, breastfeeding, green leafy vegetables, measles immunisation.
CO 19. Exposure keratitis. · outline

Exposure keratitis

  • Corneal damage from incomplete lid closure or inadequate blinking.

Causes

  • Lagophthalmos (facial palsy, scarring, after ptosis surgery), proptosis (thyroid eye disease, orbital tumours), nocturnal lagophthalmos, coma and ICU patients, poor Bell phenomenon, leprosy.

Features

  • Inferior punctate keratopathy, epithelial defects, ulceration, melting, infection, perforation.

Management

  • Lubricants, ointment at night, taping, moisture chambers (ICU: polyethylene covers), bandage contact lens or scleral lens, botulinum-induced ptosis, tarsorrhaphy (temporary or permanent), gold weight, lateral tarsal strip, treat proptosis (decompression); treat infection.
CO 20. Neuroparalytic (neurotrophic) keratitis. · outline

Neuroparalytic (neurotrophic) keratitis

  • Corneal epithelial breakdown due to loss of corneal sensory innervation (trigeminal nerve).

Causes

  • Herpes simplex and zoster keratitis, trigeminal nerve damage (surgery for acoustic neuroma or trigeminal neuralgia, stroke, tumours), diabetes, topical drug abuse (anaesthetics), chemical burns, contact lenses, corneal surgery, leprosy, congenital (familial dysautonomia).

Mackie staging

  • Stage 1: punctate keratopathy, dry surface; stage 2: persistent epithelial defect with rolled edges; stage 3: stromal ulcer, melt, perforation.

Management

  • Stop toxic drops, preservative-free lubricants, treat exposure, bandage contact lens, autologous serum, cenegermin (rhNGF), amniotic membrane, tarsorrhaphy, corneal neurotisation for long-term cure; glue or keratoplasty for perforation.

Revise from: SJS, Ocular Cicatricial Pemphigoid and Neurotrophic Keratopathy

CO 21. Persistent epithelial defects and recurrent corneal erosion — causes and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Persistent epithelial defects (PED)

  • Defect failing to heal within 2 weeks despite standard care.
  • Causes: neurotrophic keratopathy, LSCD, exposure, dry eye, toxicity (preservatives, anaesthetics, antivirals), diabetes, infection, inflammation (autoimmune).
  • Management: stop toxic drugs, lubricants, bandage contact lens, autologous serum, amniotic membrane, tarsorrhaphy, cenegermin, doxycycline (MMP inhibition), treat underlying disease.

Recurrent corneal erosion

  • Poor adhesion of epithelium to the basement membrane: after trauma (fingernail, paper), epithelial basement membrane dystrophy (map-dot-fingerprint), stromal dystrophies, diabetes.
  • Symptoms: sudden pain on waking.
  • Management: lubricant ointment at night, hypertonic saline, bandage contact lens, oral doxycycline and topical steroids, epithelial debridement, anterior stromal puncture (needle or Nd:YAG), PTK (most effective for diffuse disease).
CO 22. Filamentary keratitis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Filamentary keratitis

  • Strands of degenerated epithelial cells and mucus attached to the cornea at one end; painful (foreign body sensation, blinking pain).

Causes

  • Aqueous-deficient dry eye (Sjögren), SLK, prolonged patching, after surgery, neurotrophic keratitis, ptosis, brainstem injury, HZO, medications (antihistamines).

Management

  • Remove filaments with forceps after anaesthesia, treat underlying cause, preservative-free lubricants, hypertonic saline, mucolytics (acetylcysteine 5–10%), bandage contact lens, punctal plugs, anti-inflammatory drops.
CO 23. Meibomian gland dysfunction — newer diagnostics and treatment (thermal pulsation, IPL). · outline

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD)

  • Chronic, diffuse abnormality of meibomian glands (terminal duct obstruction, change in secretion quality) causing evaporative dry eye and lid inflammation; commonest cause of dry eye.

Newer diagnostics

  • Meibography (infrared imaging of gland dropout), interferometry (lipid layer thickness: LipiView), non-invasive TBUT, tear osmolarity, MMP-9 testing, expressibility and meibum quality grading (TFOS MGD workshop).

Treatment

  • Lid hygiene and warm compresses, lid massage, omega-3 (limited evidence), lipid-containing lubricants, perfluorohexyloctane, topical azithromycin, oral doxycycline or azithromycin, steroids for flare-ups, demodex treatment (lotilaner 0.25%, tea tree oil).
  • Thermal pulsation (LipiFlow: heat and pressure to the inner lids), intense pulsed light (IPL: reduces inflammation and telangiectasia; often with meibomian gland expression), meibomian gland probing, microblepharoexfoliation, low-level light therapy.
CO 24. Simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET), CLET and Boston ocular surface prosthesis. · outline

Simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET)

  • Developed by Sangwan (LV Prasad, 2012): small limbal biopsy (2 × 2 mm) from the healthy eye cut into 8–10 pieces, placed on amniotic membrane over the affected cornea with fibrin glue; cells expand in vivo.
  • Indications: unilateral LSCD (chemical burns); success about 75–80%; cheap, no laboratory needed.

Cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation (CLET)

  • Limbal cells expanded in the laboratory on amniotic membrane (2–3 weeks) and transplanted; autologous or allogeneic; needs cGMP facilities; Holoclar approved in Europe.

Others

  • Conjunctival limbal autograft (CLAU), keratolimbal allograft (KLAL), COMET (cultivated oral mucosal epithelium) for bilateral LSCD with systemic immunosuppression.

Boston ocular surface prosthesis (BOSP / PROSE)

  • Fluid-filled gas-permeable scleral lens vaulting over the cornea; for severe ocular surface disease (SJS, GVHD, neurotrophic keratopathy, exposure, irregular corneas); relieves pain, heals PEDs, improves vision.
CO 25. Neurotrophic keratopathy — cenegermin and corneal neurotisation. · outline

Neurotrophic keratopathy treatments

  • Cenegermin (recombinant human nerve growth factor 0.002%): 6 times daily for 8 weeks; promotes epithelial healing in stages 2 and 3 (REPARO and NGF0214 trials: about 70% healing); expensive.

Corneal neurotisation

  • Transfer of healthy nerves to the anaesthetic cornea: direct (supraorbital or supratrochlear nerve tunnelled to the limbus) or indirect (sural nerve graft coapted to a donor nerve); sensation returns in 6–12 months; restores trophic support.
  • Indications: unilateral or bilateral neurotrophic keratopathy with an intact donor nerve (trigeminal palsy after surgery, congenital).

Other options

  • Autologous serum, amniotic membrane, insulin drops, tarsorrhaphy, bandage contact lens, scleral lenses; avoid toxic drops.

Revise from: SJS, Ocular Cicatricial Pemphigoid and Neurotrophic Keratopathy

CO 91. Ocular graft-versus-host disease. NEW · outline

Ocular graft-versus-host disease (oGVHD)

  • Immune attack by donor T cells on host tissues after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation; eye involved in 40–60% of chronic GVHD.

Features

  • Severe aqueous-deficient and evaporative dry eye (lacrimal gland fibrosis, MGD), conjunctival inflammation, pseudomembranes (acute: poor prognosis), cicatricial conjunctivitis, superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis-like changes, filamentary keratitis, persistent epithelial defects, corneal melt and perforation, cataract (steroids, radiation), infections.

Diagnosis

  • NIH consensus criteria and International Chronic Ocular GVHD Consensus Group criteria (Schirmer, OSDI, corneal staining, conjunctival injection).

Management

  • Preservative-free lubricants, punctal plugs or cautery, topical cyclosporine or tacrolimus, short courses of topical steroids, autologous serum (or allogeneic cord blood serum), scleral lenses (PROSE), moisture goggles, treatment of MGD (doxycycline), amniotic membrane for epithelial defects, tarsorrhaphy, systemic immunosuppression (ruxolitinib, belumosudil) with the transplant team; screen before and regularly after transplantation.

Revise from: SJS, Ocular Cicatricial Pemphigoid and Neurotrophic Keratopathy

Conjunctiva

CO 26. Classification of conjunctivitis; discuss the intricacies in its diagnosis and best management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Infective: bacterial (acute, hyperacute gonococcal, chlamydial: trachoma and inclusion), viral (adenovirus, HSV, molluscum), fungal, parasitic.
  • Allergic: seasonal, perennial, VKC, AKC, giant papillary conjunctivitis.
  • Others: chemical and toxic, cicatricial (SJS, pemphigoid), associated with systemic disease, neonatal.

Intricacies in diagnosis

  • Discharge type (purulent, mucopurulent, watery, ropy), papillae vs follicles, membranes, preauricular nodes, itching, duration and laterality; distinguish from keratitis, uveitis and angle closure (red flags: pain, reduced vision, photophobia).
  • Swabs and cultures in hyperacute and neonatal cases, chronic follicular conjunctivitis (chlamydia PCR), membranous cases.

Management

  • Bacterial: topical antibiotics; gonococcal: systemic ceftriaxone; chlamydial: oral azithromycin.
  • Viral: supportive, hygiene; allergic: mast cell stabilisers and antihistamines, cyclosporine for VKC; avoid indiscriminate steroids.
CO 27. Viral conjunctivitis and epidemic keratoconjunctivitis — features, management and sequelae. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Adenoviral conjunctivitis

  • Pharyngoconjunctival fever (types 3, 7) and epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (types 8, 19, 37).

Features

  • Acute watery red eye, follicles, preauricular lymph node, subconjunctival haemorrhages, pseudomembranes; second eye often involved; highly contagious for about 2 weeks.
  • Keratitis: punctate epithelial keratitis, then subepithelial infiltrates (immune, 1–2 weeks later).

Management

  • Hygiene, isolation from school or work, cold compresses, lubricants; peel membranes; povidone-iodine 0.6% shortens course in some studies.
  • Topical steroids only for membranes or visually significant infiltrates (with taper; may prolong viral shedding); cyclosporine or tacrolimus for persistent infiltrates.

Sequelae

  • Subepithelial opacities (glare, reduced vision for months), symblepharon after membranes, dry eye.

Acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis

  • Highly contagious viral conjunctivitis caused by enterovirus 70 and coxsackievirus A24 variant; epidemics in tropical countries (“Apollo conjunctivitis”).

Features

  • Short incubation (1–2 days), sudden painful red eye, subconjunctival haemorrhages (petechial to confluent), follicles, watery discharge, lid oedema, pre-auricular nodes; rarely radiculomyelopathy (enterovirus 70).

Management

  • Self-limiting in 5–7 days; supportive (cold compresses, lubricants); avoid steroids; hygiene to prevent spread (hand washing, separate towels, avoid school or work); disinfect clinic equipment.

CO 29. Trachoma — WHO grading, laboratory diagnosis, complications and the SAFE strategy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Trachoma

  • Chlamydia trachomatis serovars A, B, Ba, C; spread by hands, flies, fomites; poor hygiene and water scarcity; leading infectious cause of blindness.

  • TF follicular inflammation (5 or more follicles in upper tarsus), TI intense inflammation, TS trachomatous scarring, TT trichiasis, CO corneal opacity.

  • Herbert pits, Arlt line (tarsal scarring), entropion and trichiasis, corneal scarring, pannus, dry eye, dacryocystitis.

CO 30. Allergic conjunctivitis — classification; vernal and atopic keratoconjunctivitis: pathophysiology and treatment rationale. DNB / UNIV · outline

Classification of allergic conjunctivitis

  • Seasonal and perennial allergic conjunctivitis (IgE, type I), vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC), atopic keratoconjunctivitis (AKC), giant papillary conjunctivitis (mechanical), contact allergy (type IV).

VKC

  • Boys, warm climates, recurrent seasonal; palpebral (cobblestone papillae), limbal (Horner–Trantas dots), mixed; shield ulcer, plaque, keratoconus, steroid-induced glaucoma.
  • Pathophysiology: Th2-driven (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), mast cells and eosinophils, type I and IV hypersensitivity.

CO 31. Ophthalmia neonatorum — causes and prophylaxis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ophthalmia neonatorum

  • Conjunctivitis in the first 4 weeks of life.

Causes and timing

  • Chemical (silver nitrate: first day), gonococcal (2–4 days: hyperacute purulent; corneal perforation risk), chlamydial (5–14 days: commonest; pneumonitis), herpes simplex (5–14 days: vesicles, keratitis), other bacteria (Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Haemophilus).

Investigations

  • Gram stain (intracellular Gram-negative diplococci), culture, PCR for chlamydia and HSV.

Treatment

  • Gonococcal: IV or IM ceftriaxone (single dose), saline irrigation; treat parents.
  • Chlamydial: oral erythromycin for 14 days (systemic to prevent pneumonitis).
  • HSV: IV acyclovir with topical antiviral.

Prophylaxis

  • Antenatal screening and treatment of mothers, clean delivery, prophylactic povidone-iodine 2.5% or erythromycin 0.5% ointment or tetracycline 1% at birth (Credé method with silver nitrate is historical).
CO 32. Phlyctenular keratoconjunctivitis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Phlyctenular keratoconjunctivitis

  • Delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity to microbial antigens: Staphylococcus aureus (commonest now), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (in endemic areas), others (Candida, Chlamydia, helminths).

Features

  • Children and young adults; photophobia, lacrimation; small pinkish-white nodule at or near the limbus with surrounding hyperaemia; corneal phlycten can migrate (fascicular ulcer with leash of vessels).

Investigations

  • Mantoux, chest X-ray (TB), check for blepharitis, nutrition.

Management

  • Topical steroids (rapid response), treat staphylococcal blepharitis (lid hygiene, antibiotics, oral doxycycline or azithromycin in children), ATT if TB found, nutrition.
CO 33. Superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis; giant papillary conjunctivitis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis (SLK)

  • Middle-aged women, thyroid disease association; mechanical friction between upper lid and superior bulbar conjunctiva (redundant conjunctiva).
  • Features: superior bulbar injection, papillae on upper tarsus, punctate staining (rose bengal), filaments.
  • Management: lubricants, silver nitrate 0.5% (not sticks), bandage contact lens, cyclosporine, supratarsal steroid, botulinum, thermal cautery or conjunctival resection; check thyroid.

Giant papillary conjunctivitis (GPC)

  • Mechanical and immune response to contact lenses, ocular prostheses, exposed sutures, filtering blebs.
  • Features: itching, mucus, lens intolerance, giant papillae (over 1 mm) on upper tarsus.
  • Management: remove the stimulus temporarily, lens hygiene and daily disposable lenses, mast cell stabilisers, short steroids, polish prosthesis, remove sutures.
CO 34. Follicles vs papillae; membranous and pseudomembranous conjunctivitis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Follicles

  • Lymphoid aggregates with germinal centres; round, avascular centres with vessels around; seen in viral (adenovirus, HSV), chlamydial, molluscum and toxic conjunctivitis (drugs such as brimonidine, atropine), and normal in children (inferior fornix).

Papillae

  • Vascular cores with inflammatory infiltrate; mosaic of red dots with central vessels; non-specific; bacterial, allergic (giant papillae in VKC, GPC), contact lens, floppy eyelid syndrome.

Membranous vs pseudomembranous

  • Pseudomembrane: coagulated exudate on the surface, peels easily without bleeding (adenovirus, gonococcus, SJS, ligneous).
  • True membrane: involves the epithelium, bleeds on removal (diphtheria, beta-haemolytic Streptococcus, severe adenovirus).
  • Management: peel membranes, topical steroids for adenoviral membranes, antibiotics (systemic for diphtheria), prevent symblepharon.
CO 35. Pigmented conjunctival lesions. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pigmented conjunctival lesions

  • Benign: conjunctival naevus (cystic spaces, juxtalimbal, children and young adults), complexion-associated melanosis (racial melanosis: bilateral, perilimbal), ocular melanocytosis (episcleral and scleral slate-grey; risk of uveal melanoma), secondary pigmentation (adrenochrome deposits, mascara, alkaptonuria, argyrosis).
  • Premalignant: primary acquired melanosis (PAM; with or without atypia; now conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial neoplasia, C-MIN), unilateral, middle-aged whites.
  • Malignant: conjunctival melanoma (from PAM with atypia, naevi or de novo).

Evaluation

  • Photograph, slit lamp, AS-OCT (cysts in naevi), excision biopsy for suspicious features (growth, feeder vessels, cornea involvement, nodularity, non-bulbar location).

Management

  • Observation for naevi; excision with no-touch technique, alcohol keratoepitheliectomy and cryotherapy to margins, topical MMC or interferon for PAM with atypia and melanoma; sentinel node biopsy for melanoma.
CO 36. Pterygium — histopathology, indications and techniques of surgery (including PERFECT), and prevention of recurrence (recurrent pterygium); pseudopterygium. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Histopathology

  • Elastotic degeneration of subconjunctival collagen (actinic, UV-related), fibrovascular proliferation; head, neck and body; Stocker line, Fuchs flecks.

Indications for surgery

  • Encroachment on the visual axis, induced astigmatism, restricted motility, recurrent inflammation, cosmesis, suspicion of neoplasia.

Techniques

  • Bare sclera (recurrence up to 80%: obsolete alone), conjunctival autograft with fibrin glue or sutures (gold standard, recurrence about 5–10%), conjunctival-limbal autograft, amniotic membrane graft, adjunct MMC, PERFECT (pterygium extended removal followed by extended conjunctival transplant) for very low recurrence.

Recurrent pterygium

  • More aggressive; wide excision with conjunctival-limbal autograft, MMC, symblepharon release; anti-VEGF (limited evidence).

Pseudopterygium

  • Conjunctival adhesion to a previous corneal insult (burn, ulcer); probe passes under it; any meridian; non-progressive.

CO 37. Ocular surface squamous neoplasia and premalignant epibulbar lesions — aetiopathogenesis, histopathology and antimetabolite therapy; dermolipoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiopathogenesis

  • UV exposure, HPV, HIV, xeroderma pigmentosum, smoking, older age; usually at the interpalpebral limbus.

Spectrum and histopathology

  • Conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia (mild, moderate, severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ) → invasive squamous cell carcinoma (breaches basement membrane).

Dermolipoma

  • Choristoma at the temporal fornix (yellowish, soft); associated with Goldenhar syndrome; excise only the anterior part conservatively.

CO 38. Corneal ulcer — tabular comparison of bacterial, fungal and viral keratitis; laboratory diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Comparison

  • Bacterial: acute, painful, well-defined suppurative infiltrate, purulent discharge; risk factors contact lens, trauma, surface disease.
  • Fungal: trauma with vegetable matter, agricultural workers; dry, raised, feathery margins, satellite lesions, endothelial plaque, hypopyon (often immobile); slower course.
  • Viral (HSV): recurrent, dendritic epithelial lesion, reduced corneal sensation, stromal or disciform forms.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Corneal scraping (No. 15 blade or spatula): Gram, KOH and calcofluor white, Giemsa; culture on blood, chocolate and Sabouraud agar, non-nutrient agar (Acanthamoeba).
  • PCR, confocal microscopy, corneal biopsy for deep lesions.

Management

  • Bacterial: fortified cefazolin 5% and tobramycin 1.3% or a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone hourly; cycloplegic; adjunctive steroid only after control (SCUT).
  • Fungal: natamycin 5% (Fusarium, filamentous; MUTT), voriconazole 1% (Aspergillus), intrastromal or intracameral injections in deep disease, therapeutic keratoplasty.
  • Viral: topical ganciclovir or acyclovir for epithelial disease, steroids with antiviral cover for stromal disease.

CO 39. Discuss the clinical features, diagnosis and current concepts in the management of fungal keratitis (keratomycosis). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Clinical features

  • Trauma with vegetable matter, prior steroid use; slowly progressive, raised dry infiltrate, feathery margins, satellite lesions, immune ring, endothelial plaque, thick hypopyon; Fusarium, Aspergillus, Candida (in compromised eyes).

Diagnosis

  • KOH and calcofluor white smears (septate hyphae), Sabouraud culture, confocal microscopy, PCR; biopsy for deep lesions.

Current concepts in management

  • Natamycin 5% hourly first line for filamentous keratitis (MUTT I: better than voriconazole, especially Fusarium); voriconazole 1% for Aspergillus and deep lesions.
  • MUTT II: oral voriconazole added no benefit overall. Intrastromal voriconazole (MALIN) not helpful as primary therapy; useful in recalcitrant ulcers.
  • Debridement, avoid steroids, cycloplegics; corneal cross-linking (limited evidence).
  • Therapeutic keratoplasty (with 1 mm clear margin) for non-responding or perforating ulcers.
CO 40. Management of the non-healing / non-responding corneal ulcer. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Reassess the diagnosis

  • Wrong organism (fungal, Acanthamoeba, Nocardia, atypical mycobacteria, mixed), resistant organism, drug toxicity (epithelial toxicity from fortified drops), non-compliance.

Look for host factors

  • Dry eye, exposure, neurotrophic cornea, lid problems (trichiasis, entropion), diabetes, immunosuppression, steroid use.

Steps

  • Stop drugs for 24–48 hours if possible and re-scrape; corneal biopsy; confocal microscopy; PCR.
  • Reduce toxicity (fewer drugs), lubricate, bandage contact lens, tarsorrhaphy, amniotic membrane, autologous serum.
  • Deep or progressive: intrastromal and intracameral drugs, cyanoacrylate glue with bandage lens for small perforations, therapeutic keratoplasty.

CO 41. Perforated corneal ulcer and descemetocele — management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Descemetocele and perforation

  • Descemetocele: stromal loss with only Descemet membrane remaining (transparent bulging); impending perforation.
  • Perforation: Seidel positive, shallow or flat AC, iris prolapse, hypotony.

Management by size and cause

  • Treat the cause (infection, neurotrophic, autoimmune melt), reduce collagenolysis (doxycycline, vitamin C), aqueous suppressants, avoid pressure on the eye, shield.
  • Small (up to 2–3 mm): cyanoacrylate glue with bandage contact lens; fibrin glue for small sterile perforations.
  • Moderate: multilayer amniotic membrane, patch graft (corneal or scleral), Tenon patch graft.
  • Large: therapeutic keratoplasty (tectonic), conjunctival flap for poor visual prognosis.
  • Infective perforations: therapeutic PK with continued antimicrobials.

CO 42. Acanthamoeba keratitis — features, laboratory diagnosis, prevention and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Features

  • Contact lens wear with tap or swimming water; pain out of proportion to signs; epithelial irregularity and pseudodendrites, radial keratoneuritis, ring infiltrate; often misdiagnosed as HSV or fungal.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Scraping: calcofluor white, Giemsa, KOH; culture on non-nutrient agar with E. coli overlay; PCR; in vivo confocal microscopy (double-walled cysts, trophozoites).

  • No water exposure with lenses (showering, swimming), proper solutions, daily disposables, case care.

CO 43. Viral keratitis, with special reference to herpes simplex (dendritic, disciform, stromal and recurrent) keratitis; Herpetic Eye Disease Study recommendations. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Herpes simplex keratitis

  • Primary infection then latency in the trigeminal ganglion; recurrences triggered by stress, fever, UV, immunosuppression.

Forms

  • Epithelial: dendritic (terminal bulbs, stains with fluorescein and rose bengal), geographic.
  • Stromal: necrotising (viral invasion) and immune (non-necrotising, interstitial).
  • Endotheliitis: disciform (central oedema, KPs, no stromal infiltrate).
  • Neurotrophic keratopathy (metaherpetic ulcer).

Treatment

  • Epithelial: topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel 5 times daily or acyclovir 3% ointment; debridement.
  • Stromal and disciform: topical steroids with antiviral cover.

Herpetic Eye Disease Study

  • Topical steroids with antiviral cover reduce stromal keratitis inflammation and duration.
  • Oral acyclovir adds no benefit in stromal keratitis, but 400 mg twice daily for 1 year halves recurrences (prophylaxis after recurrent disease and keratoplasty).
  • Oral acyclovir did not prevent stromal keratitis after epithelial disease.

CO 44. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus — clinical features and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO)

  • Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve; risk: age, immunosuppression (HIV test in young people).

Clinical features

  • Prodrome, vesicular rash in V1 distribution; Hutchinson sign (tip of nose: nasociliary nerve) predicts ocular involvement.

CO 45. Interstitial keratitis; tubercular lesions of the cornea. DNB / UNIV · outline

Interstitial keratitis (IK)

  • Non-suppurative inflammation of the corneal stroma with vascularisation, without primary epithelial involvement.

Causes

  • Congenital syphilis (bilateral, 5–20 years; Hutchinson triad), acquired syphilis, TB, leprosy, HSV and HZV, Cogan syndrome (vestibulo-auditory symptoms), Lyme, onchocerciasis, EBV.

Features

  • Active: pain, photophobia, stromal infiltration, salmon patch (deep vascularisation), uveitis.
  • Inactive: ghost vessels, deep stromal scars, thinning.

Management

  • Topical steroids and cycloplegics; treat cause (penicillin for syphilis, ATT for TB); systemic steroids in Cogan syndrome; keratoplasty for scars.

Tubercular corneal lesions

  • Phlyctenular keratoconjunctivitis, interstitial keratitis (sectoral, unilateral), sclerokeratitis, corneal ulcers (rare); treat with ATT and steroids.
CO 46. Atypical keratitis — Nocardia and atypical mycobacteria; workup. DNB / UNIV · outline

Nocardia keratitis

  • Gram-positive branching filaments, weakly acid-fast (modified Ziehl–Neelsen); after trauma with soil; patchy, wreath-like infiltrates, pin-head lesions.
  • Treatment: amikacin 2.5% (drug of choice), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; avoid steroids early.

Atypical (non-tuberculous) mycobacteria

  • M. chelonae, M. fortuitum, M. abscessus; after LASIK (flap interface), trauma, contact lenses; indolent, cracked windshield or ground-glass infiltrates, satellite lesions.

  • Corneal scraping: Gram, Ziehl–Neelsen (1% and 20% H₂SO₄), KOH, calcofluor white, culture on Löwenstein–Jensen and blood agar (incubate for weeks); PCR; corneal biopsy for deep lesions; confocal microscopy.

CO 47. Rosacea keratitis and other superficial keratitis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Rosacea keratitis

  • Ocular rosacea: MGD, lid telangiectasia, recurrent chalazia, conjunctival hyperaemia; skin rosacea (flushing, papules, rhinophyma) may be mild or absent; children too.
  • Cornea: inferior punctate keratopathy, marginal infiltrates, peripheral vascularisation, spade-shaped infiltrates, thinning and perforation (rare).

CO 48. Antiviral agents in ophthalmology — classification, mechanism and side effects. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Nucleoside analogues: acyclovir, valacyclovir, ganciclovir, valganciclovir, famciclovir, trifluridine; nucleotide analogue: cidofovir; pyrophosphate analogue: foscarnet.

Mechanism

  • Acyclovir and ganciclovir are phosphorylated by viral thymidine kinase (UL97 kinase for ganciclovir in CMV) and inhibit viral DNA polymerase; foscarnet inhibits polymerase directly.

Uses

  • HSV keratitis (ganciclovir gel, acyclovir ointment, oral acyclovir), herpes zoster ophthalmicus (oral acyclovir 800 mg five times daily, valacyclovir), acute retinal necrosis (IV acyclovir then oral valacyclovir), CMV retinitis (valganciclovir, intravitreal ganciclovir or foscarnet).

Side effects

  • Topical: epithelial toxicity (trifluridine), punctal stenosis.
  • Systemic: renal toxicity (acyclovir IV, foscarnet, cidofovir), bone marrow suppression (ganciclovir), hypocalcaemia (foscarnet), uveitis and hypotony (cidofovir).
CO 49. Antifungal drugs in ophthalmology — classification, mechanism and uses. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Polyenes: natamycin, amphotericin B (bind ergosterol, form pores).
  • Azoles: imidazoles (econazole, ketoconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole) and triazoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole): inhibit ergosterol synthesis (14α-demethylase).
  • Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin): inhibit cell wall glucan synthesis.
  • Pyrimidines: flucytosine.

Uses

  • Natamycin 5%: filamentous keratitis (Fusarium). Amphotericin B 0.15%: Candida. Voriconazole 1%: Aspergillus, deep and resistant cases, endophthalmitis (intravitreal 100 µg). Fluconazole: Candida; good penetration orally.

Problems

  • Poor penetration of natamycin, toxicity of amphotericin B, cost and availability; systemic azoles: liver toxicity, drug interactions.
CO 50. Role of steroids in keratitis. · outline

Role of steroids in keratitis

  • Reduce inflammation, scarring and neovascularisation; but delay healing, enhance replication of some organisms (fungi, HSV epithelial disease) and promote melting.

Bacterial keratitis

  • SCUT trial: no overall benefit at 3 months; benefit in non-Nocardia ulcers and in those with poor initial vision when started early (within 2–3 days of antibiotics).

Fungal keratitis

  • Steroids contraindicated (worse outcomes).

Herpetic keratitis

  • HEDS: topical steroids with antiviral cover speed resolution of stromal keratitis; contraindicated in epithelial disease.

Acanthamoeba

  • Steroids only after anti-amoebic therapy is established (for inflammation, scleritis).

Other uses

  • Marginal keratitis, phlyctenulosis, IK, Thygeson, adenoviral subepithelial infiltrates, graft rejection.
CO 51. Corneal cross-linking (PACK-CXL) and newer adjuncts in infective keratitis. · outline

PACK-CXL (photoactivated chromophore for keratitis cross-linking)

  • Riboflavin with UVA light generates free radicals that kill micro-organisms, cross-link collagen and resist enzymatic digestion (melting).
  • Evidence: helps in early superficial bacterial keratitis; fungal keratitis results mixed (Prajna: no benefit as adjunct in deep fungal ulcers); Acanthamoeba uncertain; accelerated protocols at the slit lamp.
  • Limitations: depth of effect about 250–300 µm, endothelial toxicity in thin corneas, cannot replace antimicrobials.

Newer adjuncts

  • Rose bengal photodynamic antimicrobial therapy (RB-PDAT: green light; promising in fungal and Acanthamoeba keratitis), intrastromal voriconazole and amphotericin injections, natamycin (MUTT I: superior to voriconazole for Fusarium), oral voriconazole not beneficial (MUTT II), corneal collagen shields, antimicrobial peptides, early therapeutic keratoplasty.

Microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis

CO 52. Peripheral ulcerative keratitis and Mooren's ulcer — differential diagnosis, workup and surgical treatment of marginal corneal ulcer; keratitis in rheumatoid arthritis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK)

  • Crescentic peripheral stromal thinning with epithelial defect and infiltrate, often with scleritis; associated with rheumatoid arthritis, GPA, polyarteritis nodosa, SLE, relapsing polychondritis; can be the first sign of fatal vasculitis.

  • Idiopathic, painful, starts at the limbus with an overhanging edge, spreads circumferentially then centrally; no scleritis or systemic disease; unilateral (older) or bilateral aggressive (young men, helminthiasis link).

  • Marginal (staphylococcal) keratitis, Terrien degeneration (painless), infectious ulcer, Mooren’s vs PUK.

  • Full blood count, ESR, CRP, RF, anti-CCP, ANCA, ANA, urine, chest X-ray, hepatitis B and C.

Treatment

  • Lubricants, steroids, collagenase inhibitors (doxycycline), systemic immunosuppression (cyclophosphamide, rituximab for vasculitis); avoid topical steroids alone in RA melting.
  • Surgical: conjunctival resection (Mooren’s), cyanoacrylate glue, amniotic membrane, lamellar or tectonic patch grafts.

CO 53. Peripheral corneal thinning disorders and peripheral degenerations (including Terrien) — management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Peripheral thinning disorders

CO 54. Corneal neovascularisation — associated conditions, mechanisms and treatment. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneal neovascularisation

  • Growth of vessels from the limbus into the normally avascular cornea.

Associated conditions

  • Hypoxia (contact lenses), infection (HSV, trachoma, bacterial, fungal), inflammation (graft rejection, chemical burns, SJS, OCP), LSCD, degeneration, trauma, interstitial keratitis (syphilis, TB).

Mechanism

  • Imbalance of angiogenic (VEGF, bFGF, MMPs) and anti-angiogenic factors (soluble VEGF receptor-1, angiostatin, pigment epithelium-derived factor); limbal barrier failure.

Consequences

  • Lipid keratopathy, scarring, reduced vision, loss of immune privilege (graft rejection risk).

Treatment

  • Treat the cause (stop contact lens wear, control inflammation), topical steroids, anti-VEGF (topical or subconjunctival bevacizumab), fine-needle diathermy and argon laser for feeder vessels, photodynamic therapy, limbal stem cell transplantation for LSCD.

CO 55. Corneal dystrophies — latest classification (IC3D); describe the stromal dystrophies with histopathology and stains. HIGH-YIELD · outline

IC3D classification (2015, revised 2024)

  • Anatomical: epithelial and subepithelial, epithelial–stromal TGFBI, stromal, endothelial; categories based on genetic evidence.

Stromal dystrophies

  • Macular: autosomal recessive, CHST6; diffuse haze to the limbus, poorly defined opacities; glycosaminoglycans stain with Alcian blue and colloidal iron.
  • Granular type 1 (TGFBI): crumb-like opacities with clear spaces; hyaline deposits, Masson trichrome red.
  • Lattice type 1 (TGFBI): branching refractile lines; amyloid, Congo red with apple-green birefringence under polarised light.

  • Schnyder (UBIAD1): crystalline cholesterol, Oil red O; central crystals and arcus.
  • Congenital stromal and fleck dystrophies.

  • PTK for superficial disease, DALK or penetrating keratoplasty; recurrences in grafts (granular, lattice).

CO 56. Fuchs endothelial dystrophy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Fuchs endothelial dystrophy

  • Bilateral, progressive endothelial disease; women over 50; TCF4 trinucleotide repeat expansion (commonest), COL8A2 (early onset).

Features

  • Central guttae (excrescences of Descemet: beaten-metal appearance), endothelial loss, stromal then epithelial oedema (morning blurring, bullae, pain), subepithelial fibrosis.
  • Krachmer grading of guttae; modified Krachmer and Scheimpflug tomography signs (loss of isopachs, posterior displacement) predict oedema.

Investigations

  • Pachymetry (central thickness), specular microscopy (dark spots, low density), Scheimpflug tomography.

Management

  • Early: hypertonic saline, hair dryer, treat IOP; rho-kinase inhibitors.
  • Definitive: DMEK (best visual results), DSAEK; DSO with ripasudil for central guttae with good peripheral endothelium; triple procedure (cataract surgery with DMEK) when combined; careful cataract surgery with endothelial protection.

Classification of degenerations

  • Pathological: band keratopathy, spheroidal (climatic droplet) degeneration, Salzmann nodular degeneration, lipid keratopathy, amyloid, Terrien, pellucid.

CO 58. Spheroidal degeneration. · outline

Spheroidal degeneration (climatic droplet keratopathy, Labrador keratopathy)

  • Amber-coloured, spherical subepithelial deposits in the interpalpebral cornea and conjunctiva; males, outdoor workers; UV exposure, wind, dryness, sand.

Pathology

  • Extracellular protein deposits (elastotic degeneration from UV-altered plasma proteins) in Bowman layer and anterior stroma.

Management

  • Protective sunglasses; lubricants; superficial keratectomy or PTK for visual obstruction; lamellar keratoplasty for advanced cases.
CO 59. Salzmann nodular degeneration. · outline

Salzmann nodular degeneration

  • Raised grey-white or bluish nodules in the mid-periphery of the cornea, often at the edge of a pannus; middle-aged women.

Causes

  • Chronic inflammation: MGD, dry eye, contact lens wear, trachoma, phlyctenulosis, VKC, previous keratitis; idiopathic.

Pathology

  • Hyaline and fibrillar material replacing Bowman layer, with thin overlying epithelium.

Effects

  • Irregular astigmatism, reduced vision, foreign body sensation, recurrent erosions, topography changes affecting biometry.

Management

  • Treat underlying inflammation, lubricants; superficial keratectomy (nodules peel off easily) with MMC or PTK; recurrence possible; delay biometry until topography stabilises.
CO 60. Band-shaped keratopathy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Band-shaped keratopathy

  • Calcium hydroxyapatite deposition in Bowman’s layer and superficial stroma in the interpalpebral zone, with a clear peripheral zone and “Swiss-cheese” holes.

Causes

  • Chronic uveitis (JIA), phthisis, silicone oil, glaucoma; hypercalcaemia, renal failure, vitamin D excess; idiopathic; mercury preservatives.

Management

  • Treat the cause; superficial keratectomy then chelation with EDTA 3% for 15–30 minutes; bandage contact lens or amniotic membrane; PTK for residual opacity.
CO 61. Keratoconus — causes of ectatic cornea, topographic features and grading, and recent management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of ectatic cornea

  • Keratoconus (genetic and environmental: eye rubbing, atopy, Down syndrome, connective tissue disorders), pellucid marginal degeneration, keratoglobus, post-LASIK ectasia.

Topographic features

  • Inferior steepening, asymmetric bowtie with skewed radial axes, central K over 47 D, I–S value over 1.4, thinnest point displaced; posterior elevation and pachymetry on tomography (Belin–Ambrosio).

Grading

  • Amsler–Krumeich, ABCD (Belin), Rabinowitz criteria, KISA%.

Signs

  • Munson sign, Rizzuti sign, Fleischer ring, Vogt striae, scissoring reflex, acute hydrops.

Recent management

  • Stop eye rubbing; spectacles, RGP, hybrid and scleral lenses.
  • Corneal collagen cross-linking (Dresden, accelerated, epi-on) to halt progression.
  • Intrastromal corneal ring segments, topography-guided PRK with CXL, toric phakic IOL.
  • DALK (preferred over PK in advanced disease); acute hydrops: patching, hypertonic saline, intracameral gas.

CO 62. Intrastromal corneal ring segments. DNB / UNIV · outline

Intrastromal corneal ring segments (ICRS)

  • PMMA arc-shaped segments (Intacs, Ferrara, Keraring) inserted into femtosecond laser or mechanical tunnels at about 70–80% stromal depth; flatten the central cornea (arc-shortening effect) and regularise ectatic corneas.

Indications

  • Keratoconus (contact lens intolerant, clear central cornea, thickness at least 400–450 µm at the tunnel), post-LASIK ectasia, pellucid marginal degeneration, low myopia (historical).

Outcomes

  • Reduced keratometry and astigmatism, better uncorrected and spectacle vision, better contact lens tolerance; combined with CXL to halt progression.

Complications

  • Segment extrusion, migration, infection, tunnel deposits, corneal melting, haloes, under or over-correction; removable.

CO 63. Posterior keratoconus. · outline

Posterior keratoconus

  • Rare, usually unilateral, non-progressive congenital anomaly: localised (central or paracentral) or generalised increased curvature of the posterior corneal surface with stromal thinning; anterior surface often normal.

Features

  • Posterior concavity on slit lamp and Scheimpflug, overlying stromal haze, Descemet and endothelium usually intact; associated with Peters anomaly and other anterior segment dysgenesis.

Management

  • Usually no treatment; amblyopia management in children, spectacles or rigid lenses for astigmatism; keratoplasty for dense central opacity.
CO 64. Corneal opacity and its congenital variants — differential diagnosis of congenital corneal opacities; sclerocornea. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Congenital corneal opacities: STUMPED

  • Sclerocornea, Tears in Descemet’s (congenital glaucoma, birth trauma), Ulcers (infection, exposure), Metabolic (mucopolysaccharidoses, cystinosis), Peters anomaly, Endothelial dystrophy (CHED, PPCD), Dermoid.

Sclerocornea

  • Scleral-like tissue extends onto the cornea, peripheral or total; non-progressive; associated with cornea plana.

Evaluation

  • Examination under anaesthesia (IOP, corneal diameter, pachymetry), UBM, B-scan, systemic and genetic workup.

Management

  • Glaucoma control, amblyopia, optical iridectomy, early keratoplasty (high failure in infants), keratoprosthesis in selected cases.
CO 65. Limbal dermoid; differential diagnosis of a limbal nodule. · outline

Limbal dermoid

  • Choristoma (skin elements: keratinised epithelium, hair, sebaceous glands) at the inferotemporal limbus; smooth, whitish-yellow, may have hair; can extend into stroma.
  • Associations: Goldenhar syndrome (oculo-auriculo-vertebral: preauricular tags, vertebral anomalies), lid coloboma.
  • Problems: astigmatism and amblyopia, cosmesis, irritation (hairs), dellen.
  • Management: refraction and amblyopia therapy; excision with lamellar keratoplasty or sclerokeratoplasty for large lesions (do not shave too deep).

Differential diagnosis of a limbal nodule

  • Inflammatory: phlycten, nodular episcleritis, nodular scleritis, pinguecula (inflamed), granuloma.
  • Neoplastic: OSSN (papilliform or leukoplakic: older people), conjunctival naevus, melanoma, lymphoma (salmon patch), papilloma, pyogenic granuloma.
  • Others: pterygium, epithelial inclusion cyst, foreign body granuloma, VKC limbal nodules, staphyloma, leprosy nodules.
CO 66. Corneal collagen cross-linking — protocols, indications and outcomes. · outline

Protocols

  • Dresden (standard epi-off): epithelium removed (9 mm), riboflavin 0.1% every 2–5 minutes for 30 minutes, UVA 370 nm at 3 mW/cm² for 30 minutes (5.4 J/cm²).
  • Accelerated: higher irradiance for shorter time (9 mW/cm² for 10 minutes; 18 mW/cm² for 5 minutes), pulsed light, oxygen supplementation.
  • Epi-on (transepithelial) and iontophoresis: less pain and infection, less effect.
  • Thin corneas (under 400 µm after epithelial removal): hypo-osmolar riboflavin, contact lens-assisted CXL, sub400 protocol.

Indications

  • Progressive keratoconus (increase in Kmax of 1 D or more, thinning, worsening refraction), post-refractive ectasia, pellucid marginal degeneration; infectious keratitis (PACK-CXL); combined with ICRS, PRK (CXL plus).

Outcomes

  • Halts progression in over 90%; flattening of Kmax by about 1–2 D; demarcation line on OCT at about 300 µm; NICE and Global consensus support CXL in progressive keratoconus.

Complications

  • Pain, haze, infection, sterile infiltrates, endothelial damage (thin corneas), failure; contraindicated in active herpes, severe dry eye, pregnancy (relative).

CO 67. Scleritis — classification (including necrotising and posterior scleritis), systemic associations and management; episcleritis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification (Watson and Hayreh)

  • Episcleritis: simple, nodular.
  • Anterior scleritis: diffuse, nodular, necrotising (with inflammation), necrotising without inflammation (scleromalacia perforans).
  • Posterior scleritis: pain, proptosis, choroidal folds, exudative RD, disc oedema; B-scan “T sign”.

Systemic associations

  • Rheumatoid arthritis, GPA, polyarteritis nodosa, SLE, relapsing polychondritis, IBD; infections (herpes zoster, TB, syphilis).

Management

  • Episcleritis: self-limiting, lubricants, topical steroids or oral NSAIDs.
  • Non-necrotising scleritis: oral NSAIDs, then oral steroids.
  • Necrotising and posterior: high-dose systemic steroids with immunosuppression (cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, rituximab); patch graft for perforation; avoid periocular steroids in necrotising disease.

CO 68. Scleromalacia perforans. DNB / UNIV · outline

Scleromalacia perforans

  • Necrotising scleritis without inflammation; seen in long-standing rheumatoid arthritis, typically elderly women.

Features

  • Painless, white eye; yellowish necrotic scleral patches that thin, revealing the blue-black uvea beneath; perforation is rare unless trauma.

Investigations

  • RF, anti-CCP, ANCA (to exclude vasculitis), ESR, CRP; ultrasound for thinning.

Management

  • Control systemic disease with immunosuppression (methotrexate, rituximab, anti-TNF); protect the eye (shield); scleral patch graft for impending perforation; lubricants; avoid trauma.
CO 69. Staphyloma — anterior, ciliary and posterior. · outline

Staphyloma

  • Ectasia of an outer coat of the eye (cornea or sclera) lined by uveal tissue.

Types

  • Anterior (corneal): ectatic scar with incarcerated iris after perforated ulcer.
  • Intercalary: at the limbus with root of iris.
  • Ciliary: over the ciliary body (2–3 mm behind the limbus): scleritis, trauma, secondary glaucoma.
  • Equatorial: at the exit of vortex veins: scleritis, high myopia, glaucoma.
  • Posterior: at the posterior pole in pathological myopia (Curtin classification), causing myopic maculopathy; also in congenital glaucoma.

Management

  • Treat the cause (IOP control, inflammation); scleral reinforcement with patch grafts for thin areas; keratoplasty or evisceration for anterior staphyloma; cosmetic shells; posterior staphyloma: manage complications (CNV, traction maculopathy).

CO 70. Discuss lamellar keratoplasties (DALK, DSAEK, DMEK) and their role in corneal disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

DALK (deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty)

  • Replaces stroma down to Descemet’s membrane (big-bubble technique, Anwar); for keratoconus, stromal scars and dystrophies with healthy endothelium.
  • Advantages: no endothelial rejection, stronger globe, longer graft survival. Disadvantages: technically demanding, interface haze, conversion to PK on perforation.

DSAEK

  • Posterior stroma with endothelium (microkeratome-cut), inserted through a small incision, air tamponade; easier, faster learning curve.

DMEK

  • Descemet’s membrane with endothelium only: best visual outcome and lowest rejection; harder handling, graft detachment and rebubbling.

Role

  • Endothelial keratoplasty is the standard for Fuchs dystrophy, pseudophakic bullous keratopathy and failed grafts; DALK for anterior disease; PK for full-thickness disease.

CO 71. Recent advances and concepts in corneal grafting, with special reference to high-risk keratoplasty. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Recent advances

  • Lamellar techniques (DALK, DSAEK, DMEK, PDEK), femtosecond-assisted keratoplasty, Descemet stripping only, cultured endothelial cells, bioengineered corneas, keratoprosthesis improvements, eye bank pre-cut and pre-loaded grafts.

High-risk keratoplasty

  • Vascularised cornea (two or more quadrants), previous graft rejection, large or eccentric grafts, active inflammation, ocular surface disease, children, HSV.

Strategies

  • Pre-operative: control inflammation and infection, treat surface and lid disease, anti-VEGF (subconjunctival bevacizumab) or fine-needle diathermy for vessels.
  • Lamellar grafts where possible, HLA matching (limited evidence), systemic immunosuppression (cyclosporine, mycophenolate), intensive topical steroids, oral antivirals for HSV, keratoprosthesis when grafts repeatedly fail.

CO 72. Corneal graft rejection — immunology, features, risk factors and management; failed graft. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Immunology

  • Loss of immune privilege; recipient T cells (type IV) recognise donor HLA antigens; vascularisation and inflammation increase access.

Types and features

  • Epithelial rejection line, subepithelial infiltrates (Krachmer spots), stromal rejection, endothelial rejection (Khodadoust line, KPs, graft oedema): the commonest and most serious.

Risk factors

  • Vascularisation, previous rejection, large or eccentric graft, young age, glaucoma, inflammation, HSV, suture problems.

Management

  • Intensive topical steroids (prednisolone 1% hourly), periocular or oral or IV pulse steroids for endothelial rejection, cycloplegics, systemic immunosuppression in high-risk cases; treat loose sutures.

Failed graft

  • Irreversible oedema: regraft (endothelial keratoplasty if suitable), or keratoprosthesis after multiple failures.

CO 73. Pseudophakic bullous keratopathy — aetiopathogenesis, prevention and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Aetiopathogenesis of PBK

  • Endothelial decompensation after cataract surgery: pre-existing endothelial disease (Fuchs, low count), surgical trauma (high phaco energy, nucleus near the endothelium, hard cataract, long surgery), Descemet detachment, AC IOLs and iris-fixated lenses (chronic contact, UGH syndrome), vitreous touch, raised IOP, TASS, post-operative inflammation.

Prevention

  • Pre-operative specular microscopy and pachymetry, dispersive OVD (soft shell technique), low energy phaco techniques (chop), working in the posterior chamber, careful with hard nuclei (SICS may be safer), avoid AC IOLs, control inflammation and IOP.

Management

  • Medical: hypertonic saline, IOP control, bandage contact lens for bullae, steroids for inflammation.
  • Surgical: endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK, DMEK), PK with IOL exchange when needed; for poor visual potential: anterior stromal puncture, amniotic membrane, PTK, conjunctival flap.
CO 74. Keratoprosthesis — types (Boston, osteo-odonto) and indications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Boston type I (collar button: PMMA front plate and optic, back plate, donor cornea carrier): for repeated graft failure with adequate tear film.
  • Boston type II (through the lid): severe dry eye and keratinised surface (SJS, pemphigoid).
  • Osteo-odonto keratoprosthesis (OOKP): patient’s own tooth root and alveolar bone support the optic; most durable in end-stage dry eye.
  • Others: AlphaCor, LV Prasad (Aurolab) KPro.

Indications

  • Bilateral corneal blindness with multiple failed grafts or a poor prognosis for grafts, with a functioning retina and optic nerve.

Complications

  • Retroprosthetic membrane, glaucoma (major cause of vision loss), endophthalmitis, tissue melt, extrusion, RD.
CO 75. Triple procedure — merits and demerits. DNB / UNIV · outline

Triple procedure

  • Combined penetrating (or endothelial) keratoplasty, cataract extraction (open-sky ECCE or phaco) and IOL implantation in one sitting.

Merits

  • Single surgery and anaesthesia, faster visual rehabilitation, lower cost, avoids endothelial damage from later cataract surgery on the graft, uses the same incision.

Demerits

  • Open-sky surgery with risk of expulsive haemorrhage, posterior capsule rupture and vitreous loss, less accurate IOL power (unknown post-keratoplasty keratometry: refractive surprise), more inflammation, longer surgery.

Current practice

  • Triple DMEK or DSAEK (phaco through closed system) is now preferred for Fuchs with cataract (targets slight myopia for hyperopic shift); sequential surgery when accuracy matters.

CO 76. Management of a shallow anterior chamber after keratoplasty. · outline

Causes of shallow AC after keratoplasty

  • Wound leak (Seidel positive), choroidal detachment (hypotony), pupillary block (no iridectomy, inflammation, air bubble), aqueous misdirection (malignant glaucoma), suprachoroidal haemorrhage.

Evaluation

  • IOP (low with leak or choroidal; high with block, misdirection, haemorrhage), Seidel test, B-scan, UBM, iridectomy patency.

Management

  • Wound leak: pressure patch, bandage contact lens, aqueous suppressants, resuturing.
  • Choroidal detachment: cycloplegics, steroids, drainage if appositional or flat AC with corneal touch.
  • Pupillary block: laser iridotomy, mydriatics, surgical iridectomy.
  • Aqueous misdirection: cycloplegics, aqueous suppressants, YAG hyaloidotomy, vitrectomy.
  • Reform AC early (air or OVD) to prevent PAS and graft endothelial damage.

CO 77. Corneal preservation — short- and intermediate-term methods and media; donor cornea evaluation. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Methods of preservation

  • Short term: moist chamber at 4 °C (up to 48 hours).
  • Intermediate: McCarey–Kaufman medium (up to 4 days); Optisol-GS and Cornisol (dextran and chondroitin sulphate; up to 7–14 days at 4 °C).
  • Long term: organ culture at 31–37 °C (up to about 4 weeks, used in Europe); cryopreservation; glycerol for tectonic use.

Donor evaluation

  • Donor history and serology (HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis); exclusion criteria (rabies, prion disease, septicaemia, leukaemia, retinoblastoma, prior refractive surgery for PK).
  • Slit-lamp examination of the corneoscleral rim, specular microscopy (endothelial cell density above about 2000 cells/mm² for PK), death-to-preservation time.

Planning an eye bank

CO 79. Conductive keratoplasty. DNB / UNIV · outline

Conductive keratoplasty (CK)

  • Radiofrequency energy delivered through a fine probe into the peripheral corneal stroma (8, 16 or 24 spots in rings at 6, 7 and 8 mm) heats collagen to shrink it, creating a band that steepens the central cornea.

Indications (historical)

  • Low hyperopia (+0.75 to +3.00 D) and presbyopia (near vision in monovision); post-LASIK or cataract surgery hyperopic overcorrection.

Limitations

  • Regression over time, induced astigmatism, not suitable for high hyperopia; largely abandoned; compared with laser thermal keratoplasty (LTK) which used holmium:YAG laser.
CO 80. Descemet stripping only (DSO) and rho-kinase inhibitors for Fuchs endothelial dystrophy. · outline

Descemet stripping only (DSO, also DWEK)

  • Removal of a central 4 mm disc of Descemet with guttae without grafting; healthy peripheral endothelial cells migrate to cover the area; clearing in 2–6 months.

Indications

  • Fuchs dystrophy with central guttae, healthy peripheral endothelium (cell density adequate), no peripheral guttae; often combined with cataract surgery.

Rho-kinase inhibitors

  • Ripasudil and netarsudil promote endothelial cell proliferation and migration; speed clearing after DSO and improve outcomes; cultured endothelial cells with ROCK inhibitor injection (Kinoshita) as cell therapy.

Advantages and limitations

  • No donor tissue (no rejection, no long-term steroids), lower cost; but slow and uncertain recovery, need for rescue DMEK in some, limited evidence.

CO 81. Critically discuss recent advances in keratorefractive procedures, with a note on patient selection. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Recent advances

  • Wavefront-optimised, wavefront-guided and topography-guided LASIK; femtosecond flaps; SMILE (flapless, lenticule extraction); trans-PRK; corneal inlays; phakic IOLs (ICL, EVO) for high myopia; refractive lens exchange; presbyopia correction.

Patient selection

CO 82. Work-up for refractive surgery and absolute contraindications to laser refractive surgery. DNB / UNIV · outline

Work-up for refractive surgery

  • History: stability of refraction (change under 0.5 D in a year), contact lens wear (stop soft lenses 1–2 weeks, rigid 3–4 weeks), occupation, expectations, systemic and ocular disease, medications (isotretinoin, amiodarone), pregnancy.
  • Examination: uncorrected and corrected acuity, manifest and cycloplegic refraction, dominant eye, pupil size (scotopic), slit lamp (MGD, dry eye, scars), tear film tests, IOP, dilated fundus (peripheral retina in myopes).
  • Investigations: topography and tomography (Pentacam: Belin–Ambrósio display), pachymetry, epithelial mapping, aberrometry, biomechanics (Corvis), specular microscopy (phakic IOL), axial length.
  • Risk assessment: ectasia risk scores (Randleman, percentage tissue altered).

Absolute contraindications to laser refractive surgery

  • Keratoconus and forme fruste keratoconus, other corneal ectasias, unstable refraction, active ocular disease (infection, inflammation), severe dry eye, uncontrolled autoimmune or collagen vascular disease, pregnancy and breastfeeding, insufficient corneal thickness for the planned ablation, unrealistic expectations, uncontrolled glaucoma, HSV keratitis (active or recent).
CO 83. LASIK — surgery and its complications (e.g. in a -8 D myope); LASEK and PRK. HIGH-YIELD · outline

LASIK

  • Flap (microkeratome or femtosecond), excimer ablation of the stromal bed, flap replacement; quick recovery, little pain.
  • For a −8 D myope: check corneal thickness (ablation depth by Munnerlyn formula: about 12–15 µm per dioptre for a 6 mm zone), residual stromal bed, high-order aberrations; phakic IOL may be better for higher errors or thin corneas.

Complications

  • Intraoperative: buttonhole, free or incomplete flap, epithelial defects.
  • Postoperative: flap striae and dislocation, diffuse lamellar keratitis (sands of Sahara), epithelial ingrowth, infectious keratitis, dry eye, regression, glare and haloes, ectasia, pressure-induced stromal keratopathy.

LASEK and PRK

  • Surface ablation without a flap (epithelium removed with alcohol or brush, or trans-PRK); slower recovery and more pain; haze (prevent with MMC 0.02%); preferred in thin corneas, contact sports and anterior basement membrane dystrophy.

CO 84. Customised (wavefront-guided) excimer laser corneal ablation. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Measures total ocular aberrations (Hartmann–Shack, Tscherning, ray tracing) and ablates a customised profile that corrects lower- and higher-order aberrations.

Types

  • Wavefront-optimised (pre-compensates for induced spherical aberration), wavefront-guided (individual aberrometry), topography-guided (corneal irregularity: ectasia, decentred ablations).

Requirements

  • Eye tracking with cyclotorsion control, iris registration, stable aberrometry.

Outcomes

  • Better contrast sensitivity and fewer night vision symptoms in eyes with high pre-existing aberrations; for most normal eyes, outcomes similar to wavefront-optimised.
CO 85. Phototherapeutic keratectomy — indications. DNB / UNIV · outline

Phototherapeutic keratectomy (PTK)

  • Excimer laser (193 nm) ablation of superficial corneal tissue (up to about 100 µm) to remove opacities or smooth the surface.

Indications

  • Anterior corneal dystrophies (Reis–Bücklers, Thiel–Behnke, granular, lattice, epithelial basement membrane dystrophy), recurrent corneal erosions, superficial scars, band keratopathy (after EDTA), Salzmann nodules, irregular surface (with masking agents), spheroidal degeneration.

Limitations and complications

  • Hyperopic shift, haze (MMC), recurrence of dystrophy, infection, delayed healing, irregular astigmatism; not for deep opacities (lamellar keratoplasty instead).
CO 86. Historical refractive procedures — radial keratotomy, epikeratophakia and laser thermal keratoplasty. DNB / UNIV · outline

Radial keratotomy (RK)

  • Radial incisions (4–16) to about 90% depth in the peripheral cornea flatten the centre: corrected myopia (PERK study); problems: diurnal fluctuation, progressive hyperopic shift, glare, weakened globe (rupture on trauma), difficult IOL power calculation later.

Epikeratophakia

  • Lathed donor lenticule sutured onto the de-epithelialised cornea (living contact lens) for aphakia, keratoconus and high myopia; poor predictability; abandoned.

Laser thermal keratoplasty (LTK)

  • Holmium:YAG laser spots in the peripheral cornea shrink collagen to steepen the centre for low hyperopia; regression; replaced by CK and then LASIK.

Others

  • Keratomileusis (Barraquer), automated lamellar keratoplasty (ALK), hexagonal keratotomy for hyperopia (unstable), astigmatic keratotomy (still used: arcuate incisions).
CO 87. Dry eye after LASIK. DNB / UNIV · outline

Dry eye after LASIK

  • Commonest complication (about 20–40% temporarily); mainly neurotrophic (corneal nerves cut by the flap and ablation), also goblet cell loss from suction ring, inflammation, change in corneal shape (blink-surface mismatch).

Risk factors

  • Pre-existing dry eye and MGD, women, Asian ethnicity, high corrections, superior hinge, thicker flaps, contact lens intolerance.

Course

  • Peaks in first month; most recover in 6–12 months as nerves regenerate.

Prevention and management

  • Treat pre-existing dry eye, consider PRK or SMILE (less nerve damage), preservative-free lubricants, punctal plugs, cyclosporine or lifitegrast, autologous serum for severe cases; treat MGD; LASIK-induced neurotrophic epitheliopathy may need cenegermin.
CO 88. Astigmatic and refractive corneal surgeries; refractive procedures for hyperopia. · outline

Astigmatic corneal surgery

  • Limbal relaxing incisions and astigmatic keratotomy (manual or femtosecond arcuate incisions), opposite clear corneal incisions, wedge resection and compression sutures (post-keratoplasty astigmatism), toric procedures with excimer (LASIK, PRK, SMILE).

Refractive corneal surgeries overview

  • Incisional (RK, AK), excimer (PRK, LASEK, epi-LASIK, LASIK, transPRK), lenticule extraction (SMILE), thermal (CK, LTK), additive (ICRS, inlays).

Procedures for hyperopia

  • Hyperopic LASIK (up to about +4 to +5 D; ablation in the mid-periphery steepens the centre), hyperopic PRK, hyperopic SMILE (in trials), CK (historical), phakic IOLs (ICL for hyperopia in suitable AC depth), refractive lens exchange (older patients, high hyperopia with angle closure risk).
CO 89. Phakic IOLs — types, indications, contraindications, power/size calculation and complications. DNB / UNIV · outline

Phakic IOL types

  • Anterior chamber angle-supported (largely withdrawn: endothelial loss), iris-fixated (Artisan or Verisyse, Artiflex), posterior chamber (implantable collamer lens ICL with central port: EVO; IPCL).

Indications

  • High myopia (up to −18 D or more), hyperopia, astigmatism (toric), thin corneas or suspect topography unsuitable for laser, stable refraction, age 21–45.

Contraindications

  • Shallow AC (under 2.8–3.0 mm from endothelium for ICL), low endothelial count (by age), cataract, glaucoma, uveitis, abnormal iris, unstable refraction.

Power and size calculation

  • Power from refraction, keratometry, AC depth and vertex distance (manufacturer software: Van der Heijde formula).
  • ICL size from white-to-white or sulcus-to-sulcus (UBM or AS-OCT); aim for vault of 250–750 µm.

Complications

  • Cataract (low vault), raised IOP and pupil block (high vault, no iridotomy in older models), endothelial loss, pigment dispersion, lens rotation (toric), glare and haloes, endophthalmitis, RD in high myopes.

CO 90. SMILE — principles and comparison with LASIK. · outline

SMILE (small incision lenticule extraction)

  • Femtosecond laser cuts an intrastromal lenticule (posterior and anterior surfaces) and a 2–4 mm incision; the lenticule is dissected and removed; no flap, no excimer laser.

Comparison with LASIK

  • Similar efficacy and safety for myopia and myopic astigmatism.
  • Advantages: no flap complications (dislocation, striae), less dry eye (fewer nerves cut), better biomechanics (anterior stroma preserved), one laser.
  • Disadvantages: slower visual recovery in first days, learning curve, limited hyperopia treatment (trials), harder enhancements (PRK or CIRCLE conversion), cyclotorsion compensation limited, suction loss, lenticule remnants.

CO 93. Ectasia after LASIK — risk factors, screening and management. NEW · outline

Ectasia after LASIK

  • Progressive steepening and thinning of the cornea after refractive surgery, with myopic shift, irregular astigmatism and loss of vision; may occur weeks to years later.

Risk factors

  • Abnormal pre-operative topography or tomography (forme fruste keratoconus: most important), thin cornea, low residual stromal bed (under 250–300 µm), high percentage tissue altered (over 40%), young age, high myopia, thick or deep flaps, eye rubbing, family history.
  • Randleman ectasia risk score; Belin–Ambrósio enhanced ectasia display; epithelial mapping; biomechanics (Corvis TBI).

Screening

  • Tomography of both eyes, epithelial thickness mapping, biomechanics; choose PRK, SMILE or no surgery when risk is raised.

Management

  • Corneal cross-linking (halts progression), rigid gas-permeable or scleral lenses, intrastromal corneal ring segments, topography-guided PRK with CXL (selected), DALK or PK for advanced cases; avoid eye rubbing.
