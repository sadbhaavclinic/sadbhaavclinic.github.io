Ophthalmic Nursing Module 5: Ophthalmic Pharmacology and Drug Administration

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Right patient, right eye, right drug, right dose, right time, right route, right documentation: the eye adds “right eye”.
  • Wait at least 5 minutes between different drops; suspensions are given after solutions; ointment goes last.
  • Cap colours help but are not proof: always read the label.
  • Know the systemic side effects: timolol (asthma, slow pulse), atropine and cyclopentolate (children), acetazolamide (tingling, kidney stones, low potassium), mannitol (fluid overload).

Giving eye drops and ointment correctly

  1. Check the prescription: patient, eye, drug, strength, frequency. Check expiry and the date the bottle was opened (most bottles are discarded about 4 weeks after opening unless stated otherwise).
  2. Wash hands. Explain the procedure. Seat or lie the patient with the head tilted back.
  3. Gently pull the lower lid down to form a pocket; ask the patient to look up.
  4. Instil one drop into the pocket without touching the eye, lashes or lid with the tip.
  5. Ask the patient to close the eye gently (not squeeze) and press the inner corner for 1–2 minutes (punctal occlusion), especially for children, the elderly and drugs with systemic effects.
  6. Wipe excess from the cheek with a clean tissue, one tissue per eye.
  7. For ointment: a 1 cm ribbon along the lower lid pocket from inner to outer corner.
  8. Record the drug, eye and time.

Order of instillation: solutions before suspensions, drops before gels and ointments, and at least 5 minutes between different medicines. If anaesthetic and other drops are both needed for an examination, the anaesthetic often goes first to reduce stinging.

Cap colour code

Many manufacturers follow a colour code for caps (for example red for mydriatics and cycloplegics, green for miotics, yellow or blue for beta-blockers, teal for prostaglandins, grey for NSAIDs, pink for steroids, tan for anti-infectives, purple for alpha-agonists, orange for carbonic anhydrase inhibitors). It helps patients with poor vision, but it is not followed by every company: always read the label.

Calculations you may need

  • Percentage strength: 1% = 1 g in 100 mL = 10 mg/mL. So 0.5% timolol = 5 mg/mL.
  • Dilution: to make cefuroxime 1 mg in 0.1 mL (10 mg/mL), 50 mg can be dissolved in 5 mL of saline. Always follow the unit’s validated protocol and double-check with a second person.
  • Mannitol 20%: 20 g in 100 mL. A dose of 1 g/kg for a 60 kg patient is 60 g = 300 mL.
  • Acetazolamide: commonly 250 mg tablets; dose and frequency as prescribed, adjusted for kidney function.

Drug safety

  • Store fortified drops, some prostaglandins and anti-VEGF agents in the fridge (2–8 °C) with a temperature log.
  • Label every syringe on the sterile field. Unlabelled syringes are a known cause of serious intraocular errors.
  • Look-alike, sound-alike names exist (for example dorzolamide and timolol combinations in several brands). Read twice.
  • Report and record any error or reaction; patient safety culture depends on reporting.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

Why should you press on the inner corner after drops?

To block drainage into the nose, which reduces systemic absorption and keeps the drug in the eye longer.

Which glaucoma drop is unsafe in asthma?

Timolol and other non-selective beta-blockers (bronchospasm).

Which glaucoma drop is unsafe in infants?

Brimonidine: it can cause drowsiness and breathing depression.

Name three side effects of oral acetazolamide.

Tingling of hands and feet, frequent urination, low potassium (also kidney stones, tiredness, taste change).

How much mannitol 20% contains 60 g?

300 mL (20 g per 100 mL).

Self-test: 8 MCQs

Answer all, then press “Check my score”. Option order is mixed.

1. The minimum wait between two different eye drops is about:

Show answer

Answer: 5 minutes. Avoids wash-out.

2. Which drop darkens the iris and lengthens lashes?

Show answer

Answer: Latanoprost. Prostaglandin analogue.

3. Atropine overdose in a child may cause:

Show answer

Answer: Fever, flushing, fast pulse and agitation. Anticholinergic toxicity.

4. 1% solution contains:

Show answer

Answer: 10 mg/mL. 1 g per 100 mL.

5. Phenylephrine 10% should be avoided in:

Show answer

Answer: Uncontrolled hypertension and infants. Raises blood pressure.

6. Mitomycin C must be handled as:

Show answer

Answer: Cytotoxic. Gloves and cytotoxic disposal.

7. Which drug reduces IOP by increasing uveoscleral outflow?

Show answer

Answer: Latanoprost. Prostaglandin analogue.

8. Topical anaesthetic drops given to patients for home use can cause:

Show answer

Answer: Corneal damage and ulceration. Never dispense for home use.
