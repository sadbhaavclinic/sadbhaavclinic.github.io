MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Neuro-ophthalmology (53 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 53 neuro-ophthalmology theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Visual Pathway

NO 1. How would you evaluate the integrity of the visual pathways? Draw the pathway with its blood supply and discuss the localisation of lesions at each level. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Evaluating the visual pathways

  • Visual acuity, colour vision (Ishihara), contrast, pupils (RAPD), visual fields (confrontation, perimetry), fundus (disc), VEP, OCT (RNFL, GCL), MRI.

Pathway with blood supply

  • Retina and optic nerve (CRA, posterior ciliary arteries) → chiasm (circle of Willis: superior hypophyseal, anterior cerebral, anterior communicating) → optic tract (anterior choroidal) → LGN (anterior and posterior choroidal) → radiations (anterior choroidal and middle cerebral; Meyer loop) → visual cortex (posterior cerebral; macular area dual supply from middle cerebral).

Localisation

  • Optic nerve: unilateral loss, central scotoma, RAPD.
  • Junction: junctional scotoma (ipsilateral central, contralateral superotemporal).
  • Chiasm: bitemporal hemianopia.
  • Tract: incongruous homonymous hemianopia, RAPD contralateral, band atrophy.
  • LGN: sectoranopia.
  • Temporal radiation: superior homonymous quadrantanopia (“pie in the sky”); parietal: inferior.
  • Occipital: congruous homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing; cortical blindness (bilateral).

Revise from: Visual Pathway and Field Defects Made Easy: Five Rules, USMLE Ophthalmology High-Yield: Visual Pathway, Pupils, Drugs, Systemic Disease and Eye Emergencies

NO 2. Anatomy of the optic chiasma, tracts and radiations; discuss the lesions affecting the visual fields (bitemporal and homonymous hemianopia). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Chiasm

  • About 12 mm wide; lies above the diaphragma sellae and pituitary; prefixed (over tuberculum: 10%), central (80%), postfixed (over dorsum: 10%).
  • Nasal fibres cross (53%); inferonasal fibres loop into the contralateral optic nerve (Wilbrand knee, debated); macular fibres cross posteriorly.

Tracts and radiations

  • Tracts wind round the cerebral peduncles to LGN; radiations through temporal (Meyer loop: superior field) and parietal lobes (inferior field) to striate cortex.

Bitemporal hemianopia

  • Pituitary adenoma (starts superotemporal), craniopharyngioma (starts inferotemporal), meningioma, aneurysm, glioma, trauma, tilted discs (pseudo-bitemporal).

Homonymous hemianopia

  • Stroke (commonest), tumour, trauma; congruity increases posteriorly; macular sparing in occipital lesions; Riddoch phenomenon.

Revise from: Visual Pathway and Field Defects Made Easy: Five Rules

NO 3. Describe the aetiopathogenesis and appearance of visual field changes in neurological disorders. · outline

Aetiopathogenesis of neurological field defects

  • Compression (tumours, aneurysms), ischaemia (stroke, NAION), demyelination, inflammation, trauma, raised ICP, toxic and nutritional; site of damage determines the pattern.

Appearance by site

  • Optic nerve: central or centrocaecal scotoma (optic neuritis, toxic), altitudinal (NAION), arcuate, generalised depression; respect horizontal meridian.
  • Chiasm: bitemporal hemianopia (superior first with pituitary, inferior first with craniopharyngioma), junctional scotoma.
  • Optic tract: incongruous homonymous hemianopia with contralateral RAPD.
  • Temporal radiation: contralateral superior quadrantanopia (pie in the sky); parietal: inferior quadrantanopia (pie on the floor).
  • Occipital cortex: congruous homonymous hemianopia, macular sparing, homonymous scotomas, temporal crescent sparing.
  • Bilateral occipital: cortical blindness, checkerboard defects.
  • Respect for the vertical meridian indicates chiasmal or post-chiasmal disease.

Revise from: Visual Field Reference Card: Reliability, Glaucoma Criteria and Staging

NO 4. Scotoma — definition, positive vs negative; altitudinal field defects. DNB / UNIV · outline

Scotoma

  • An area of reduced or absent vision within the visual field surrounded by an area of normal vision.

Types

  • Absolute (no perception of any stimulus) vs relative (only weaker stimuli missed).
  • Positive: seen by the patient as a dark or coloured patch (lesions in front of the photoreceptors or retina: haemorrhage, macular disease, migraine aura scintillating).
  • Negative: not noticed by the patient; found on testing (physiological blind spot, optic nerve lesions, glaucoma).
  • By location: central, paracentral, centrocaecal, arcuate (Bjerrum), ring, Seidel, nasal step.

Altitudinal field defects

  • Loss of the upper or lower half of the field respecting the horizontal meridian.
  • Causes: NAION and AAION (commonest), branch retinal artery occlusion, glaucoma (advanced), optic neuritis (occasionally), optic disc coloboma or drusen, bilateral occipital lesions (above or below calcarine fissure), chiasmal compression (rare).

Revise from: Visual Field Reference Card: Reliability, Glaucoma Criteria and Staging

NO 5. Visual cortex. · outline

Visual cortex

  • Primary visual cortex (V1, Brodmann area 17, striate cortex) on the medial occipital lobe above and below the calcarine fissure; upper bank represents the inferior field; macula at the occipital pole (large representation: cortical magnification); peripheral field anteriorly (temporal crescent from contralateral eye).
  • Blood supply: posterior cerebral artery (calcarine branch); occipital pole also from middle cerebral artery (macular sparing).
  • Layers: six layers; layer 4C receives LGN input; ocular dominance and orientation columns; blobs (colour).

Association areas

  • V2, V3 (area 18, 19), V4 (colour: achromatopsia), V5/MT (motion: akinetopsia); dorsal stream (where) and ventral stream (what: object and face recognition).

Lesions

  • Congruous homonymous hemianopia, cortical blindness (Anton syndrome: denial), visual agnosia, prosopagnosia, Balint syndrome (simultagnosia, optic ataxia), Riddoch phenomenon, visual hallucinations (release).
NO 6. Pituitary lesions and craniopharyngioma for the ophthalmologist. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pituitary adenoma

  • Commonest cause of chiasmal compression; functioning (prolactinoma, GH: acromegaly, ACTH: Cushing) or non-functioning (present with vision loss).
  • Features: bitemporal field defects (superior first), optic atrophy (band or bow-tie in crossed fibres), see-saw nystagmus rarely, pituitary apoplexy (sudden headache, vision loss, ophthalmoplegia: emergency).
  • Investigations: fields, OCT (GCL predicts recovery), MRI with contrast, hormone profile.
  • Treatment: dopamine agonists (cabergoline) for prolactinomas; trans-sphenoidal surgery; radiotherapy.

Craniopharyngioma

  • Rathke pouch remnant; children and older adults; calcified cystic suprasellar mass.
  • Features: inferior bitemporal defects first, papilloedema (hydrocephalus), growth failure, diabetes insipidus.
  • Treatment: surgery, radiotherapy.
NO 49. Posterior cortical atrophy and disorders of higher visual function. NEW · outline

Posterior cortical atrophy (PCA)

  • Neurodegenerative syndrome (usually atypical Alzheimer disease) affecting the parieto-occipital cortex; onset 50s–60s; memory relatively preserved early.
  • Patients often present to eye clinics first with visual complaints despite normal eye examination and acuity.

Features

  • Difficulty reading (losing place), judging distances, finding objects, navigating, dressing; simultanagnosia, optic ataxia and oculomotor apraxia (Balint syndrome); visual agnosia, prosopagnosia, alexia, acalculia (Gerstmann), homonymous field defects.

Evaluation

  • Suspect when complaints are out of proportion to eye findings; cognitive screening, visual perception tests, MRI (posterior atrophy), PET, CSF or blood amyloid and tau biomarkers; refer to neurology.

Disorders of higher visual function (other)

  • Visual agnosia (apperceptive vs associative), prosopagnosia (fusiform gyrus), achromatopsia (V4), akinetopsia (V5), hemispatial neglect (right parietal), Charles Bonnet syndrome, Anton syndrome.

Management

  • Neurology care, low-vision and occupational therapy, reading aids, safety (driving), support for families.

Revise from: Posterior Cortical Atrophy (PCA) in the Eye Clinic: Normal Eyes, Failing Vision, and Other Brain-Based Visual Disorders

Optic Nerve

NO 7. Describe the anatomy, relations and blood supply of the optic nerve; functional anatomy of the optic nerve head and its FFA. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Anatomy

  • About 50 mm long: intraocular (1 mm), intraorbital (25 mm, S-shaped), intracanalicular (9 mm), intracranial (10–16 mm); 1.2 million axons; myelinated behind the lamina cribrosa.
  • Relations: annulus of Zinn (recti origins: pain on eye movements in optic neuritis), ophthalmic artery (below in the canal), sphenoid and ethmoid sinuses (medial), ICA.

Blood supply

  • Surface layer: CRA branches; prelaminar and laminar: short posterior ciliary arteries, circle of Zinn–Haller; retrolaminar: pial vessels; intracranial: superior hypophyseal, ICA.

Functional anatomy of the optic nerve head

  • Surface nerve fibre layer, prelaminar, laminar (lamina cribrosa: weak point in glaucoma), retrolaminar; arcuate fibres respect the horizontal raphe.

FFA of the disc

  • Early filling from posterior ciliary arteries (prelaminar flush), superficial capillary filling with the retinal arterial phase; late staining of disc margin normal; leakage in papilloedema, NAION (sectoral delay), papillitis.

NO 8. Signs of optic nerve dysfunction and investigations to assess optic nerve function. DNB / UNIV · outline

Signs of optic nerve dysfunction

  • Reduced visual acuity, RAPD, reduced colour vision (dyschromatopsia; red desaturation), reduced contrast sensitivity, reduced brightness sense, visual field defects (central, centrocaecal, arcuate, altitudinal), disc changes (swelling, pallor, cupping), delayed VEP.

Investigations

  • Ishihara and Hardy–Rand–Rittler plates, Farnsworth–Munsell 100-hue, Pelli–Robson chart, perimetry (Humphrey and Goldmann), OCT (RNFL and GCL thickness), VEP (latency and amplitude), pattern ERG (ganglion cell function), MRI orbits and brain with contrast, blood tests (ESR, CRP, B12, folate, syphilis, NMO and MOG antibodies), genetic testing (LHON, OPA1).

Revise from: Optic Nerve, Pupils and Visual Pathway for MBBS Students: Papilloedema, Optic Neuritis, Optic Atrophy and Field Defects

NO 9. Discuss the aetiopathogenesis, differential diagnosis and management of unilateral optic disc swelling. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of unilateral disc swelling

  • Ischaemic: NAION, AAION (GCA), diabetic papillopathy.
  • Inflammatory and infectious: optic neuritis (papillitis), neuroretinitis (cat-scratch), syphilis, TB, sarcoid.
  • Vascular: CRVO, hypotony.
  • Compressive and infiltrative: orbital tumours (meningioma), leukaemia, lymphoma.
  • Others: LHON (pseudo-oedema), uveitis, early papilloedema with asymmetry, Foster Kennedy syndrome (one disc swollen, other atrophic), pseudopapilloedema (drusen).

Approach

  • Age, vision, pain, RAPD, colour, fields, systemic history (GCA symptoms, vasculopathic risk), ESR and CRP, B-scan (drusen), OCT, MRI orbits and brain, serology.

Management

  • Treat the cause: urgent steroids for GCA, optic neuritis treatment, antimicrobials for infections, control vascular risk factors in NAION.

Revise from: Optic Disc Drusen vs Papilloedema: How to Tell Them Apart

NO 10. Papilloedema — pathogenesis, pathology, features and differential diagnosis (pseudopapilloedema); papilloedema and head injury. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Bilateral disc swelling due to raised intracranial pressure.

Pathogenesis

  • Raised CSF pressure in the subarachnoid space around the optic nerve → stasis of axoplasmic flow → axonal swelling, venous congestion.

Features

  • Early: transient visual obscurations, headache, nausea, pulsatile tinnitus, diplopia (6th nerve); disc hyperaemia, loss of SVP, peripapillary haemorrhages, Paton lines.
  • Established and chronic: elevated disc, obscured vessels, champagne-cork disc; atrophic: pale disc, field loss (enlarged blind spot, constriction).
  • Frisén grading 0–5.

Differential diagnosis (pseudopapilloedema)

  • Disc drusen (B-scan, autofluorescence, OCT), crowded hyperopic discs, tilted discs, myelinated nerve fibres, Bergmeister papilla.

Causes

  • Intracranial mass, hydrocephalus, venous sinus thrombosis, IIH, meningitis, hypertension (malignant).

Papilloedema and head injury

  • Uncommon early after acute head injury; appears over days with haematoma, oedema or venous sinus thrombosis; urgent imaging.

NO 11. AION — types (arteritic, NAION), aetiopathogenesis, clinical picture and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Arteritic AION (GCA): older patients (over 50), headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, PMR; chalky-white pale disc swelling, cilioretinal artery occlusion, raised ESR and CRP; temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound.
  • NAION: “disc at risk” (small cup), hypertension, diabetes, sleep apnoea, nocturnal hypotension, PDE5 inhibitors, semaglutide (reported association); altitudinal field defect; sectoral hyperaemic swelling.

Management

  • AAION: immediate high-dose steroids (IV methylprednisolone if vision loss, then oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg), tocilizumab as steroid-sparing (GiACTA), protect the other eye.
  • NAION: no proven treatment (IONDT: decompression harmful; steroids and others unproven); control risk factors, treat sleep apnoea, avoid nocturnal antihypertensives; second eye risk about 15% in 5 years.

Revise from: Optic Neuritis vs NAION vs Arteritic AION: How to Tell Them Apart, Semaglutide and NAION: What the Evidence Says

NO 12. Giant cell (temporal) arteritis — clinical features and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Giant cell arteritis (GCA)

  • Granulomatous vasculitis of medium and large arteries (especially extracranial branches of the carotid); age over 50 years, women.

Clinical features

  • Systemic: new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (most specific), polymyalgia rheumatica, fever, weight loss, malaise.
  • Ocular: AAION (chalky-white disc swelling), CRAO, cilioretinal artery occlusion, PION, amaurosis fugax, diplopia (ischaemic cranial nerve palsy), ocular ischaemic syndrome.

Diagnosis

  • ESR and CRP raised (rarely normal), platelets high; temporal artery biopsy (skip lesions: 1.5–2 cm specimen within 2 weeks of steroids), temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign), PET-CT for large vessel involvement; ACR/EULAR 2022 criteria.

Management

  • Immediate high-dose steroids without waiting for biopsy: IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g for 3 days if vision loss, then oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg (max 60–80 mg) with slow taper over 1–2 years.
  • Tocilizumab (GiACTA) as steroid-sparing therapy; aspirin (debated); bone protection, gastric protection, monitor blood sugar and BP.

Revise from: Giant Cell Arteritis: The Emergency You Can't Miss

NO 13. Optic neuritis, papillitis and neuroretinitis — classification, aetiology, features, diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Retrobulbar (normal disc), papillitis (swollen disc), neuroretinitis (disc swelling with macular star), perineuritis.
  • Typical (demyelinating, MS) vs atypical (NMOSD, MOGAD, infectious, autoimmune).

Aetiology

  • Demyelination (MS), NMOSD (aquaporin-4), MOG antibody, infections (viral, syphilis, TB, cat-scratch for neuroretinitis), autoimmune (sarcoid, SLE), post-vaccinal.

Features

  • Subacute vision loss, pain on eye movement, RAPD, dyschromatopsia, central scotoma, Uhthoff phenomenon, Pulfrich.

Diagnosis

  • MRI brain and orbits with contrast (white-matter lesions: MS risk), AQP4 and MOG antibodies, VEP (delayed P100), OCT, serology.

Management

  • ONTT: IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3 days, then oral prednisolone, speeds recovery but does not change final vision; oral prednisolone alone (standard dose) increased recurrence.
  • NMOSD and MOGAD: urgent high-dose steroids, plasma exchange, long-term immunosuppression (rituximab, eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab).
  • MS risk: disease-modifying therapies with neurology.
  • Neuroretinitis: treat infection (doxycycline, azithromycin for Bartonella).

Revise from: Optic Neuritis vs NAION vs Arteritic AION: How to Tell Them Apart, Landmark Trials in Neuro-Ophthalmology: ONTT, IONDT, IIHTT, Steroids in Trauma and Giant Cell Arteritis

NO 14. Drusen of the optic nerve head. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optic disc drusen

  • Calcified acellular deposits within the optic nerve head anterior to the lamina; bilateral in about two thirds; small crowded discs; autosomal dominant tendency.

Features

  • Buried in children (pseudopapilloedema), become visible with age (refractile bodies); field defects (inferonasal, enlarged blind spot, arcuate), rarely NAION, CNV, haemorrhages.

Diagnosis

  • B-scan (hyperreflective with shadowing), autofluorescence, EDI-OCT (hyporeflective core with hyperreflective rim; ODDS consortium), CT (calcification), FFA (no leakage, nodular staining).

Management

  • No treatment; monitor fields and RNFL; IOP lowering sometimes for progressive field loss; anti-VEGF for CNV.

Revise from: Optic Disc Drusen vs Papilloedema: How to Tell Them Apart

NO 15. Toxic and drug-induced optic neuropathies; toxic amblyopia. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes

  • Tobacco-alcohol amblyopia (nutritional deficiency: vitamin B12, folate, thiamine), methanol (acute, metabolic acidosis), ethambutol (dose-related, over 15 mg/kg/day), isoniazid, linezolid, amiodarone (bilateral NAION-like), chloramphenicol, disulfiram, vincristine, infliximab, sildenafil.

Features

  • Bilateral, symmetric, painless, progressive loss of vision, dyschromatopsia (red-green with ethambutol: blue-yellow early), centrocaecal scotomas, temporal pallor later.

Investigations

  • Colour vision, fields, OCT (RNFL thinning papillomacular bundle), VEP; vitamin B12 and folate levels; methanol and blood gases in acute cases.

Management

  • Stop the offending drug (baseline and monthly screening with ethambutol), nutritional supplements (B12 injections, multivitamins), stop tobacco and alcohol; methanol: ethanol or fomepizole, bicarbonate, dialysis, IV steroids (sometimes).

Revise from: Hereditary and Compressive Optic Neuropathies: LHON, DOA and Chiasmal Lesions

NO 16. Describe the clinical features, diagnosis and management of methyl alcohol poisoning. · outline

Methyl alcohol poisoning

  • Ingestion of adulterated liquor (hooch tragedies in India), industrial spirits; toxic metabolite formic acid inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase (optic nerve and retina), causing metabolic acidosis.

Clinical features

  • Latent period of 12–24 hours; nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, headache, breathlessness (Kussmaul), confusion, coma.
  • Eye: blurred vision, snowstorm vision, photophobia, reduced acuity to blindness; dilated poorly reactive pupils (poor prognostic sign), disc hyperaemia and oedema, peripapillary retinal oedema, later optic atrophy and cupping; central or centrocaecal scotomas.

Diagnosis

  • Blood methanol levels, high anion gap metabolic acidosis, raised osmolar gap; MRI (putaminal necrosis).

Management

  • Correct acidosis (sodium bicarbonate), block alcohol dehydrogenase (fomepizole or ethanol), haemodialysis (removes methanol and formate), folinic acid or folic acid (enhances formate metabolism), IV methylprednisolone (some reports), supportive care; outcome depends on early treatment.
NO 17. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy — features and differential diagnosis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON)

  • Mitochondrial DNA mutations (11778 commonest and worst prognosis, 14484 best recovery chance, 3460); maternal inheritance; young men (80–90%), incomplete penetrance (smoking, alcohol triggers).

Clinical features

  • Acute or subacute painless central vision loss in one eye, then the other within weeks to months; central or centrocaecal scotoma, dyschromatopsia.
  • Acute phase: disc hyperaemia, peripapillary telangiectatic microangiopathy, swelling of the RNFL (pseudo-oedema) without leakage on FFA; later optic atrophy (temporal).
  • Pupil reflexes relatively preserved (ipRGC sparing); cardiac conduction defects (pre-excitation); LHON plus (MS-like disease: Harding disease).

Differential diagnosis

  • Optic neuritis (painful, MRI changes), toxic or nutritional optic neuropathy, compressive lesions, dominant optic atrophy (OPA1: childhood onset, slowly progressive, temporal pallor), NAION, infiltrative and inflammatory optic neuropathies.

Management

  • Genetic testing; idebenone (approved in Europe, improves recovery chances if started early); stop smoking and alcohol; gene therapy (lenadogene nolparvovec: ND4) under evaluation; low vision support; genetic counselling.

Revise from: Hereditary and Compressive Optic Neuropathies: LHON, DOA and Chiasmal Lesions

NO 18. Foster Kennedy syndrome. DNB / UNIV · outline

Foster Kennedy syndrome

  • Optic atrophy in one eye (from direct compression of the optic nerve) and papilloedema in the other (from raised intracranial pressure), with anosmia.

Causes

  • Olfactory groove meningioma, frontal lobe tumours, sphenoid ridge meningioma.

Pseudo-Foster Kennedy syndrome

  • Similar appearance from sequential bilateral NAION (atrophy from an old event in one eye, acute swelling in the other), optic neuritis, or other causes; commoner than the true syndrome.

Management

  • MRI brain with contrast; neurosurgical treatment of the tumour.
NO 19. Cortical blindness. DNB / UNIV · outline

Cortical blindness

  • Bilateral loss of vision from damage to both occipital lobes (or bilateral geniculocalcarine pathways), with normal pupillary light reflexes and normal fundi.

Causes

  • Bilateral posterior cerebral artery infarction (basilar artery embolism), hypoxia (cardiac arrest), PRES (eclampsia, hypertensive encephalopathy, immunosuppressants), trauma, tumours, infections, migraine, post-angiography contrast, metabolic.

Features

  • Loss of vision (may be incomplete), normal pupils, no OKN response, preserved blink to threat lost; Anton syndrome (denial of blindness with confabulation), Riddoch phenomenon (motion perception), blindsight.

Investigations

  • MRI brain (DWI), MRA, EEG, VEP (abnormal or normal), cardiac evaluation.

Management

  • Treat the cause (BP control in PRES: often reversible), stroke management, rehabilitation.
NO 20. Optic atrophy — primary and secondary. DNB / UNIV · outline

Optic atrophy

  • Degeneration of optic nerve axons with pallor of the disc and loss of RNFL.

Primary (simple) optic atrophy

  • No preceding disc swelling; flat, white disc with sharp margins, clearly visible lamina cribrosa, reduced capillaries (Kestenbaum sign: fewer than 6).
  • Causes: retrobulbar neuritis, compressive lesions (tumours, aneurysms), toxic and nutritional, hereditary (LHON, DOA), trauma, syphilis (tabes dorsalis).

Secondary optic atrophy

  • Follows disc swelling (papilloedema, papillitis, AION); greyish-white disc with blurred margins, gliosis obscuring the lamina, sheathing of vessels.

Consecutive optic atrophy

  • Secondary to retinal disease (RP, CRAO, extensive PRP, chorioretinitis): waxy pallor (RP), attenuated vessels.

Other types

  • Glaucomatous (cupping), temporal pallor (MS, toxic, LHON), band or bow-tie atrophy (chiasmal and tract lesions).

Management

  • Investigate cause (MRI, blood tests, genetics), treat underlying disease, low-vision aids.
NO 21. Benign (idiopathic) intracranial hypertension — presentation, causes, diagnosis and treatment. DNB / UNIV · outline

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH)

  • Raised ICP without mass lesion, hydrocephalus or abnormal CSF composition; young obese women of child-bearing age.

Presentation

  • Headache, transient visual obscurations, pulsatile tinnitus, diplopia (sixth nerve), back pain; papilloedema (bilateral), enlarged blind spot, field constriction, rarely vision loss.

Causes and risk factors

  • Obesity, recent weight gain, female sex, drugs (tetracyclines, vitamin A and retinoids, lithium, growth hormone, steroid withdrawal), endocrine (PCOS, Addison, hypoparathyroidism), venous sinus stenosis, anaemia, sleep apnoea.

Diagnosis (modified Dandy criteria, Friedman 2013)

  • Papilloedema, normal neurological examination except cranial nerve abnormalities, normal brain MRI (with MRV to exclude venous sinus thrombosis; signs: empty sella, flattened posterior globe, tortuous optic nerves), normal CSF composition, raised opening pressure (over 25 cm H₂O in adults).

Treatment

  • Weight loss (5–10%; bariatric surgery; GLP-1 agonists), acetazolamide (IIHTT: with weight loss improved fields), topiramate; stop causative drugs.
  • Surgery for vision loss: optic nerve sheath fenestration, CSF shunting (ventriculoperitoneal), venous sinus stenting.
  • Monitor fields and OCT.

Revise from: Papilloedema vs Pseudopapilloedema

NO 22. Multiple sclerosis and demyelinating disease — ocular aspects. · outline

Ocular aspects of multiple sclerosis

  • Afferent: optic neuritis (often first symptom), RNFL and GCL thinning on OCT even without optic neuritis, Uhthoff phenomenon, Pulfrich phenomenon, dyschromatopsia, field defects (chiasmal and retrochiasmal plaques).
  • Efferent: internuclear ophthalmoplegia (often bilateral), gaze palsies, one-and-a-half syndrome, nystagmus (pendular, gaze-evoked, upbeat, downbeat), saccadic dysmetria, skew deviation, sixth nerve palsy.
  • Others: intermediate uveitis (pars planitis) and periphlebitis (HLA-DR15).

Diagnosis

  • McDonald criteria (2017; 2024 update includes optic nerve as a site): dissemination in space and time by MRI, CSF oligoclonal bands.

Management

  • Acute: IV methylprednisolone for optic neuritis or relapses; disease-modifying therapies (interferons, glatiramer, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, fingolimod: monitor for macular oedema); avoid anti-TNF agents (demyelination).
NO 23. Optic nerve tumours — glioma and meningioma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optic nerve glioma

  • Pilocytic astrocytoma; first decade; about 30% have NF1 (NF1 gliomas less aggressive, often bilateral).
  • Features: gradual proptosis (axial), vision loss, RAPD, disc oedema or atrophy, strabismus, nystagmus.
  • MRI: fusiform enlargement with kinking; chiasmal involvement.
  • Management: observation if stable; chemotherapy (carboplatin, vincristine) for progressive disease; surgery for blind, disfiguring eye.

Optic nerve sheath meningioma

  • Middle-aged women; from the meningothelial cells of the nerve sheath.
  • Features: slow painless visual loss, optociliary shunt vessels, disc oedema then atrophy (triad).
  • CT or MRI: tram-track sign, calcification, tubular enlargement.
  • Management: observation; fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (treatment of choice for progressive cases); surgery rarely.
NO 24. MOG antibody disease and NMOSD (aquaporin-4); lessons from the ONTT and IIHTT. · outline

NMOSD (aquaporin-4 antibody)

  • Astrocytopathy; severe bilateral or sequential optic neuritis (chiasmal involvement, long segment over half of the nerve), longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (3 or more segments), area postrema syndrome (intractable hiccups, vomiting); women, Asian and African ancestry; poor recovery.
  • Treatment: high-dose steroids and early plasma exchange; long-term rituximab, eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab, azathioprine, mycophenolate; avoid MS drugs (interferon, natalizumab can worsen).

MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD)

  • Recurrent bilateral optic neuritis with marked disc swelling, perineural enhancement, anterior long segment involvement; ADEM in children; good recovery with steroids but steroid-dependent relapses.
  • Treatment: steroids with slow taper, IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate for relapses.

Lessons from ONTT

  • IV methylprednisolone speeds recovery but does not change final vision; oral prednisolone alone (1 mg/kg) increases recurrence; MRI predicts MS risk (overall 50% at 15 years; 25% with normal MRI); most recover vision.

Lessons from IIHTT

  • In mild vision loss, acetazolamide (up to 4 g/day) with weight loss improved visual field, papilloedema grade and quality of life compared with weight loss alone.

NO 50. Semaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonists) and NAION. NEW · outline

GLP-1 receptor agonists and NAION

  • Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide are widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity.

Evidence

  • Retrospective cohort studies (Hathaway et al., JAMA Ophthalmology 2024) reported an increased risk of NAION with semaglutide; later large database studies gave mixed results.
  • The European Medicines Agency (2025) concluded that NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide and updated product information.
  • Possible mechanisms: rapid changes in blood glucose and blood pressure, nocturnal hypotension, effects on optic nerve head perfusion; confounding by diabetes, obesity and sleep apnoea.
  • Rapid improvement in glycaemic control can also cause early worsening of diabetic retinopathy (SUSTAIN-6).

Practical approach

  • Ask about GLP-1 drug use in patients with NAION; discuss stopping or switching with the prescribing physician (especially to protect the fellow eye); counsel patients with prior NAION or crowded discs; baseline retinal screening in patients with diabetic retinopathy starting these drugs.

NO 25. Describe the pupillary pathways (light reflex and sympathetic) in detail, their affections and the tests to elucidate them. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Light reflex pathway

  • Retinal ganglion cells (including intrinsically photosensitive cells) → optic nerve → chiasm → tract → pretectal nucleus (bypassing LGN) → both Edinger–Westphal nuclei → oculomotor nerve → ciliary ganglion → short ciliary nerves → sphincter pupillae.

Near reflex

  • Cortical pathway via the occipital and frontal areas to EW nuclei: accommodation, convergence and miosis.

Sympathetic pathway

  • First-order: hypothalamus → ciliospinal centre of Budge (C8–T2).
  • Second-order: through the chest apex → superior cervical ganglion.
  • Third-order: along the ICA → cavernous sinus → nasociliary and long ciliary nerves → dilator pupillae and Müller muscle.

Affections

  • RAPD (optic nerve), Argyll Robertson (syphilis: small irregular, light–near dissociation), Adie tonic pupil (ciliary ganglion), Horner syndrome, third nerve palsy, Parinaud (dorsal midbrain), pharmacological mydriasis.

Tests

  • Swinging flashlight (RAPD), near reflex, dilution and slit lamp (vermiform movements), pilocarpine 0.125% (Adie), 1% pilocarpine (pharmacological block), cocaine 4–10% or apraclonidine 0.5% (Horner), hydroxyamphetamine 1% (localisation).

NO 26. Detail your examination and management of a case of anisocoria — causes and differential diagnosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Examination

  • Measure pupils in dim and bright light: anisocoria greater in light → abnormal large pupil (parasympathetic defect); greater in dark → abnormal small pupil (sympathetic defect: Horner).
  • Check lids (ptosis: Horner, third nerve), eye movements, slit lamp (iris damage, synechiae, vermiform movements), near reflex.

Causes

  • Physiological (about 20% of people; under 1 mm, same in light and dark).
  • Small pupil: Horner syndrome, uveitis synechiae, pilocarpine, Argyll Robertson.
  • Large pupil: third nerve palsy, Adie tonic pupil, pharmacological, traumatic mydriasis, angle closure.

Management

  • Pharmacological tests to confirm; urgent imaging for painful Horner (carotid dissection: CT or MR angiography) and pupil-involving third nerve palsy (aneurysm: CTA or MRA).
  • Reassure for physiological; Adie: reading glasses, dilute pilocarpine.

NO 27. Relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn pupil). DNB / UNIV · outline

Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD; Marcus Gunn pupil)

  • Asymmetric afferent input between the two eyes, detected by the swinging flashlight test: when the light is moved to the affected eye, both pupils dilate (paradoxical dilatation).

Causes

  • Unilateral or asymmetric optic nerve disease (optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, glaucoma, compression, trauma, LHON asymmetry), extensive retinal disease (CRAO, CRVO, large RD), optic tract lesion (contralateral RAPD), dense amblyopia (mild).
  • Not caused by cataract, refractive error or vitreous haemorrhage (unless dense).

Grading

  • Neutral density filters (0.3–1.8 log units) to quantify; clinical grades 1–4.

Significance

  • Objective sign of afferent pathway asymmetry; helpful in malingering; reverse RAPD testing when one pupil cannot react (look at the reacting pupil).

NO 28. Light-near dissociation — Argyll Robertson pupil, Adie tonic pupil and other causes. DNB / UNIV · outline

Light-near dissociation

  • Pupils constrict better to near effort than to light.

Argyll Robertson pupil

  • Neurosyphilis; bilateral small (miotic), irregular pupils, light–near dissociation, poor dilatation; lesion in the dorsal midbrain (pretectal) region; vision normal.

Adie tonic pupil

  • Young women; unilateral dilated pupil with poor light reaction, slow sustained near constriction and slow redilatation, vermiform movements of the iris, cholinergic supersensitivity (constricts with dilute pilocarpine 0.125%); damage to the ciliary ganglion; Holmes–Adie syndrome with absent deep tendon reflexes.

Other causes

  • Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome (pinealoma, hydrocephalus), diabetes (autonomic neuropathy), aberrant regeneration of the third nerve, severe bilateral afferent loss (optic neuropathy), myotonic dystrophy, Lyme disease, amyloidosis, alcoholism.

NO 29. Horner syndrome — clinical features, pharmacological tests and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Horner syndrome (oculosympathetic paresis)

  • Ptosis (mild, Müller muscle), miosis, anhidrosis (pre-ganglionic lesions), apparent enophthalmos, reverse ptosis of lower lid, dilatation lag, heterochromia (congenital: lighter iris on affected side).

Localisation

  • First order: hypothalamus, brainstem (lateral medullary syndrome), spinal cord.
  • Second order (pre-ganglionic): apical lung tumour (Pancoast), neck trauma, thyroid surgery, cervical rib, neuroblastoma (children).
  • Third order (post-ganglionic): carotid dissection (painful: urgent), cavernous sinus lesions, cluster headache, skull base tumours.

Pharmacological tests

  • Cocaine 4–10%: normal pupil dilates; Horner pupil fails to dilate (confirms diagnosis).
  • Apraclonidine 0.5–1%: reversal of anisocoria (Horner pupil dilates due to denervation supersensitivity), often after days to weeks.
  • Hydroxyamphetamine 1%: dilates if third-order neuron intact (pre-ganglionic lesion); fails in post-ganglionic lesion.

Management

  • Urgent imaging for acute painful Horner (CTA or MRA head and neck for dissection); chest imaging (Pancoast); children: urine catecholamines and MRI (neuroblastoma); ptosis surgery if needed.

NO 30. Anatomy of the 3rd, 4th and 6th cranial nerves; applied anatomy of the 3rd nerve nucleus. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Third nerve

  • Nucleus in the midbrain at the level of the superior colliculus; subnuclei: single central levator subnucleus (bilateral ptosis), paired SR (contralateral), paired MR, IR, IO; EW nucleus.
  • Course: fascicles through the red nucleus (Benedikt) and cerebral peduncle (Weber) → between posterior cerebral and superior cerebellar arteries (aneurysm of PCom) → lateral wall of cavernous sinus → superior orbital fissure (superior and inferior divisions).

Fourth nerve

  • Nucleus at the inferior colliculus; only nerve to exit dorsally and decussate; longest intracranial course (head trauma); supplies the contralateral superior oblique.

Sixth nerve

  • Nucleus in the pons (under the facial colliculus); long vertical course over the petrous apex (Dorello canal; Gradenigo syndrome); inside the cavernous sinus next to the ICA; false localising sign in raised ICP.

Applied anatomy of the third nerve nucleus

  • Nuclear lesion: bilateral ptosis and contralateral SR weakness; pupil fibres lie superficially and medially on the nerve (compression affects the pupil).

NO 31. Third nerve palsy — localisation of the lesion, causes, aberrant regeneration and treatment. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Localisation

  • Nuclear (bilateral ptosis, contralateral SR), fascicular (Weber: contralateral hemiplegia; Benedikt: contralateral tremor; Claude), subarachnoid (PCom aneurysm, uncal herniation), cavernous sinus (with 4, 5, 6 and sympathetic involvement), orbital (divisional).

Causes

  • Microvascular (diabetes, hypertension: pupil spared, painful, recovers in 3 months), aneurysm (pupil involving), trauma, tumour, inflammation (Tolosa–Hunt), ophthalmoplegic migraine, congenital.

Aberrant regeneration

  • After trauma, aneurysm or compression (not microvascular): lid elevation on adduction or downgaze (pseudo-Graefe sign), pupil constriction on adduction, retraction on vertical gaze; primary aberrant regeneration suggests a slowly growing cavernous sinus lesion.

Treatment

  • Urgent CTA or MRA for pupil-involving or incomplete palsies; treat the cause; prisms or occlusion for diplopia; surgery after 6–12 months of stability (large recess-resect, superior oblique transposition, globe tethering); ptosis surgery carefully (risk of exposure).

NO 32. Fourth nerve palsy — clinical features. DNB / UNIV · outline

Fourth nerve palsy

  • Commonest cause of isolated vertical diplopia; trauma (commonest acquired cause, bilateral common), congenital (decompensated), microvascular, tumours.

Clinical features

  • Vertical and torsional diplopia, worse looking down and to the opposite side (reading, stairs); hypertropia of the affected eye increasing on contralateral gaze and ipsilateral head tilt (Parks–Bielschowsky three-step test); excyclotorsion (double Maddox rod; over 10° suggests bilateral); compensatory head tilt to the opposite shoulder, chin down.
  • Congenital: large vertical fusional amplitudes, facial asymmetry, old photographs show head tilt.

Management

  • Observe for 6 months in acquired cases; prisms, occlusion; surgery (IO weakening, SO tuck, contralateral IR recession, Harada–Ito for torsion).

NO 33. Sixth nerve palsy — aetiology, clinical features, investigations and management; sequelae of lateral rectus palsy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Aetiology

  • Microvascular (diabetes, hypertension: older adults), raised ICP (false localising sign), trauma, tumours (clivus, cerebellopontine angle, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), aneurysm, cavernous sinus disease, inflammation (Gradenigo syndrome: petrous apicitis with facial pain and otitis), MS, Wernicke, Duane syndrome (congenital mimic), children: post-viral benign sixth nerve palsy, pontine glioma.

Clinical features

  • Horizontal uncrossed diplopia worse at distance and on gaze to the affected side, esotropia, abduction deficit, face turn towards the affected side.

Investigations

  • BP, blood sugar, ESR and CRP (over 50: GCA), MRI brain with contrast (young patients, other neurological signs, no recovery in 3 months, children), lumbar puncture if raised ICP suspected.

Management

  • Observe microvascular palsy (recovers in 3 months), prisms, occlusion, botulinum toxin to the ipsilateral MR (prevent contracture); surgery after 6–12 months: recess-resect for partial palsy, transposition procedures (Hummelsheim, Jensen, full tendon, superior rectus transposition) for complete palsy.

Sequelae of LR palsy

  • Contracture of the ipsilateral MR, overaction of the contralateral MR (secondary deviation: greater when fixing with the paretic eye), spread of comitance with time, inhibitional palsy of the contralateral LR.

NO 34. Describe the investigations and management of a case of ophthalmoplegia. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ophthalmoplegia: approach

  • Unilateral or bilateral, painful or painless, pupil involvement, isolated nerve or multiple nerves, variability (myasthenia), proptosis (orbit), systemic features.

Investigations

  • Blood sugar, BP, ESR, CRP (GCA), thyroid function and antibodies (TED), acetylcholine receptor and MuSK antibodies, ice pack test, single-fibre EMG, CT or MRI brain and orbits, MRA or CTA (aneurysm), lumbar puncture, mitochondrial genetics (CPEO).

Causes

  • Myasthenia gravis, thyroid eye disease, CPEO and Kearns–Sayre, cranial nerve palsies (microvascular, aneurysm, tumour), cavernous sinus disease, orbital inflammation, Miller Fisher syndrome, botulism, Wernicke encephalopathy.

Management

  • Treat the cause; diplopia relief with prisms, occlusion, botulinum toxin; strabismus surgery after stabilisation.

NO 35. Types of eye movements and their supranuclear control; gaze palsies, internuclear ophthalmoplegia and one-and-a-half syndrome. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types of eye movements

  • Saccades (frontal eye fields, PPRF for horizontal, riMLF for vertical), smooth pursuit (parieto-occipital, cerebellum), vergence, vestibulo-ocular reflex, optokinetic, fixation.

Supranuclear control

  • Horizontal: frontal eye field (contralateral saccades) → PPRF → sixth nerve nucleus → ipsilateral LR, and via MLF to the contralateral MR subnucleus.
  • Vertical: riMLF and interstitial nucleus of Cajal in the midbrain.

Gaze palsies

  • Horizontal: frontal (eyes look toward the lesion), pontine (away from the lesion), sixth nucleus lesion (ipsilateral gaze palsy).
  • Vertical: Parinaud (dorsal midbrain): upgaze palsy, light–near dissociation, convergence-retraction nystagmus, lid retraction (Collier sign); PSP (downgaze).

INO

  • MLF lesion: ipsilateral adduction deficit with contralateral abducting nystagmus, preserved convergence; MS (young, bilateral), stroke (elderly, unilateral).

One-and-a-half syndrome

  • PPRF or sixth nucleus plus ipsilateral MLF: ipsilateral gaze palsy and INO; only abduction of the contralateral eye remains.

NO 36. Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO)

  • Mitochondrial myopathy (single large deletions of mtDNA, POLG and other nuclear genes); slowly progressive, bilateral, symmetrical ptosis and ophthalmoplegia; diplopia uncommon (symmetrical); pupils spared; orbicularis weakness.

Kearns–Sayre syndrome

  • Onset before 20 years: CPEO, pigmentary retinopathy (salt-and-pepper), heart block (pacemaker), plus cerebellar ataxia, raised CSF protein.

Diagnosis

  • Muscle biopsy (ragged red fibres, COX-negative fibres), genetic testing, ECG, serum lactate, MRI.

Differential diagnosis

  • Myasthenia gravis (variable, fatigable), oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy, myotonic dystrophy, thyroid eye disease, progressive supranuclear palsy (vertical gaze, supranuclear).

Management

  • No cure; ptosis surgery with caution (frontalis sling, conservative lift: poor Bell phenomenon), lubricants, prisms; cardiac surveillance; coenzyme Q10 (limited evidence).
NO 37. Painful ophthalmoplegia — differential diagnosis (Tolosa-Hunt syndrome). DNB / UNIV · outline

Painful ophthalmoplegia: differential diagnosis

  • Orbital: orbital cellulitis, idiopathic orbital inflammation, thyroid eye disease (acute), orbital apex syndrome (mucormycosis), tumours (metastases, lymphoma).
  • Cavernous sinus and superior orbital fissure: Tolosa–Hunt syndrome, carotid-cavernous fistula, cavernous sinus thrombosis, aneurysm of the intracavernous ICA, pituitary apoplexy, tumours (meningioma, metastases, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), herpes zoster.
  • Posterior: posterior communicating artery aneurysm (painful third nerve palsy), microvascular cranial nerve palsy (diabetes: painful), giant cell arteritis.
  • Others: ophthalmoplegic migraine (recurrent painful ophthalmoplegic neuropathy), Gradenigo syndrome, sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease.

Tolosa–Hunt syndrome

  • Idiopathic granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus or SOF; unilateral orbital pain with ipsilateral 3, 4, 6 and V1 palsies; MRI: enhancing tissue in the cavernous sinus; dramatic response to steroids (within 72 hours); diagnosis of exclusion; relapses possible.
NO 38. Myasthenia gravis — pathogenesis, ocular aspects, diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathogenesis

  • Autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors (or MuSK, LRP4) at the neuromuscular junction; thymic hyperplasia or thymoma.

Ocular aspects

  • Ocular involvement in most (about 50% start with ocular symptoms); variable fatigable ptosis and diplopia, Cogan lid twitch, enhanced ptosis (Hering), any pattern of ophthalmoplegia; pupils spared; about half of ocular cases generalise within 2 years.

Diagnosis

  • Ice pack test, sleep test, fatiguability, AChR and MuSK antibodies, repetitive nerve stimulation, single-fibre EMG (most sensitive), CT chest (thymoma), thyroid function.

Management

  • Pyridostigmine (symptomatic), steroids (start low, increase slowly), azathioprine and other immunosuppressants, thymectomy (MGTX trial benefit in generalised AChR-positive MG), IVIG and plasma exchange for crises, newer agents (efgartigimod, eculizumab, rozanolixizumab); prisms and ptosis crutches; strabismus surgery for stable deficits.

NO 39. Cavernous sinus — depict and discuss its ophthalmic connections; cavernous sinus thrombosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Cavernous sinus

  • Paired venous sinuses on either side of the sella; contains the ICA and sixth nerve within it; third, fourth, ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) nerves in its lateral wall; sympathetic fibres on the ICA.

Ophthalmic connections

  • Superior and inferior ophthalmic veins drain into it; communicates with the pterygoid plexus, facial veins (danger triangle), petrosal sinuses and the opposite sinus.

Cavernous sinus syndrome

  • Ophthalmoplegia (3, 4, 6), V1 and V2 sensory loss, Horner syndrome, proptosis and chemosis.

Cavernous sinus thrombosis

  • Septic (from facial, sinus or dental infection: Staphylococcus aureus, mucormycosis) or aseptic.
  • Features: fever, headache, bilateral proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, papilloedema, reduced vision.
  • Management: high-dose IV antibiotics (or antifungals), treat source, anticoagulation (debated), steroids for adrenal insufficiency; high mortality.
NO 40. Carotid-cavernous fistula. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Carotid-cavernous fistula

  • Abnormal communication between the carotid system and the cavernous sinus.
  • Direct (type A, high flow): trauma or ruptured aneurysm; sudden onset.
  • Indirect (dural; Barrow types B, C, D, low flow): spontaneous in older women, hypertension.

Features

  • Pulsatile proptosis, orbital bruit, arterialised corkscrew conjunctival vessels, chemosis, raised IOP, ophthalmoplegia (sixth nerve commonest), venous stasis retinopathy, ischaemia.

Investigations

  • CT or MRI (dilated superior ophthalmic vein, enlarged muscles), Doppler (reversed flow), digital subtraction angiography (gold standard).

Management

  • Direct: endovascular coil or balloon occlusion.
  • Indirect: observation (many close spontaneously), intermittent carotid compression (manual), endovascular embolisation via the superior ophthalmic vein if vision threatened; control IOP.
NO 51. Skew deviation vs fourth nerve palsy — the upright-supine test. NEW · outline

Skew deviation

  • Vertical misalignment due to imbalance of otolith (utricular) inputs in the brainstem or cerebellum (supranuclear); part of the ocular tilt reaction (head tilt, skew, ocular torsion).
  • Causes: brainstem or cerebellar stroke, MS, tumours, trauma.

Fourth nerve palsy

  • Peripheral weakness of the superior oblique: hypertropia increasing on contralateral gaze and ipsilateral head tilt, excyclotorsion of the hypertropic eye.

Distinguishing features

  • Parks three-step test may be positive in both.
  • Torsion: fourth nerve palsy shows excyclotorsion of the higher eye; skew often shows incyclotorsion of the higher eye (ocular tilt reaction).
  • Upright-supine test (Wong): the vertical deviation decreases by 50% or more when lying supine in skew deviation (otolith input changes); unchanged in fourth nerve palsy.
  • Associated neurological signs (nystagmus, INO, ataxia, Horner, hemisensory loss) point to skew.

Management

  • Skew: urgent neuroimaging (MRI with DWI), stroke evaluation; prisms or occlusion for diplopia; often improves.

NO 41. Nystagmus — types, investigations, associated conditions and management; optokinetic nystagmus. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Physiological: end-point, optokinetic, vestibular (caloric).
  • Infantile (congenital motor or sensory, latent and manifest latent, spasmus nutans).

NO 42. Migraine — types, ocular migraine and differential diagnosis of visual phenomena. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Migraine

  • Without aura (common), with aura (classic), retinal migraine (monocular), hemiplegic, basilar; childhood periodic syndromes.

Ocular migraine and visual aura

  • Fortification spectra (scintillating scotoma), zigzag lines spreading over 5–60 minutes, followed by headache; cortical spreading depression; binocular.
  • Retinal migraine: monocular, repeated episodes of vision loss; exclude amaurosis fugax (embolic).

Differential diagnosis of visual phenomena

  • Transient monocular loss: amaurosis fugax (carotid disease), GCA, retinal migraine, transient visual obscurations (papilloedema), angle closure (haloes), retinal vasospasm.
  • Flashes: PVD and retinal tears (unilateral, persistent), migraine (binocular, moving).
  • Visual snow, occipital seizures (coloured circles, brief), TIA (sudden, no march), Charles Bonnet syndrome (formed images in visual loss).

Management

  • Avoid triggers, analgesics and triptans for attacks, prophylaxis (propranolol, amitriptyline, topiramate, flunarizine), CGRP antagonists; neuro-imaging for atypical features.
NO 43. Critically evaluate the differential diagnosis of failing vision in an elderly patient; sudden painless profound loss of vision in a young and an elderly patient. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Failing vision in an elderly patient (gradual)

  • Refractive (presbyopia, index myopia), cataract, glaucoma, AMD, diabetic retinopathy, ERM, compressive optic neuropathy, corneal dystrophy (Fuchs), drug toxicity, nutritional optic neuropathy.

Sudden painless profound loss in the elderly

  • CRAO and BRAO, CRVO, NAION, AAION (GCA: urgent ESR and steroids), vitreous haemorrhage, RD, wet AMD (submacular haemorrhage), stroke (occipital).

Sudden painless loss in a young patient

  • RD, vitreous haemorrhage (diabetes, Eales), optic neuritis (usually painful), CSCR, LHON, retinal artery occlusion (cardiac emboli, thrombophilia), toxic (methanol), functional vision loss.

Approach

  • History (onset, pain, transient episodes, systemic disease), vision, RAPD, fundus, OCT, fields, B-scan, ESR and CRP, carotid Doppler, echocardiography, MRI; treat the cause urgently where possible.

NO 44. Malingering — detection. · outline

Malingering and functional vision loss

  • Malingering: deliberate feigning for gain; functional (non-organic) vision loss: not consciously produced.

Detection for unilateral vision loss

  • Normal pupils (no RAPD with claimed profound unilateral loss), fogging test (high plus lens over the good eye), prism dissociation test (4 prism dioptres base-down), red-green glasses with duochrome or Worth four-dot, stereoacuity (good stereopsis implies good vision in both eyes), Snellen at varying distances, OKN drum, mirror test (eyes follow a moving mirror), VEP and ERG.

Bilateral claimed blindness

  • Mirror test, OKN, menace reflex, normal pupils and fundi, proprioception tests (touch fingertips: blind people can do it), signature writing, observing navigation.

Visual fields

  • Tubular (non-expanding) fields on tangent screen at different distances, spiralling fields on Goldmann, inconsistent results.

Management

  • Reassure, avoid confrontation, document objective findings, rule out organic disease (OCT, MRI, electrophysiology).
NO 45. Ocular manifestations of head injury vis-à-vis their role in management. · outline

Ocular manifestations of head injury

  • Orbit and adnexa: lid lacerations, orbital fractures, retrobulbar haemorrhage (orbital compartment syndrome), CSF leak.
  • Globe: hyphaema, commotio, open globe, retinal haemorrhages (abusive head trauma in infants), Purtscher retinopathy, Terson syndrome.
  • Optic nerve: traumatic optic neuropathy, avulsion, chiasmal injury (bitemporal field defect, diabetes insipidus).
  • Pupils: unilateral fixed dilated pupil (uncal herniation compressing the third nerve: neurosurgical emergency, Hutchinson pupil), Horner (carotid dissection), traumatic mydriasis (local damage).
  • Motility: third, fourth (commonest cranial nerve injured in head trauma), sixth nerve palsies, skew deviation, gaze palsies.
  • Cortical: homonymous defects, cortical blindness.
  • Papilloedema (raised ICP from oedema or haematoma: later).

Role in management

  • Pupil signs (Glasgow Coma Scale adjunct) guide neurosurgical intervention; vision assessment and RAPD for traumatic optic neuropathy and orbital compartment syndrome (immediate canthotomy); retinal haemorrhages document non-accidental injury; diplopia rehabilitation.
NO 46. Essential blepharospasm. DNB / UNIV · outline

Essential (benign) blepharospasm

  • Focal dystonia: bilateral involuntary spasms of the orbicularis oculi; women over 50; worse with stress, bright light, fatigue; relieved by sensory tricks (touching the face), sleep, concentration.

Associations

  • Meige syndrome (with oromandibular dystonia), dry eye and photophobia, apraxia of lid opening.

Differential diagnosis

  • Hemifacial spasm (unilateral, involves lower face; vascular compression of the facial nerve: MRI), facial myokymia, reflex blepharospasm (ocular surface disease), tics, Parkinson disease, drug-induced (levodopa, neuroleptics), functional.

Management

  • Botulinum toxin A injections (pretarsal or preseptal orbicularis, every 3–4 months; first-line), treat dry eye, tinted glasses (FL-41), oral drugs (clonazepam, trihexyphenidyl, baclofen: limited), surgery (myectomy) for refractory cases, deep brain stimulation (Meige).

NO 47. Paraneoplastic syndromes of the eye. DNB / UNIV · outline

Paraneoplastic syndromes of the eye

  • Immune-mediated damage from antibodies against tumour antigens that cross-react with ocular tissue.

Cancer-associated retinopathy (CAR)

  • Small cell lung cancer, breast, gynaecological; anti-recoverin antibodies; rapid bilateral vision loss, photopsia, ring scotomas, nyctalopia, normal-looking fundus early, attenuated vessels; ERG reduced (rods and cones).

Melanoma-associated retinopathy (MAR)

  • Metastatic cutaneous melanoma; antibodies against bipolar cells (TRPM1); shimmering lights, night blindness; negative ERG (reduced b-wave).

Bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation (BDUMP)

  • Ovarian, uterine, lung cancers; multiple pigmented uveal tumours, rapid cataract, exudative RD.

Paraneoplastic optic neuropathy and neuro-ophthalmic syndromes

  • CRMP-5 antibodies (optic neuropathy with vitritis), opsoclonus-myoclonus (neuroblastoma in children, breast and lung in adults: anti-Ri), Lambert–Eaton syndrome (ptosis), cerebellar degeneration (nystagmus).

Management

  • Find and treat the cancer, immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, plasmapheresis, rituximab); prognosis for vision often poor.
NO 48. Localising signs of intracranial tumours; ocular aspects of cerebellopontine angle lesions and facial palsy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Localising signs of intracranial tumours

  • Frontal: Foster Kennedy syndrome, anosmia, gaze deviation (frontal eye fields).
  • Temporal: superior homonymous quadrantanopia, uncal herniation (third nerve palsy).
  • Parietal: inferior homonymous quadrantanopia, asymmetrical OKN, visual neglect.
  • Occipital: congruous homonymous hemianopia, visual hallucinations.
  • Pituitary and parasellar: bitemporal hemianopia, see-saw nystagmus, cavernous sinus palsies.
  • Pineal and dorsal midbrain: Parinaud syndrome.
  • Brainstem: gaze palsies, INO, cranial nerve palsies, skew deviation.
  • Cerebellum: nystagmus, ocular dysmetria, papilloedema (hydrocephalus).
  • False localising signs: sixth nerve palsy, papilloedema.

Cerebellopontine angle lesions (acoustic neuroma, meningioma)

  • Reduced corneal reflex (fifth nerve; earliest sign), sixth nerve palsy, facial nerve palsy (lagophthalmos, exposure keratopathy), nystagmus (Bruns nystagmus: gaze-evoked towards the lesion, vestibular away), papilloedema, hearing loss and tinnitus; NF2 (bilateral).

Facial palsy: ocular aspects

  • Lagophthalmos, exposure keratopathy (worse with corneal anaesthesia: neurotrophic), paralytic ectropion, epiphora (pump failure), crocodile tears (aberrant regeneration), synkinesis.
  • Management: lubricants, taping, moisture chamber, tarsorrhaphy, gold weight, lateral tarsal strip, botulinum-induced ptosis, facial reanimation.

NO 52. Visual snow syndrome. NEW · outline

Visual snow syndrome

  • Continuous tiny flickering dots across the entire visual field (like static on a TV) lasting more than 3 months, with at least two additional visual symptoms: palinopsia, enhanced entoptic phenomena (floaters, blue field phenomenon), photophobia, nyctalopia (impaired night vision).
  • Young adults; often with migraine, tinnitus, anxiety or depression; may follow hallucinogen use (HPPD as a differential).

Pathophysiology

  • Cortical hyperexcitability (visual cortex and lingual gyrus hypermetabolism on PET), thalamocortical dysrhythmia; normal eye examination.

Evaluation

  • Diagnosis of exclusion: full eye examination, OCT, fields, ERG (to exclude retinal disease such as CAR or retinal dystrophy), MRI in atypical cases, drug history.

Management

  • Reassurance and explanation (non-progressive, no vision loss), treat migraine and anxiety, tinted lenses (FL-41 or blue-yellow), lamotrigine (limited benefit), mindfulness and CBT.

NO 53. Neuroimaging for the ophthalmologist — CT vs MRI vs angiography: which test, when. NEW · outline

CT scan

  • Fast, widely available; best for bone (orbital fractures, canal fractures), calcification (retinoblastoma, drusen, meningioma), acute haemorrhage, metallic foreign bodies, sinus disease, orbital cellulitis and abscess; radiation dose (avoid repeated CT in children and heritable retinoblastoma).
  • CT angiography: aneurysms (pupil-involving third nerve palsy), carotid dissection; CT venography for venous sinus thrombosis.

MRI

  • Best for soft tissues: optic nerve (fat-suppressed, contrast-enhanced orbits), chiasm and pituitary (dedicated sella protocol), brainstem, demyelination, posterior fossa; DWI for acute stroke (skew, gaze palsies, CRAO-related stroke screening); MRV for IIH and venous thrombosis; MRA for aneurysms; contraindicated with metallic IOFB and some implants.

Which test, when (examples)

  • Orbital trauma or suspected IOFB: CT orbits.
  • Optic neuritis: MRI brain and orbits with gadolinium.
  • Papilloedema: urgent CT, then MRI with MRV.
  • Bitemporal field defect: MRI pituitary.
  • Pupil-involving third nerve palsy: CTA or MRA urgently.
  • Painful Horner syndrome: CTA or MRA head and neck.
  • Isolated sixth nerve palsy in a young person: MRI brain.
  • Proptosis: CT for bone and orbital cellulitis; MRI for tumour characterisation and apex.

Good practice

  • Write the clinical question and the area of interest on the request; speak to the radiologist; review images yourself.
