History of Ophthalmology: Timelines, Pioneers and Eponyms

Last updated: October 2026

The milestones and people behind modern ophthalmology, arranged as timelines by subspecialty, followed by short profiles of the pioneers and the people behind the eponyms we use every day.

The people behind the eponyms

Jan Evangelista Purkinje (Purkyně), 1787–1869

Czech physiologist known for Purkinje cells (cerebellum), Purkinje fibres (heart) and the Purkinje images. He founded the world's first department of physiology (Breslau, 1839), introduced the term plasma, described sweat glands and recognised fingerprints as a means of identification. He studied vision by experimenting on himself, even with belladonna, and was a friend of Goethe and a translator of Schiller and Shakespeare into Czech.

Friedrich Gustav Jakob Henle, 1809–1885

German anatomist behind Henle's fibre layer, Hassall–Henle bodies and the loop of Henle. His Allgemeine Anatomie was the first systematic work on histology, and he was among the first to use the term "cell". A talented musician, he was briefly imprisoned in 1835 for his liberal politics.

Hermann von Helmholtz, 1821–1894

Physician and physicist who invented the ophthalmoscope while preparing a lecture in 1850 to explain the glow of animals' eyes. On 17 December 1850 he wrote to his father that he had made "a discovery which may be of the utmost importance in ophthalmology". On first seeing the living fundus, von Graefe is said to have exclaimed, "Helmholtz has unfolded to us a new world!"

Douglas Argyll Robertson, 1837–1909

Scottish ophthalmologist and pupil of von Graefe who described the small, irregular pupils of neurosyphilis that accommodate but do not react to light. In 1862 he dropped Calabar bean extract into his own eye and showed that physostigmine constricts the pupil. He died in 1909 at Gondal, in Saurashtra (Gujarat), while visiting his friend the Thakore Sahib of Gondal, and was cremated there.

Where residents slip

  • Saying Helmholtz invented the first ophthalmoscope: Babbage built one earlier, but Helmholtz's was the one that changed practice.
  • Dating the first IOL to 1950: Ridley's first implant was on 29 November 1949 (a two-stage procedure completed in 1950).
  • Calling Gullstrand's Nobel Prize one of several: he remains the only ophthalmologist to win it.
