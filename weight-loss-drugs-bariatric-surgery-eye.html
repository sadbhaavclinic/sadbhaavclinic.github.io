Weight-Loss Medicines, Bariatric Surgery and the Eye: GLP-1 Drugs, Diabetic Retinopathy, NAION, IIH and Vitamin Deficiency

For: PG residents · Practising ophthalmologists · Patients and families

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) and tirzepatide are now widely used for diabetes and obesity, so eye clinics see many patients taking them.
  • Rapid falls in HbA1c can cause early worsening of diabetic retinopathy; examine the retina before starting and follow closely in the first year if retinopathy is present.
  • Semaglutide has been linked to NAION; the European Medicines Agency lists it as a very rare side effect, and in November 2026 the US FDA added a NAION warning to every GLP-1 receptor agonist, tirzepatide included.
  • In idiopathic intracranial hypertension, weight loss is disease-modifying; bariatric surgery and GLP-1 drugs both have trial evidence.
  • After bariatric surgery, look for vitamin A deficiency (night blindness, xerophthalmia), and also B1, B12 and copper deficiency causing ocular motor or optic nerve problems.

"I have started weekly injections for weight loss. Will they affect my eyes?" Eye doctors now hear this every week. The answer depends on the patient: a person with diabetic retinopathy, a young woman with papilloedema, a man with a crowded disc, and a patient years after gastric bypass each face a different eye issue.

Diabetic retinopathy: early worsening

In the SUSTAIN-6 trial, semaglutide was associated with more retinopathy complications than placebo, mostly in patients who already had retinopathy and whose HbA1c fell quickly. The same "early worsening" was seen decades ago with intensive insulin therapy in the DCCT and is thought to reflect the speed of glucose lowering rather than a direct toxic effect. Long-term, better glucose control protects the retina. The FOCUS trial is studying the effect of semaglutide on retinopathy directly.

Practical approach

  1. Ask every diabetic patient about GLP-1 or tirzepatide treatment, and when it started.
  2. Examine the retina (with OCT if possible) before or soon after starting, especially if HbA1c is high or retinopathy is known.
  3. If there is moderate or worse non-proliferative retinopathy, proliferative retinopathy or macular oedema, review every 3 to 4 months in the first year.
  4. Treat sight-threatening disease as usual. Do not ask the physician to stop the drug without good reason; discuss a slower reduction in HbA1c if retinopathy is advanced.
  5. Expect temporary changes in refraction while glucose is changing; delay new spectacles until it is stable.

NAION

Update, November 2026: on 9 November 2026 the US FDA required a warning about a potential increased risk of NAION in the labels of all GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus), liraglutide (Victoza, Saxenda), dulaglutide (Trulicity), exenatide, tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) and oral orforglipron. The FDA calls NAION rare and the risk "potential", not proven: in a pooled analysis of 126 trials the rate was about 6.4 per 100,000 person-years on GLP-1 drugs against 3.8 on placebo. Patients should report sudden, painless loss of vision or of part of the field in one eye straight away; if NAION is confirmed, the prescriber should consider stopping the drug unless the benefits clearly outweigh the risk. The European review (2025) covered semaglutide only.

Observational studies since 2024 have reported a higher rate of non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy in people taking semaglutide, and in 2025 the European Medicines Agency concluded that NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide medicines (up to 1 in 10,000 people). Patients with a crowded disc, sleep apnoea or previous NAION in one eye may deserve a discussion with their physician. The details are in semaglutide and NAION.

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension

  • Weight gain is a major risk factor, and weight loss of around 5 to 15% often brings remission.
  • The IIH:WT trial showed that bariatric surgery lowered intracranial pressure more than a community weight-management programme, with the effect lasting at two years.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce CSF secretion in laboratory studies; exenatide lowered ICP in a small trial, and observational studies of semaglutide and tirzepatide in IIH are encouraging.
  • Weight loss is a long-term plan. It does not replace urgent treatment (acetazolamide, CSF shunting or optic nerve sheath fenestration) when vision is threatened.

Night blindness or dry, gritty eyes after bariatric surgery is vitamin A deficiency until proved otherwise. Check serum retinol, start replacement with the physician, and look for corneal involvement. Untreated xerophthalmia can progress to corneal melt.

Other practical points

  • Anaesthesia: GLP-1 drugs slow gastric emptying and may increase aspiration risk under general anaesthesia or sedation. Follow your anaesthesia department's current policy; most cataract surgery under topical or local anaesthesia is unaffected.
  • Refraction: fluctuating glucose with rapid weight loss changes the refraction; avoid prescribing until stable.
  • Topiramate: any patient on a weight-loss combination containing topiramate who develops sudden bilateral blur, myopia or eye pain needs an urgent angle assessment.

Pearls

  • Ask about weight-loss injections in every diabetic and every patient with a swollen or crowded disc.
  • Rapid HbA1c reduction plus existing retinopathy: follow closely in the first year.
  • Weight loss is disease-modifying in IIH; refer to weight-management services early.
  • Night blindness after gastric bypass: think vitamin A.
  • Sudden bilateral myopia and angle closure on a weight-loss pill: think topiramate.

Common mistakes

  • Advising patients to stop GLP-1 drugs because of early retinopathy worsening without discussing it with the physician.
  • Prescribing new spectacles during rapid changes in glucose.
  • Missing vitamin A deficiency because surgery was "many years ago".
  • Relying on weight loss alone when IIH is threatening vision.
