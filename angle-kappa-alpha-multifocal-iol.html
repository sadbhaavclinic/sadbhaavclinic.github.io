Angle Kappa vs Angle Alpha: Why They Matter for Multifocal IOLs

Last updated: October 2026

Short answer: angle kappa (measured clinically as chord mu) and angle alpha (measured as chord alpha) describe how far the patient's visual axis lies from the centre of the pupil and the centre of the limbus. If they are large, the visual axis may pass off-centre through a multifocal IOL's rings, causing glare, haloes and poorer quality of vision. Many surgeons become cautious at values above about 0.5–0.6 mm.

The axes in plain language

TermWhat it isClinical measurement
Angle kappaAngle between the pupillary axis and the visual axisChord mu: distance (mm) between the pupil centre and the corneal light reflex seen coaxially while the patient fixates
Angle alphaAngle between the visual axis and the optical axis of the eyeChord alpha: distance (mm) between the corneal vertex and the centre of the limbus (white-to-white)
Angle lambdaAngle between the pupillary axis and the line of sightPractically very similar to angle kappa; the terms are often used interchangeably

Why it matters for multifocal and EDOF IOLs

  • A multifocal IOL sits centred in the capsular bag, roughly in line with the limbal centre, not the pupil centre.
  • Light enters the eye along the visual axis. If that axis is far from the IOL centre, it passes through the edge of the central zone or the diffractive rings.
  • The result can be haloes, glare, reduced contrast and lower uncorrected near or distance vision, even when the IOL is perfectly centred in the bag.
  • Monofocal IOLs are much more tolerant of this offset.

Chord mu or chord alpha: which should you use?

  • Chord alpha is generally considered the more reliable predictor. It is referenced to the limbus, which is where the IOL will centre, and it does not change with pupil size, dilation or cataract removal.
  • Chord mu depends on the pupil centre, which moves with pupil size and lighting and can change after surgery.
  • Look at the direction as well as the size of the offset; some studies suggest the orientation of chord alpha also affects outcomes.

Practical thresholds

  • A pragmatic approach in the literature is to be cautious about multifocal IOLs when chord values exceed roughly 0.5–0.6 mm.
  • Thresholds depend on the device and the IOL design. Trifocal and diffractive lenses are typically less tolerant than some EDOF designs.
  • The evidence is still debated, and a large kappa alone should not automatically exclude a patient. Combine it with pupil size, corneal higher-order aberrations, ocular surface health and the patient's expectations.

How to measure it

  • Chord mu is reported by devices such as the Pentacam, Galilei, Orbscan, iTrace and IOLMaster 700.
  • Chord alpha is reported by devices such as the IOLMaster 700, Galilei and iTrace.
  • Values are not interchangeable between devices, and degrees cannot be compared directly with millimetres. Use one device consistently.
  • Measure under the same lighting conditions every time, because pupil-based values change with pupil size.

Clinical pearls

  • Hyperopic eyes tend to have larger angle kappa than myopic eyes.
  • A large angle kappa can make an eye look as if it has a small exotropia (pseudo-exotropia) on the Hirschberg test.
  • For laser refractive surgery, centration choice (pupil centre vs corneal vertex) is another situation where angle kappa matters.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal angle kappa?

Most eyes have a small positive angle kappa, with chord mu values typically well below 0.5 mm. Values differ between devices, so compare a patient with the normal range for the device you use.

Is chord alpha better than angle kappa for multifocal IOL selection?

Chord alpha is generally considered more reliable, because it is referenced to the limbal centre, where the IOL centres, and it does not change with pupil size or after cataract removal.

Can you implant a multifocal IOL if angle kappa is high?

It is possible, but the risk of haloes, glare and reduced quality of vision is higher. Many surgeons prefer a monofocal or a more tolerant EDOF design when chord values are above about 0.5–0.6 mm, after discussing the trade-offs with the patient.
