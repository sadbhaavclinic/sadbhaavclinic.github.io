Retina OSCE Stations: 10 Spot Stations on OCT, Fundus Pictures, B-scan, Injections and Tamponade

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 10 spot stations, 40 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 10 original stations cover the retina topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. OCT in wet AMD, 2. Macular hole, 3. Central retinal artery occlusion, 4. Central retinal vein occlusion, 5. Grading diabetic retinopathy, 6. Horseshoe retinal tear, 7. B-scan ultrasound, 8. Intravitreal injection, 9. Gas and silicone oil, 10. ROP screening

Station 1: OCT in wet AMD

The examiner shows you: A macular OCT: a dome-shaped elevation of the RPE with hyperreflective material above it, subretinal fluid and a few intraretinal cysts.

  1. Describe and diagnose.
    A fibrovascular pigment epithelial detachment with subretinal hyperreflective material, subretinal fluid and intraretinal fluid: active neovascular AMD.
  2. Name the types of macular neovascularisation.
    Type 1 (beneath the RPE, the old "occult"), type 2 (above the RPE, the old "classic") and type 3 (retinal angiomatous proliferation).
  3. Which fluid matters most for vision?
    Intraretinal fluid is linked to worse vision; some stable subretinal fluid may be tolerated in treat-and-extend regimens.
  4. What is the treatment?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept or faricimab), usually monthly loading then treat-and-extend.

Station 2: Macular hole

The examiner shows you: An OCT: a full-thickness defect in the fovea with cystic edges and a minimum width of 350 µm; the vitreous is still attached to the hole edge.

  1. What is the diagnosis and stage?
    A full-thickness macular hole, medium sized by the International Vitreomacular Traction Study classification (250–400 µm), with vitreomacular traction.
  2. Name the classic symptom and test.
    Central blur and distortion; the Watzke–Allen test, in which a thin slit beam appears broken.
  3. What is the treatment?
    Pars plana vitrectomy, internal limiting membrane peeling, gas tamponade and face-down positioning; large holes may need an inverted ILM flap.
  4. Which factor best predicts closure?
    Hole size (minimum linear diameter): smaller holes close more often; duration also matters.

Station 3: Central retinal artery occlusion

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph: whitened, opaque posterior pole with a cherry-red spot at the fovea and attenuated arterioles with segmented ("box-carring") blood columns.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Central retinal artery occlusion.
  2. Why is there a cherry-red spot?
    The fovea has no inner retina, so the normal choroidal circulation shows through, surrounded by opaque ischaemic inner retina.
  3. What is your urgent priority?
    Treat it as an eye stroke: urgent referral for stroke assessment (carotid imaging, cardiac work-up, blood pressure, glucose, lipids). In older patients exclude giant cell arteritis with ESR and CRP the same day.
  4. Is ocular massage or paracentesis effective?
    No good evidence that they change outcome; thrombolysis within a few hours is being studied in trials.

Station 4: Central retinal vein occlusion

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph: dilated, tortuous veins, flame and dot haemorrhages in all four quadrants, disc swelling and cotton wool spots.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Central retinal vein occlusion.
  2. How do you tell ischaemic from non-ischaemic?
    Ischaemic: poor vision (often worse than 6/60), RAPD, extensive haemorrhage and cotton wool spots, more than 10 disc areas of capillary non-perfusion on angiography, reduced ERG b-wave.
  3. Which systemic tests do you do?
    Blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c, lipids, full blood count and ESR; in younger patients, thrombophilia and other tests guided by history.
  4. What does follow-up look for, and how do you treat?
    Neovascularisation of the iris and angle (gonioscopy) for the first 3–6 months; PRP if it appears. Treat macular oedema with anti-VEGF or a dexamethasone implant.

Station 5: Grading diabetic retinopathy

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph: dot and blot haemorrhages in four quadrants (more than 20 in each), venous beading in two quadrants, no new vessels.

  1. Grade the retinopathy.
    Severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
  2. What is the 4-2-1 rule?
    Severe NPDR if any one of: haemorrhages in 4 quadrants, venous beading in 2 or more quadrants, or IRMA in 1 or more quadrant.
  3. What are high-risk characteristics?
    NVD of a quarter to a third of a disc area or more, any NVD with vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage, or NVE of half a disc area or more with haemorrhage (from DRS).
  4. How would you manage this patient?
    Close follow-up (about every 2–3 months), consider PRP in some patients (for example poor attenders or before cataract surgery), and optimise glucose, blood pressure and lipids.

Station 6: Horseshoe retinal tear

The examiner shows you: On indirect ophthalmoscopy with depression: a U-shaped tear at 1 o'clock with its flap pulled anteriorly, a small vitreous haemorrhage and no subretinal fluid.

  1. What do you see?
    A symptomatic horseshoe (flap) retinal tear with vitreous traction.
  2. What is the treatment?
    Barrier laser retinopexy (two to three rows around the tear, extending to the ora anteriorly), or cryotherapy if the view is poor or the tear is very anterior.
  3. Which symptoms should the patient report?
    A shower of new floaters, increasing flashes, or a shadow or curtain in the vision: these need same-day review.
  4. What is Shafer's sign?
    Pigment ("tobacco dust") in the anterior vitreous, strongly suggesting a retinal break.

Station 7: B-scan ultrasound

The examiner shows you: A B-scan of an eye with dense vitreous haemorrhage: a thick, bright membrane attached at the optic disc, which moves little on eye movement and stays bright at low gain.

  1. What does the membrane represent?
    A retinal detachment: attached to the disc, highly reflective (100% spike on A-scan), with restricted after-movement.
  2. How does a posterior vitreous detachment differ?
    A thinner, less reflective membrane that moves freely with eye movements and may or may not be attached at the disc.
  3. How does a choroidal detachment look?
    Smooth, thick, dome-shaped elevations that do not attach to the disc, often "kissing" in the middle of the eye.
  4. When is B-scan essential?
    When the fundus cannot be seen: dense cataract, vitreous haemorrhage, corneal opacity, or after trauma (excluding an intraocular foreign body).

Station 8: Intravitreal injection

The examiner shows you: A sterile tray: speculum, povidone-iodine 5%, calipers, a 30 G needle and a prefilled syringe of anti-VEGF.

  1. Where do you inject?
    3.5–4 mm behind the limbus in pseudophakic or aphakic eyes, 4 mm in phakic eyes (3–3.5 mm in infants for ROP), usually inferotemporally, aiming at the centre of the globe.
  2. What is the single most important step against infection?
    Povidone-iodine 5% on the conjunctiva and lashes, with a lid speculum to keep lashes away; antibiotic drops are not needed.
  3. What do you check after the injection?
    Perfusion of the optic disc or counting-fingers vision, and IOP if needed.
  4. Name serious complications.
    Endophthalmitis (about 1 in 2,000–3,000 injections), retinal tear or detachment, lens touch, raised IOP and, rarely, intraocular inflammation or vasculitis.

Station 9: Gas and silicone oil

The examiner shows you: A post-vitrectomy patient asks when they can fly; the notes say "C3F8 15%". Another eye contains silicone oil.

  1. How long do the gases last?
    Air: about 3–5 days. SF6: about 2 weeks. C3F8: about 6–8 weeks.
  2. What advice about flying and anaesthesia?
    No flying, no travel to high altitude and no nitrous oxide anaesthesia until the gas has fully gone: expansion can cause a dangerous rise in IOP. A warning wristband is usual.
  3. Why choose silicone oil?
    Long-term tamponade for complicated detachments (PVR, giant tears), when the patient cannot posture or must fly, and to allow early vision.
  4. Name complications of silicone oil.
    Raised IOP, emulsification, cataract, band keratopathy and oil in the anterior chamber; it usually needs removing later.

Station 10: ROP screening

The examiner shows you: A neonatal unit chart: baby born at 29 weeks, birth weight 1,200 g, now 4 weeks old.

  1. Does this baby need screening?
    Yes. Indian guidelines screen babies born under 34 weeks or under 1,750 g, and older babies with risk factors; UK and US criteria use lower thresholds (under 31–32 weeks or under 1,500 g).
  2. When should the first examination be?
    By about 4 weeks after birth or 30–31 weeks postmenstrual age, whichever is later (in India, by day 20–30 of life).
  3. What are the zones?
    Zone I: a circle centred on the disc with radius twice the disc–fovea distance. Zone II: from zone I to the nasal ora. Zone III: the remaining temporal crescent.
  4. Which ROP needs treatment?
    Type 1 ROP (ETROP): zone I any stage with plus disease, zone I stage 3 without plus, or zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus. Treat with laser or anti-VEGF.
