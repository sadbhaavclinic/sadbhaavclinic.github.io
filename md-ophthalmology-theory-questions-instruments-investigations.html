MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Instruments, Imaging and Investigations (45 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 45 instruments, imaging and investigations theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Ophthalmoscopy & Slit Lamp

IN 1. Optics of the direct and binocular indirect ophthalmoscope — magnification, field and comparison. Detail the selection criteria for your own ophthalmoscopes, their everyday applications and the care needed for their maintenance. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Direct ophthalmoscope

  • Upright, virtual image; magnification about ×15 (60/4); field about 5–6°; monocular; small pupil possible.

Binocular indirect ophthalmoscope

  • Condensing lens (+20 D: magnification about ×3, field about 45°; +28 D and +30 D: lower magnification, wider field); inverted, real image; stereopsis; with scleral depression shows the ora serrata.

Comparison

  • Direct: detail of disc and macula, no stereopsis, small field. Indirect: wide field, periphery, stereopsis, works through hazy media, needs dilation.

Selection criteria for your own instruments

  • Direct: halogen or LED illumination, rechargeable handle, red-free filter, slit and fixation target, lens range.
  • Indirect: lightweight headband, adjustable interpupillary distance and mirror, small-pupil setting, LED source, wireless option; +20 D and +28 D lenses.

Everyday use and maintenance

  • Fundus screening, RD, ROP, diabetic retinopathy; clean lenses with soft cloth, avoid scratches, check bulbs and batteries, store in cases, disinfect contact surfaces.

Revise from: Indirect Ophthalmoscopy and Scleral Depression: Technique, Lens Choice and Pitfalls, Direct Ophthalmoscopy for Medical Students: Technique, How to Present and a Practice Gallery of Fundus Drawings

IN 2. Optics and magnification of +90D/+78D and 20D lenses; slit-lamp lenses for the central retina. DNB / UNIV · outline

+90 D and +78 D lenses

  • High-power condensing lenses used with the slit lamp for indirect biomicroscopy: produce a real, inverted, laterally reversed image in front of the lens.
  • +90 D: wider field (about 74°), lower magnification (about 0.76×), small pupil use; +78 D: higher magnification (about 0.93×), narrower field; +60 D: macula and disc detail (about 1.15×).
  • Stereoscopic view of the disc and macula.

20 D lens (indirect ophthalmoscopy)

  • Magnification about 3× (60/20), field about 46°; 28 D: wider field (about 53°), lower magnification (2.27×), for small pupils; 30 D for children.

Slit-lamp lenses for the central retina

  • Non-contact: +60, +78, +90 D, Super Field, Digital Wide Field.
  • Contact: Goldmann three-mirror (central lens), Volk Area Centralis, Mainster lenses (laser), Hruby lens (−58.6 D, historical).

Revise from: Ophthalmic Examination Lenses: 90D to 20D, Gonioscopy and Laser Lenses Compared, Slit-Lamp Laser Lenses: Magnification and Spot Size Factors

IN 3. Slit-lamp biomicroscope — optics, illumination techniques (including specular reflection) and accessory lenses. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optics

  • Compound binocular microscope (Galilean magnification changer, ×6–×40) with a slit illumination system on a common pivot (co-pivotal, parfocal).

Illumination techniques

  • Diffuse, direct focal (optic section, parallelepiped, conical beam for cells and flare), indirect, retro-illumination (direct and indirect: from the iris or fundus), sclerotic scatter (central corneal opacities), specular reflection (endothelium; angle of incidence equals angle of reflection), tangential (iris surface).

Accessory lenses

  • Non-contact: +90 D, +78 D, +60 D, Hruby lens. Contact: Goldmann three-mirror, gonioscopes, panfundoscope. Applanation tonometer, pachymeter, camera attachments.

Revise from: Keratometry and Slit Lamp Illumination Techniques for Optometrists, Slit-Lamp Examination: Systematic Technique, Illumination Methods and Grading Scales

IN 4. Prisms in ophthalmic instruments — slit lamp, indirect ophthalmoscope, operating microscope and applanation tonometer. · outline

Prisms in ophthalmic instruments

  • Slit lamp: Porro–Abbe or roof prisms in the binocular microscope erect the image; prisms to converge the eyepieces; beam splitters for observation and photography.
  • Indirect ophthalmoscope: prisms or mirrors reduce interpupillary distance (about 15 mm) so that both eyes see through a small pupil (stereopsis).
  • Operating microscope: inverting prisms (erect image), beam splitters for assistant and camera; stereo-inverter in vitrectomy wide-angle systems.
  • Applanation tonometer: biprism splits the fluorescein ring into two semicircles (doubling principle); inner edges touch when 3.06 mm diameter is flattened.
  • Others: synoptophore and prism bars, prism cover test, keratometers (doubling principle: prisms in Javal–Schiotz and Bausch & Lomb).

Revise from: Instruments in Ophthalmology: Who Invented What

IN 5. Lensometer (focimeter) — principle, including Badal's principle. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Lensometer (focimeter)

  • Measures back vertex power, cylinder axis, prism and optical centre of spectacle lenses.

Badal’s principle

  • The standard lens is placed so that its focal point lies at the back vertex of the test lens; the target movement is then linear with lens power and the image size stays constant.

Types

  • Manual (eyepiece, target crossed lines), projection and automated (electronic) lensometers; can measure UV transmission and progressive lenses.

Revise from: Lensometer (Focimeter) Step by Step: Single Vision, Prism, Bifocals and Progressive Lenses

IN 6. Amsler grid. DNB / UNIV · outline

Amsler grid

  • 10 cm square grid with 5 mm squares viewed at 30 cm with near correction: each square subtends 1°, covering the central 20° (10° each side of fixation).

Charts

  • Chart 1 (standard), chart 2 (diagonals for central fixation), chart 3 (red on black: subtle defects, toxic maculopathy), chart 4 (dots: scotomas), chart 5 (horizontal lines: metamorphopsia reading), chart 6 and 7 (finer grids).

Uses

  • Detection of metamorphopsia and scotomas in macular disease (AMD, CSCR, ERM), home monitoring of AMD, hydroxychloroquine toxicity screening adjunct.

Limitations

  • Low sensitivity (filling-in), depends on patient cooperation; newer: preferential hyperacuity perimetry and home OCT.
IN 7. Three-mirror lens and other diagnostic contact lenses. DNB / UNIV · outline

Goldmann three-mirror lens

  • Central lens (posterior pole), mirrors at 73° (equator to mid-periphery: area just behind equator), 67° (periphery and ora), 59° (angle and extreme periphery: gonioscopy).
  • Uses: examination of fundus and angle, laser photocoagulation.

Other diagnostic contact lenses

  • Gonioscopy: Goldmann one- and two-mirror, Zeiss four-mirror (indentation, no coupling fluid), Sussman, Posner; Koeppe (direct gonioscopy, children).
  • Fundus: Volk QuadrasPheric, SuperQuad 160 (wide-field: laser), Mainster wide-field and focal lenses, Area Centralis.
  • Laser lenses: Abraham (iridotomy), Wise, Latina SLT lens, Ritch lens (trabeculoplasty), capsulotomy lenses (Abraham, Peyman).

Care

  • Disinfection between patients, coupling gel, avoid corneal abrasion.
IN 8. Ophthalmodynamometry. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ophthalmodynamometry

  • Measurement of the pressure in the ophthalmic and central retinal arteries by applying external pressure to the globe while viewing the disc vessels: the pressure at which arterial pulsation begins (diastolic) and ceases (systolic).

Uses (historical)

  • Carotid artery insufficiency (lower readings on the affected side), ocular ischaemic syndrome; venous ophthalmodynamometry for CRVO and raised intracranial pressure.

Limitations

  • Uncomfortable, risky (raising IOP), imprecise; replaced by carotid Doppler, CT and MR angiography, and contact lens dynamometers in research.
IN 43. Smartphone-based and handheld devices for eye examination and screening. NEW · outline

Smartphone-based and handheld devices

  • Fundus imaging: smartphone adaptors with a condensing lens (D-Eye, Peek Retina), portable non-mydriatic cameras (Remidio Fundus on Phone, Volk iNview); handheld fundus cameras for ROP (3nethra Neo).
  • Anterior segment: slit-lamp adaptors for photography, smartphone macro imaging of the cornea and lids.
  • Vision testing apps: Peek Acuity, visual acuity and near vision apps, Amsler grid apps, home monitoring of AMD.
  • Handheld devices: autorefractors and photoscreeners (Plusoptix, Spot), handheld OCT (children), rebound tonometers.

Uses

  • Screening in the community (diabetic retinopathy, ROP, school vision screening), teleophthalmology from vision centres, teaching and documentation, examination of bedbound or ICU patients.

Advantages

  • Cheap, portable, easy to share images; integration with AI (offline AI for diabetic retinopathy grading).

Limitations

  • Image quality (small pupils, media opacity), smaller field, need for training, validation of apps, data privacy, medico-legal issues; should support, not replace, a full examination.

Revise from: Online Eye Consultations in India: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, What Can Be Prescribed and When to See the Patient

Operating Microscope & Viewing Systems

IN 9. Discuss, with labelled diagrams, the optical principles of the ophthalmic operating microscope and its technological advances. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optical principles

  • Binocular stereomicroscope: objective lens (working distance 150–200 mm), Galilean or zoom magnification changer, binocular tube with eyepieces; coaxial illumination for red reflex.
  • Total magnification = (tube focal length / objective focal length) × eyepiece magnification × magnification changer.
  • Beam splitter for assistant and camera.

Technological advances

  • Motorised zoom and focus, X–Y coupling, LED and xenon illumination with filters, wide-angle viewing for vitrectomy, intraoperative OCT, 3D heads-up digital visualisation, image-guided toric alignment.
IN 10. Wide-angle viewing systems in vitrectomy — optics, areas visualised and applications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principles

  • Indirect ophthalmoscopy through the operating microscope: a high-plus contact or non-contact lens forms an inverted real image; a stereo-inverter (reinverter) re-erects it.

Systems

  • Non-contact: BIOM, Resight, Merlin. Contact: Volk, Landers wide-field lenses.

Areas visualised

  • Up to about 130° of the fundus, more with scleral indentation; good view through small pupils and air-filled eyes.

Applications

  • Vitrectomy for RD, PVR, diabetic membranes, endophthalmitis, giant retinal tears; fewer instrument exchanges.
IN 11. Three-dimensional heads-up surgery and intraoperative OCT. · outline

3D heads-up surgery

  • Stereoscopic high-dynamic-range camera on the microscope displays the image on a large 3D screen viewed with polarised glasses (NGENUITY, ARTEVO).
  • Advantages: better ergonomics (surgeon’s neck posture), lower light intensity (less phototoxicity), digital filters and magnification, teaching (all see the same view), integration with imaging overlays.
  • Disadvantages: latency, cost, learning curve, depth perception issues for some surgeons.

Intraoperative OCT

  • Microscope-integrated or handheld OCT showing real-time cross-sections.
  • Uses: DMEK graft orientation and attachment, DALK (depth of dissection, big bubble), macular hole and ERM surgery (residual membranes, ILM flap position), subretinal injections (gene therapy blebs), paediatric anterior segment surgery, glaucoma (MIGS placement).
  • Studies: DISCOVER, PIONEER showed surgical decisions changed in a significant minority of cases.

Corneal Evaluation

IN 12. Critically evaluate the principles, advantages and limitations of various keratometers. Recent advances in examining and documenting the cornea (specular microscopy, confocal microscopy, Pentacam, Orbscan, AS-OCT, wavefront). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Keratometers

  • Principle: the anterior cornea acts as a convex mirror; size of a reflected mire gives radius of curvature.
  • Helmholtz (two-position, fixed object size), Javal–Schiøtz (one-position, variable mire), automated keratometers.
  • Advantages: quick, cheap, reproducible. Limitations: only the central 3 mm zone, assumes a regular sphero-cylindrical cornea, no posterior surface, unreliable in irregular corneas and after refractive surgery.

Recent advances in examining the cornea

  • Specular microscopy: endothelial cell density, CV, hexagonality.
  • Confocal microscopy: layer-by-layer imaging (Acanthamoeba cysts, fungal hyphae, nerves).
  • Pentacam (Scheimpflug), Orbscan (slit scanning + Placido): anterior and posterior elevation, pachymetry maps.
  • AS-OCT: epithelial mapping, angle, flap thickness. Wavefront aberrometry: higher-order aberrations.

Revise from: Specular Microscopy Reference Card: Cell Density, CV and Hexagonality, Specular Microscopy Practice: 22 Simulated Endothelial Cell Reports

IN 13. Describe the optical principles, documentation and applications of corneal topography and tomography; Scheimpflug principle. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Topography

  • Placido disc reflection: analyses anterior surface curvature (axial and tangential maps), simulated K, indices of irregularity; does not measure the posterior surface.

Tomography

  • Scheimpflug (Pentacam, Galilei): rotating camera images the cornea in sections; anterior and posterior elevation, pachymetry, keratometric power.

Scheimpflug principle

  • When the object plane, lens plane and image plane intersect at one line, the whole oblique slit is in focus, giving sharp images with depth.

Documentation and applications

  • Colour-coded maps (warm colours steep), Belin–Ambrosio display; detecting keratoconus and ectasia risk before refractive surgery, contact lens fitting, IOL power in post-refractive eyes, monitoring cross-linking.

Revise from: How to Read Corneal Topography and Tomography: Hard-Core Tips, Corneal Topography Practice: 53 Simulated Tomography Maps With Explanations

IN 14. Pachymetry — methods, and the role of corneal thickness in glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Methods

  • Ultrasound pachymetry (gold standard, contact), optical: specular microscopy, Orbscan, Pentacam, AS-OCT, confocal; normal central thickness about 540 µm.

Corneal thickness in glaucoma

  • Goldmann applanation is calibrated for about 520 µm: thick corneas overestimate and thin corneas underestimate IOP.
  • OHTS: thin CCT (< 555 µm) is an independent risk factor for conversion to glaucoma.
  • No reliable correction formula; use CCT as a risk factor, and consider alternative tonometers (dynamic contour, ORA corneal hysteresis).

Revise from: Central Corneal Thickness and IOP: Why There Is No Correction Formula

IN 15. Specular microscopy — optics and interpretation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Principle (specular reflection)

  • Light reflected from the interface between endothelium and aqueous (difference in refractive index) forms an image of the endothelial cell mosaic; contact and non-contact instruments; confocal microscopy as an alternative.

Parameters

  • Endothelial cell density (normal adult about 2500–3000 cells/mm²; decompensation below about 500), coefficient of variation in cell size (polymegathism; normal under 0.30), percentage of hexagonal cells (pleomorphism; normal over 60%), central corneal thickness.

Interpretation

  • Guttae appear as dark bodies (Fuchs dystrophy), ICE cells (light-dark reversal), PPCD vesicles; contact lens wear causes polymegathism.

Uses

  • Before cataract surgery (risk of decompensation), donor cornea evaluation (eye banks), after keratoplasty and phakic IOL monitoring, research.

Revise from: Specular Microscopy Reference Card: Cell Density, CV and Hexagonality, Specular Microscopy Practice: 22 Simulated Endothelial Cell Reports

IN 16. Ocular Response Analyzer and corneal biomechanics. · outline

Ocular Response Analyzer (ORA)

  • Non-contact air-puff tonometer recording two applanation pressures (inward P1 and outward P2) with an electro-optical system.

Parameters

  • Corneal hysteresis (CH = P1 − P2): viscous damping of the cornea; low CH is a risk factor for glaucoma progression (independent of CCT).
  • Corneal resistance factor (CRF), Goldmann-correlated IOP (IOPg), corneal-compensated IOP (IOPcc: less affected by corneal properties).

Corneal biomechanics

  • Cornea is viscoelastic; biomechanics change in keratoconus, after refractive surgery and with cross-linking.
  • Corvis ST (Scheimpflug high-speed camera) gives deformation parameters and the Corvis biomechanical index (keratoconus screening) and Tomographic-Biomechanical Index (TBI) with Pentacam.

Uses

  • Glaucoma risk assessment, more accurate IOP after refractive surgery, keratoconus screening before LASIK.

Tonometry

IN 17. Goldmann applanation tonometry — principle, technique, advantages and sources of error. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Imbert–Fick law: pressure = force / area. Flattening a 3.06 mm diameter area balances corneal rigidity and tear surface tension; then grams × 10 = mmHg.

Technique

  • Topical anaesthetic and fluorescein, cobalt blue light, biprism; align the two semicircles so their inner edges just touch.

Advantages

  • Gold standard, accurate, reproducible, little effect of ocular rigidity.

Sources of error

  • CCT (thick overestimates), corneal oedema and scars, astigmatism over 3 D (rotate the prism), excess or too little fluorescein, pressure on the globe, breath-holding, calibration error, after refractive surgery (underestimates).

Revise from: Tonometry for Optometrists: Non-Contact, Goldmann, Rebound and Perkins, With Sources of Error

IN 18. Types of tonometers — indentation, non-contact and rebound: merits and demerits. DNB / UNIV · outline

Indentation (Schiotz)

  • Plunger indents the cornea; weight 5.5, 7.5, 10, 15 g; scale converted by Friedenwald tables.
  • Merits: cheap, portable; demerits: affected by scleral rigidity (high myopia, thyroid eye disease), less accurate, needs supine position.

Non-contact (air-puff)

  • Air jet flattens cornea; time to applanation converted to IOP.
  • Merits: no anaesthesia, no contact (no infection), screening; demerits: less accurate at high IOP, affected by corneal properties, startle response.

Rebound tonometry (iCare)

  • Light probe bounces off the cornea; deceleration relates to IOP.
  • Merits: no anaesthesia, children and bedridden patients, home use; demerits: affected by CCT, needs horizontal position (some models), probe cost.

Others

  • Tono-Pen (Mackay–Marg principle: small applanation, useful in scarred corneas), Perkins (handheld Goldmann), pneumatonometer.

Revise from: Tonometry for Optometrists: Non-Contact, Goldmann, Rebound and Perkins, With Sources of Error, Tonometers Compared: Goldmann, Perkins, iCare, Tono-Pen and Non-Contact, With Disinfection

IN 19. Dynamic contour tonometry and other newer tonometers. · outline

Dynamic contour tonometry (Pascal)

  • Contour-matched tip with a pressure sensor; when the cornea matches the tip contour, pressure at the surface equals IOP (Pascal principle), largely independent of corneal thickness and rigidity; also measures ocular pulse amplitude.
  • Limitations: needs slit lamp, cost, longer measurement, quality scores, discontinued in some markets.

Other newer tonometers

  • Corvis ST (Scheimpflug, biomechanically corrected IOP), ORA (corneal-compensated IOP), iCare HOME (self-tonometry), transpalpebral tonometers (Diaton: through the lid), continuous monitoring (Triggerfish contact lens sensor, implantable sensors).

Posterior Segment & Glaucoma Imaging

IN 20. Discuss the principles, evolution and applications of OCT in ophthalmology (cornea, retina, glaucoma) as compared to other modalities. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Low-coherence interferometry: measures echo time delay of reflected near-infrared light; time domain → spectral (Fourier) domain → swept source (deeper penetration, faster).

Evolution

  • Time domain (Stratus, about 10 µm), spectral domain (about 5 µm axial, faster), swept source, OCT angiography, intraoperative OCT, AI analysis.

Applications

  • Cornea: epithelial and flap thickness, angle, AS-OCT in keratoconus and keratoplasty.
  • Retina: macular oedema, AMD (fluid, CNV), macular holes, ERM, CSCR, vitreomacular traction, biomarkers (DRIL, hyper-reflective foci).
  • Glaucoma: RNFL, ganglion cell complex, disc parameters, progression analysis.

Compared with other modalities

  • Non-invasive, quick, quantitative and repeatable; no dye (unlike FFA), but limited field, shows no leakage, and needs clear media and fixation.
IN 21. Fundus fluorescein angiography — principles, technique, causes of hyper- and hypofluorescence, complications and management of dye reactions; oral fluorescein angiography. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Fluorescein (excitation 465–490 nm, emission 520–530 nm) injected intravenously (5 mL of 10%); 80% binds to protein; does not leak through the healthy inner and outer blood–retinal barrier.

Phases

  • Choroidal (pre-arterial, 10–12 s), arterial, arteriovenous (laminar flow), venous, late (recirculation).

Hyperfluorescence

  • Transmission (window) defects, leakage (CNV, disc oedema, CME), pooling (CSCR, PED), staining (drusen, scars), autofluorescence and pseudofluorescence.

Hypofluorescence

  • Blocked fluorescence (blood, pigment), filling defects (capillary non-perfusion, arterial occlusion).

Complications and management

  • Skin and urine discolouration, nausea and vomiting (commonest), urticaria, syncope, anaphylaxis (rare), extravasation.
  • Have resuscitation equipment; antihistamines, adrenaline, IV fluids; previous reaction: premedicate or use oral fluorescein.

Oral fluorescein angiography

  • Oral dose (about 25–30 mg/kg); late phases only; useful for leakage in children and those with reactions.

Revise from: FFA Indications: When Fluorescein Angiography Still Wins, Medical Emergencies in the Eye Clinic: Crash Cart Checklist, Anaphylaxis, Fainting, Local Anaesthetic Toxicity and Fluorescein Reactions

IN 22. Indocyanine green angiography — principles and applications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Indocyanine green (excitation about 805 nm, emission about 835 nm, infrared); 98% protein bound so stays in choroidal vessels; penetrates pigment and blood.

Applications

  • Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (polyps, branching vascular network) and planning PDT; occult CNV; CSCR (choroidal hyperpermeability); choroiditis (white dot syndromes, VKH, birdshot, TB); choroidal tumours (haemangioma); retinal angiomatous proliferation.

Precautions

  • Contains iodine: caution in iodine allergy; safe in pregnancy is uncertain.

Revise from: ICG Angiography Indications: PCV, CSCR and Choroiditis

IN 23. Role of OCT and FFA in retinal disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

OCT in retinal disorders

  • Diagnosis and monitoring: diabetic macular oedema (thickness, fluid), AMD (intraretinal, subretinal fluid, PED), macular hole (staging), vitreomacular traction, ERM, CSCR, retinal vein occlusion, retinoschisis; guide anti-VEGF re-treatment.

FFA in retinal disorders

  • Leakage and perfusion: CNV classification, capillary non-perfusion and neovascularisation (diabetes, vein occlusion), CSCR leak site for laser, vasculitis, retinal telangiectasia.

Together

  • OCT shows structure without injection; FFA shows dynamic leakage and ischaemia; widefield FFA guides PRP; OCT angiography now replaces FFA in some uses.

Revise from: FFA Indications: When Fluorescein Angiography Still Wins

IN 24. Advances in diagnostic ophthalmic ultrasonography (A-scan, B-scan, orbital echography). HIGH-YIELD · outline

A-scan

  • One-dimensional amplitude display (8–10 MHz); axial length biometry, tissue characterisation (standardised A-scan: melanoma has low-to-medium internal reflectivity, choroidal haemangioma high).

B-scan

  • Two-dimensional brightness display (10 MHz); opaque media: RD, vitreous haemorrhage, PVD, choroidal detachment, IOFB, tumours (melanoma collar-button shape, acoustic hollowness), posterior scleritis (T sign), disc drusen.

Orbital echography

  • Thyroid eye disease (muscle thickness), orbital tumours, optic nerve sheath (30° test).

Advances

  • High-frequency UBM (35–50 MHz), 3D ultrasound, colour Doppler, contrast-enhanced ultrasound.

Revise from: B-Scan Ultrasound Practice: 26 Simulated Eye Ultrasound Images, B-scan Ultrasound Patterns: RD, PVD, Choroidal Detachment and Masses

IN 25. Ultrasound biomicroscopy — principles and its role today; advantages and disadvantages compared with AS-OCT. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • High-frequency ultrasound (35–50 MHz); resolution about 25–50 µm; depth about 4–5 mm.

Role today

  • Ciliary body and posterior iris (cannot be seen on AS-OCT): plateau iris, ciliary body cysts and tumours, cyclodialysis clefts, supraciliary effusion, angle in opaque corneas, IOL haptic position, malignant glaucoma.

Compared with AS-OCT

  • UBM advantages: sees behind the iris and through opaque media.
  • Disadvantages: contact (water bath), supine, operator dependent, lower resolution and slower.
  • AS-OCT: non-contact, faster, higher resolution, upright, but cannot penetrate pigment.
IN 26. Anterior segment OCT — principles and applications. · outline

Principles

  • Time-domain (1310 nm wavelength: better penetration through sclera and limbus; Visante) and Fourier-domain (spectral and swept-source: faster, higher resolution; CASIA, Anterion).

Applications

  • Angle assessment (angle opening distance, angle recess area, trabecular iris space area; dark and light comparison), plateau iris (limited view of ciliary body vs UBM).
  • Cornea: pachymetry and epithelial mapping (keratoconus screening, refractive surgery), DMEK graft attachment, depth of opacities before lamellar keratoplasty, flap thickness after LASIK, Descemet detachment.
  • Lens: phakic IOL vault, IOL position, lens thickness and vault.
  • Glaucoma surgery: bleb morphology, tube position.
  • Ocular surface: OSSN (thickened hyper-reflective epithelium), tear meniscus height.
  • Biometry (swept-source OCT biometers).
IN 27. IOL Master and optical biometry; A-scan biometry. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optical biometry (IOL Master)

  • Partial coherence interferometry or swept-source OCT measures axial length to the RPE; keratometry, ACD, lens thickness, white-to-white.
  • Advantages: non-contact, accurate (about 0.01–0.02 mm), quick, measures to the fovea.
  • Limitations: dense cataract, poor fixation, corneal scars.

A-scan biometry

  • Ultrasound axial length to the internal limiting membrane; contact (compression shortens about 0.1–0.3 mm) or immersion (more accurate).

Formulae

  • Third generation (SRK/T, Hoffer Q, Holladay 1), fourth (Haigis, Holladay 2), new generation (Barrett Universal II, Kane, Hill-RBF); toric calculators; post-refractive formulas.

Revise from: Biometry Printout Practice: 25 Simulated Optical Biometry Reports

IN 28. Retinal nerve fibre layer imaging — GDx, HRT (confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy) and OCT. · outline

Scanning laser polarimetry (GDx)

  • Uses the birefringence of microtubules in RNFL axons; measures retardation (proportional to thickness); corneal compensation (VCC, ECC) needed; largely obsolete.

Confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (HRT)

  • 670 nm laser; topographic images of the optic disc; rim area, cup volume, Moorfields regression analysis and glaucoma probability score; needs a reference plane; largely replaced by OCT.

OCT

  • Spectral-domain OCT: peripapillary RNFL thickness (3.46 mm circle), macular ganglion cell complex (GCL plus IPL), Bruch’s membrane opening minimum rim width; normative databases and progression analysis.

Interpretation

  • Look at the scan quality, segmentation, artefacts (myopia, tilted discs, ERM, PVD traction), red disease vs green disease; correlate with disc and fields; floor effect in advanced glaucoma.

Revise from: Retinal Layers and OCT Made Easy: Where Every Lesion Sits

IN 29. CT and MRI in ophthalmology; ocular colour Doppler. HIGH-YIELD · outline

CT

  • Bone, calcification, fractures, foreign bodies (metallic), thyroid eye disease (muscle enlargement), orbital cellulitis and abscess; fast; radiation.

MRI

  • Soft tissue, optic nerve and chiasm, demyelination (optic neuritis), tumours (meningioma, glioma), intracranial lesions; fat suppression and gadolinium; contraindicated with metallic foreign body and some implants.

Ocular colour Doppler

  • Blood flow velocity and resistivity in the ophthalmic artery, CRA and posterior ciliary arteries; carotid-cavernous fistula (reversed superior ophthalmic vein flow), ocular ischaemic syndrome, tumour vascularity.
IN 30. OCT angiography — principles, artefacts and clinical applications. · outline

Principle

  • Repeated OCT B-scans at the same location detect motion contrast (decorrelation) from moving red blood cells: maps of retinal and choroidal flow without dye (SSADA, OMAG algorithms).
  • En face slabs: superficial capillary plexus, deep capillary plexus, outer retina (normally avascular), choriocapillaris.

Artefacts

  • Projection artefacts (superficial vessels appear in deeper slabs), segmentation errors, motion artefacts (doubling, stretching), shadowing (media opacity, haemorrhage), low signal strength, cannot show leakage, limited field of view.

Clinical applications

  • CNV detection and activity (AMD, myopic, CSCR-related), diabetic retinopathy (FAZ enlargement, non-perfusion, neovascularisation), RVO, macular telangiectasia, glaucoma (peripapillary vessel density), optic neuropathies, choroiditis (choriocapillaris flow voids), sickle cell maculopathy.

Revise from: OCT Angiography Practice: 28 Simulated OCTA Reports With Explanations

IN 31. Ultra-widefield imaging and autofluorescence in retinal disease. · outline

Ultra-widefield imaging

  • Scanning laser ophthalmoscope with an ellipsoidal mirror (Optos) capturing about 200° (about 80% of the retina) in a single non-mydriatic shot; widefield FFA and ICGA; other systems (Clarus, montage OCT).
  • Uses: diabetic retinopathy (predominantly peripheral lesions predict progression), RVO (peripheral non-perfusion), uveitis and vasculitis, ROP (RetCam), peripheral tumours, retinal detachment documentation, teleophthalmology.
  • Limitations: peripheral distortion, eyelash artefacts, pseudocolour images.

Fundus autofluorescence

  • Lipofuscin in RPE fluoresces (blue 488 nm excitation; green; near-infrared shows melanin).
  • Patterns: hyperautofluorescence (lipofuscin accumulation: Stargardt flecks, Best lesions, RP ring, CSCR gravitational tracts, drusen), hypoautofluorescence (RPE loss: geographic atrophy, blocked signal).
  • Uses: GA measurement and progression, hydroxychloroquine toxicity, inherited retinal dystrophies, white dot syndromes, choroidal naevus vs melanoma (orange pigment), disc drusen.

Revise from: OCT, Fundus Cameras and Ultra-Widefield Imaging Compared: Which Device for Which Clinic

IN 32. Artificial intelligence in ophthalmic imaging — screening for diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma and ROP. · outline

Artificial intelligence in ophthalmic imaging

  • Deep learning (convolutional neural networks) trained on large labelled image sets; tasks: classification, segmentation, prediction.

Diabetic retinopathy screening

  • Autonomous AI systems (IDx-DR / LumineticsCore, EyeArt) approved for referable DR detection from fundus photographs; Indian studies with smartphone cameras and AI (Remidio Medios) show high sensitivity.

Glaucoma

  • Disc photograph and OCT analysis, field progression prediction; not yet autonomous.

ROP

  • i-ROP deep learning for plus disease; vascular severity scores; useful for telemedicine.

Others

  • AMD progression prediction, OCT fluid quantification, keratoconus detection, oculomics (cardiovascular risk, kidney disease, anaemia from retinal images), large language models in education.

Challenges

  • Image quality, generalisability across populations and cameras, bias, explainability, regulation, medicolegal responsibility, data privacy.

Revise from: AI in Ophthalmology: What Every Resident Should Know

IN 44. Adaptive optics imaging — principles and uses. NEW · outline

Principle

  • Adaptive optics (AO), borrowed from astronomy, measures the eye’s optical aberrations with a wavefront sensor (Hartmann–Shack) and corrects them in real time with a deformable mirror, allowing near-diffraction-limited imaging of the retina.

Systems

  • AO flood-illumination fundus camera (rtx1), AO scanning laser ophthalmoscope (AOSLO: confocal and split-detection), AO-OCT.

Uses

  • Imaging individual cones and rods (cone density and mosaic), RPE cells, capillaries and blood flow, lamina cribrosa pores, nerve fibre bundles.
  • Research and clinical studies: inherited retinal dystrophies (monitoring gene therapy), hydroxychloroquine toxicity, macular telangiectasia, AMD, glaucoma, retinal vascular wall-to-lumen ratio in hypertension.
  • AO microperimetry: function of single cones.

Limitations

  • Small field of view, expensive, needs clear media and good fixation, long acquisition, mainly research tools; normative data are limited.

Electrophysiology & Psychophysics

IN 33. Electroretinogram — types, technique, interpretation and clinical uses. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Full-field ERG (ISCEV standard: dark-adapted 0.01, dark-adapted 3.0 and 10.0, oscillatory potentials, light-adapted 3.0 and 30 Hz flicker); pattern ERG (macular ganglion cells); multifocal ERG.

Technique

  • Dilated pupils, dark adaptation 20 minutes, corneal (DTL, contact lens) and skin electrodes, Ganzfeld stimulation.

Waves

  • a-wave: photoreceptors. b-wave: bipolar and Müller cells. Oscillatory potentials: amacrine cells.

Clinical uses

  • Retinitis pigmentosa (reduced or extinguished), congenital stationary night blindness (negative ERG), X-linked retinoschisis (negative ERG), cone dystrophies, Leber congenital amaurosis, toxicity (hydroxychloroquine, vigabatrin), siderosis, CRAO (negative ERG), unexplained visual loss in children.
IN 34. Multifocal ERG — principle and applications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Many hexagonal stimuli (61 or 103) flicker in a pseudo-random sequence; mathematical cross-correlation extracts a local response from each area.

Applications

  • Localised macular dysfunction: hydroxychloroquine toxicity (paracentral ring loss), occult macular dystrophy (normal fundus and full-field ERG), Stargardt, cone dystrophy, AZOOR; objective correlation with visual fields.
IN 35. Detail the techniques of recording VEP/VER, their types, role in modern practice and limitations. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Technique

  • Pattern reversal (checkerboard): main peak P100 (about 100 ms); pattern onset; flash VEP for infants and poor co-operation; occipital electrodes (Oz), monocular testing.

Types

  • Transient and steady-state; multifocal VEP (local field assessment).

Role

  • Optic neuritis and demyelination (delayed latency), compressive lesions (reduced amplitude), malingering, visual acuity estimation in infants, chiasmal lesions (cross asymmetry), cortical visual impairment.

Limitations

  • Non-specific (latency delay not diagnostic), affected by refraction, media, attention and fixation; needs normative data.
IN 36. Electro-oculogram — principle and clinical application. DNB / UNIV · outline

Principle

  • Measures the standing potential between cornea (positive) and retina (negative), generated mainly by the RPE, as the eyes move horizontally between fixation points; recorded in dark (trough) and light (peak) adaptation.

Arden ratio

  • Light peak divided by dark trough: normal 1.8 or more; under 1.5 abnormal.

Clinical applications

  • Best vitelliform macular dystrophy (abnormal EOG with normal ERG: diagnostic), pattern dystrophies, diffuse RPE disease (RP, toxicity), adult vitelliform dystrophy (EOG usually normal); also records eye movements (nystagmus, saccades).

Revise from: ERG, EOG and VEP Made Easy: One Test per Level

IN 37. Automated perimetry — indications, utility and analysis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Indications

  • Glaucoma diagnosis and progression, neuro-ophthalmic disorders (chiasmal and post-chiasmal lesions), drug toxicity (ethambutol, vigabatrin), retinal disease, driving fitness, functional visual loss.

Utility

  • Quantitative, repeatable, statistical comparison with age-matched normals; strategies (SITA Standard, Fast, Faster); test patterns 24-2, 30-2, 10-2, 60-4.

Analysis

  • Reliability indices (fixation losses, false positives and negatives), total and pattern deviation plots, global indices (MD, PSD, VFI), Glaucoma Hemifield Test, progression analysis (GPA and trend).

Revise from: Perimetry Algorithms: SITA Standard, Fast and Faster

IN 38. Short-wavelength automated perimetry, frequency-doubling and static perimetry; Bjerrum screen. DNB / UNIV · outline

Static perimetry

  • Stimulus of fixed position, intensity varied to find threshold (Humphrey 24-2, 30-2, 10-2; SITA strategies); detects subtle glaucomatous defects; reliability indices.

Short-wavelength automated perimetry (SWAP)

  • Blue stimulus on yellow background tests the koniocellular (blue cone) pathway, which may be affected early in glaucoma; affected by lens yellowing; long test; largely replaced by SITA-SWAP or abandoned.

Frequency-doubling technology (FDT)

  • Low spatial frequency grating flickering at high temporal frequency; tests magnocellular My-cells; quick screening; Humphrey Matrix.

Bjerrum (tangent) screen

  • Black screen at 1 or 2 m with white targets; central 30° kinetic testing; historical; still useful for functional visual loss (tubular fields not expanding with distance).

Others

  • Goldmann kinetic perimetry (neuro-ophthalmology, advanced fields), microperimetry (macular disease), Esterman (driving).

Revise from: Perimetry Algorithms: SITA Standard, Fast and Faster

IN 45. Microperimetry — principles and uses in macular disease. NEW · outline

Microperimetry (fundus-controlled perimetry)

  • Measures retinal sensitivity at precise points while viewing the fundus, with eye tracking that compensates for eye movements; results mapped on the fundus image.
  • Devices: MAIA, MP-3 (Nidek), Compass; scotopic microperimetry for rod function.

Measures

  • Retinal sensitivity in decibels over the macula, fixation stability (bivariate contour ellipse area), fixation location (preferred retinal locus), dense and relative scotomas.

Uses

  • AMD (geographic atrophy and intermediate AMD: functional end-points in trials), macular hole and ERM (before and after surgery), diabetic macular oedema, inherited retinal disease (Stargardt, gene therapy trials), hydroxychloroquine toxicity, glaucoma (central field), low vision rehabilitation (training of a preferred retinal locus with biofeedback).

Advantages and limitations

  • Precise structure–function correlation, follow-up at the same points; but slower, needs cooperation, fewer normative data, cost.

Lasers & Energy in Ophthalmology

IN 39. Lasers in ophthalmology — physics, tissue effects and applications (argon, Nd:YAG, diode, excimer, PASCAL, micropulse, transpupillary thermotherapy). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Physics

  • Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation; monochromatic, coherent, collimated. Effects depend on wavelength, energy, spot size, duration.

Tissue effects

  • Photocoagulation (thermal): argon, diode, PASCAL.
  • Photodisruption (plasma): Nd:YAG (1064 nm), femtosecond.
  • Photoablation: excimer (193 nm ArF), breaking molecular bonds.
  • Photochemical: PDT with verteporfin. Photothermal low-energy: micropulse, TTT (810 nm).

Applications

  • Argon and PASCAL: PRP, focal laser, retinopexy, ALT. Nd:YAG: capsulotomy, iridotomy, hyaloidotomy, SLT (frequency doubled 532 nm).
  • Diode: cyclophotocoagulation, ROP. Excimer: PRK, LASIK. Femtosecond: SMILE, flaps, cataract surgery.
  • Micropulse: CSCR, DME, cyclophotocoagulation. TTT: small choroidal melanoma (largely superseded).

Revise from: Lasers in Ophthalmology: Wavelengths, Settings and Safety

IN 40. Femtosecond laser — principles and applications in ophthalmology. DNB / UNIV · outline

Principles

  • Near-infrared laser (about 1053 nm) with ultrashort pulses (femtoseconds: 10^-15 s); photodisruption creates a microplasma and cavitation bubbles with minimal collateral damage; precise cuts at any depth in transparent tissue.

Applications

  • Refractive: LASIK flap creation, SMILE (lenticule extraction).
  • Keratoplasty: femtosecond-assisted PK (shaped cuts: top-hat, mushroom, zigzag), DALK channels, intrastromal corneal ring segment tunnels, astigmatic keratotomy.
  • Cataract: femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS: capsulotomy, lens fragmentation, corneal incisions, arcuate incisions); FEMCAT and FACT trials: no clear benefit over phaco and higher cost.

Complications

  • Suction loss, opaque bubble layer, vertical gas breakthrough, capsular tears (FLACS), miosis, cost.
IN 41. Cryotherapy in ophthalmology — principles and uses. DNB / UNIV · outline

Principles of cryotherapy

  • Rapid freezing using expansion of compressed gas (Joule–Thomson effect: nitrous oxide −89°C, carbon dioxide −79°C) or liquid nitrogen (−196°C).
  • Tissue destruction by intracellular ice crystals, cell dehydration, vascular stasis; creates chorioretinal adhesion (inflammatory scar).
  • Freeze-thaw cycles (rapid freeze, slow thaw) maximise damage in tumour treatment.

Uses

  • Retina: retinal breaks and RD surgery (retinopexy), ROP (historical: CRYO-ROP), Coats disease, retinoblastoma (peripheral small tumours), haemangioblastoma.
  • Glaucoma: cyclocryotherapy (largely replaced by diode).
  • Lids and conjunctiva: trichiasis, OSSN and conjunctival melanoma margins, lid tumours, warts.
  • Cataract: cryoextraction in ICCE (historical).

Complications

  • Inflammation, PVR (excessive cryo), hypotony, scleral thinning, pain.

Dyes in Ophthalmology

IN 42. Various dyes in ophthalmology, including intraocular and vital dyes (fluorescein, ICG, trypan blue, brilliant blue, rose bengal, lissamine green). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Diagnostic dyes

  • Fluorescein: epithelial defects, Seidel test, tear break-up time, applanation, angiography.
  • Rose bengal: dead and devitalised cells and mucus (stings).
  • Lissamine green: similar staining, better tolerated (dry eye, conjunctival staining).
  • ICG: choroidal angiography.

Intraocular (vital) dyes

  • Trypan blue 0.06%: anterior capsule staining; 0.15% for ERM.
  • ICG (low concentration): ILM peeling (toxicity concerns).
  • Brilliant blue G 0.025%: ILM staining (safer). Triamcinolone: vitreous visualisation (not a dye).
  • Methylene blue: lacrimal surgery and fistula identification; not for intraocular use.

Revise from: Surgical Dyes and Triamcinolone in Eye Surgery: Trypan Blue, ICG, Brilliant Blue and How to Prepare Them
