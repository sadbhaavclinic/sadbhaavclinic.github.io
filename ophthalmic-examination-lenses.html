Ophthalmic Examination Lenses: 90D to 20D, Gonioscopy and Laser Lenses Compared

For: PG residents · Practising ophthalmologists

Last updated: November 2026

A good wide-field slit-lamp lens can handle most routine peripheral retina screening, with the indirect ophthalmoscope and scleral depression kept for high-risk eyes. This guide compares the lenses for each job, from 90D to 20D, gonioscopy and laser, with their pros, cons and current prices in India and the US.

Why look beyond the indirect ophthalmoscope

Indirect ophthalmoscopy remains the gold standard for the peripheral retina, but in a busy OPD it costs time and is hard on the examiner's neck and back. Slit-lamp lenses let you stay seated, use binocular stereo and adjustable magnification, and move straight to laser when you need to.

The guide starts with lenses for peripheral retina screening, then covers the rest of the kit: other slit-lamp lenses, indirect ophthalmoscopy (BIO) lenses, gonioscopy lenses and laser lenses.

At a glance: retina screening lenses

Wider field means lower magnification: the SuperQuad 160 gives the biggest overview and the Mainster Wide Field the sharpest detail among the contact lenses.

Static vs dynamic field: static field is what you see with the eye and lens held still; dynamic field is the total you can reach by tilting the lens and moving your view around. Figures are the manufacturers' own.

Non-contact lenses

For routine dilated screening, a SuperField or Digital Wide Field adds about 30° of dynamic field over the 90D, with no anaesthesia, gel or patient discomfort. All of these lenses give an inverted, laterally reversed image.

Volk 90D

  • Pros: long working distance (about 7 mm), easy to manipulate, forgiving through small pupils.
  • Cons: narrowest field of the group (89° dynamic), so reaching the periphery takes a lot of steering.

Volk SuperField

  • Pros: same 0.76× magnification and 7 mm working distance as the 90D, with a 116° dynamic field. A strong choice for the one lens you pick up for every patient.
  • Cons: still needs good dilation and patient cooperation to approach the far periphery.

Volk Digital Wide Field

  • Pros: 124° dynamic field with high resolution and reduced reflections.
  • Cons: 4–5 mm working distance leaves less room to tilt and manipulate the lens; some examiners find it fiddly.

Volk Super VitreoFundus

  • Pros: 124° dynamic field designed for small pupils (3–4 mm), useful in diabetics, the elderly and pseudoexfoliation.
  • Cons: low magnification (0.57×), so fine detail is softer; short working distance (4–5 mm).

Shared limits: no scleral depression, a hand-held image that moves with every tremor, and a view of the ora that depends on full dilation and a cooperative patient. Non-contact lenses also collect oil from the lashes, so clean them often.

Wide-field contact lenses

Contact lenses rest on the cornea and move with the eye, so the view is steadier, the stereo is better and you can laser through the same lens. The price is topical anaesthesia, a coupling gel and a little patient discomfort.

Volk SuperQuad 160

  • Pros: the widest field of the slit-lamp lenses here (160° / 165°), from disc and macula out towards the ora. Excellent for spotting and treating peripheral tears, lattice and PRP in one sitting.
  • Cons: low magnification (0.50×), so small holes need a second look at higher slit-lamp magnification; the most expensive lens here.

Volk QuadrAspheric

  • Pros: 120° / 144° field and good performance through small pupils; flange, no-flange and ANF+ (advanced no fluid) versions are offered.
  • Cons: narrower than the SuperQuad 160 for a similar price.

Ocular Mainster Wide Field

  • Pros: crisper image (0.68×) with 1.50× laser spot magnification, good from posterior pole to mid-periphery; an NMR (no methylcellulose required) version exists.
  • Cons: 127° dynamic field, so the least peripheral reach of the wide-field contact lenses.

Goldmann three-mirror

  • Pros: upright image in the central lens with high magnification; the mirrors reach the ora and vitreous base more reliably than any wide-field lens. The best seated way to confirm a suspected break or retinal tuft.
  • Cons: slow (you scan mirror by mirror, clock hour by clock hour), so it suits a targeted look, not screening. Each mirror shows the retina opposite it: the mirror at 12 o'clock shows the inferior retina, upside down but not reversed side to side.

Shared limits: avoid with corneal epithelial defects, recent corneal surgery or infection; the gel blurs the cornea, so do OCT and photos first.

Other slit-lamp lenses: 60D to 78D and high magnification

Lower-power slit-lamp lenses give more magnification and better stereo but a smaller field, so they suit the disc and macula rather than the periphery. The rule of thumb holds across every series: as dioptric power rises, the field widens, magnification falls and working distance shortens.

Indirect ophthalmoscopy (BIO) lenses: 14D to 40D

Gonioscopy lenses

For routine gonioscopy, a four-mirror lens (Sussman, Posner or Zeiss style) is fastest and the only type that allows indentation. Keep a Goldmann-type lens for a stable view, laser and photography.

Every method needs a lens: light from the angle meets the tear film–air interface at more than the critical angle (about 46°), so it is totally internally reflected and the angle cannot be seen directly. The lens replaces that interface with one the light can cross.

Goldmann-type (one-, two- and three-mirror)

  • Pros: steady, high-quality view; the best choice for angle laser and photography. The three-mirror also examines the fundus and ora.
  • Cons: needs gel, which blurs later fundus imaging; you must rotate it to see the whole angle; no indentation, so it cannot tell appositional from synechial closure.

Four-mirror (Sussman, Posner, Zeiss style)

  • Pros: no gel; all four mirrors face the angle, so you see 360° with little rotation; indentation (dynamic) gonioscopy separates appositional from synechial closure.
  • Cons: small contact area, harder to hold steady; slight pressure artificially opens the angle or causes corneal folds; less stable for laser.

Koeppe and other direct lenses

  • Pros: upright, panoramic view; lets you compare both eyes side by side; the standard for examination under anaesthesia and paediatric glaucoma.
  • Cons: patient must be supine, and you need a hand-held microscope or an operating microscope plus an illuminator.

Surgical gonioprisms (Swan-Jacob, Hill, Volk Vold, Mori upright) are used under the operating microscope for MIGS and goniotomy, not for diagnosis in clinic.

Reading the mirror: an indirect gonio lens shows the angle opposite the mirror. With the mirror at 12 o'clock you see the inferior angle; the image is flipped top to bottom but not side to side. Grading systems are covered in Gonioscopy Grading: Shaffer, Scheie and Spaeth.

How far do we really see?

Measured from the centre of the eye, the ora serrata lies at roughly 220°, beyond the 200° that ultra-widefield cameras such as Optos are rated for. Anatomically it sits about 6–7 mm behind the limbus, a little more anterior nasally than temporally.

Field-of-view numbers are not comparable across devices, because manufacturers measure from different reference points:

  • Internal angle (from the eye's centre): used by Optos, which makes numbers look large. 200° covers roughly 80% of the retina.
  • External angle (from the pupil): used by Zeiss Clarus and most lens specifications. Clarus's 133° per image is in the same range as Optos's 200°.

So a "160°" lens is not 60° short of the ora. In practice a dynamic exam with a wide-field lens, with the patient looking into each quadrant, gets close to the ora in most eyes. Seeing the ora itself and the vitreous base still calls for a three-mirror lens or scleral depression.

Technique pearls

Full dilation and asking the patient to look towards the quadrant you are examining matter more than which lens you buy.

  1. Dilate fully. Most lenses here perform poorly below about 5 mm; the Super VitreoFundus and SuperPupil XL are the exceptions.
  2. Start at low slit-lamp magnification (10×) with a wide, moderately bright beam, focus on the disc, then move outward.
  3. Ask the patient to look towards the quadrant you want: look up for the superior retina, and so on.
  4. Tilt the lens slightly and shift the joystick the opposite way to push the view past the equator.
  5. Remember the image is inverted: a lesion at the top of your view is in the inferior retina.
  6. Contact lenses: topical anaesthetic, fill the cup with gel and no bubbles, have the patient look up, place the lower edge first, then tilt the lens onto the cornea.
  7. Afterwards, wipe off the gel and instil a lubricant drop to cut complaints of blurred vision.
  8. Document what you did, for example "wide-field contact lens examination, periphery to the equator flat, no breaks", rather than "periphery normal".

Cleaning and disinfection

  • Contact lenses (wide-field, three-mirror, gonio and laser lenses) touch the cornea and tear film, so they count as semi-critical devices: clean and then disinfect them between every patient, following the manufacturer's care guide for the agent and soak time.
  • Poorly disinfected contact lenses can spread adenovirus and other infections between patients.
  • Do not autoclave or heat-sterilise standard lenses; only the ACS (autoclavable) versions tolerate it.
  • Rinse off disinfectant fully before the next patient, and check the contact surface for scratches, which harbour debris and degrade the image.
  • Non-contact lenses: clean the surfaces with a lens cloth or the manufacturer's cleaner and keep them in their case; scratched coatings cause glare.

When indirect ophthalmoscopy is still a must

A wide-field lens can replace the indirect ophthalmoscope for low-risk routine screening, not for eyes at real risk of a retinal break. Do a dilated indirect examination with scleral depression, or at least a three-mirror examination, in these patients:

  • Acute flashes or floaters, new PVD, or vitreous haemorrhage. This is the standard of care, and a missed horseshoe tear here becomes a detachment.
  • High myopia, and before refractive, ICL or cataract surgery in eyes at risk.
  • Ocular trauma.
  • A personal history of retinal detachment in either eye, or a family history of it.
  • Stickler syndrome, Marfan syndrome and other high-risk conditions.
  • Any suspicious peripheral finding seen with a slit-lamp lens.

Scleral depression gives what no lens can: a dynamic view that shows vitreous traction and turns a flat-looking lesion into an obvious tear. See also the retinal detachment guide and fundus drawing colour codes.

Buying tips

  • Authorised distributors often quote 10–20% below online prices, especially for two or more lenses bought together; get a written quote.
  • Buy from authorised dealers so the warranty holds and to avoid counterfeit lenses.
  • Check the contact options before you buy (with flange, no flange, ANF+ or NMR); they change handling and whether you need gel.

Suggested kit and bottom line

A three-lens kit of a SuperField (or Digital Wide Field), a SuperQuad 160 and a three-mirror covers screening, laser and the ora for about ₹1.4 lakh including GST.

  1. Every dilated patient: SuperField or Digital Wide Field, with the Super VitreoFundus as the alternative if you see many small pupils.
  2. Steady wide view, or laser: SuperQuad 160 (or QuadrAspheric).
  3. Ora, vitreous base, confirming a break: three-mirror.
  4. High-risk and symptomatic eyes: indirect ophthalmoscope with scleral depression, still.

This split keeps the indirect ophthalmoscope for the small group that truly needs it, saving time and your back without lowering the standard of care.

Where residents slip

  • Calling the periphery "normal" after a 90D view that never passed the equator.
  • Forgetting the image is inverted and drawing a tear in the wrong quadrant.
  • Using the dial setting as the true spot size through a wide-field lens.
  • Grading the angle open through a four-mirror lens while pressing on the cornea.
  • Skipping scleral depression in a patient with acute PVD symptoms.
