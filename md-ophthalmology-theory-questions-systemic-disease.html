MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Systemic Disease and General Topics (31 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 31 systemic disease and general topics theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Systemic Disease & the Eye

SY 1. Outline important systemic disorders affecting the eye and their salient ocular symptoms and signs; fundus changes in hypertension, leukaemia and diabetes. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Important systemic disorders and their eye signs

  • Diabetes: retinopathy, maculopathy, cataract, cranial nerve palsies, NVG, refractive fluctuations, papillopathy.
  • Hypertension: retinopathy, choroidopathy, optic neuropathy, vein occlusion, macroaneurysm.
  • Thyroid disease: thyroid eye disease (lid retraction, proptosis, restrictive myopathy).
  • Connective tissue disorders: dry eye, scleritis, PUK, retinal vasculitis.
  • Infections: TB, syphilis, HIV, leprosy, herpes zoster.
  • Haematological: anaemia, leukaemia, sickle cell disease.
  • Neurological: MS (optic neuritis, INO), raised ICP (papilloedema).
  • Metabolic and genetic: Marfan (ectopia lentis), homocystinuria, Wilson (KF ring), albinism.

Fundus changes

  • Hypertension: arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping, haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, macular star, disc oedema.
  • Leukaemia: Roth spots, intraretinal haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, infiltrates, optic nerve infiltration.
  • Diabetes: microaneurysms, dot-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, IRMA, venous beading, neovascularisation.

Revise from: The Eye in Systemic Disease for MBBS Students: Diabetes, Hypertension, Thyroid, TB, HIV, Rheumatology and More

SY 2. Juvenile systemic disorders affecting the eye. · outline

Juvenile systemic disorders affecting the eye

  • Juvenile idiopathic arthritis: chronic anterior uveitis (white eye), band keratopathy, cataract, glaucoma (slit-lamp screening).
  • Type 1 diabetes: retinopathy after puberty, snowflake cataract, refractive changes.
  • Infections: congenital TORCH, measles (keratitis, VAD blindness), meningitis (papilloedema, cranial nerve palsies), cat-scratch disease (neuroretinitis).
  • Haematological: leukaemia (infiltrates, haemorrhages), sickle cell disease.
  • Neurocutaneous: NF1, tuberous sclerosis, Sturge–Weber, VHL.
  • Metabolic: galactosaemia, homocystinuria, Wilson disease, MPS, cystinosis.
  • Connective tissue: Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos, Stickler (high myopia, RD), Kawasaki disease (bilateral conjunctival injection, uveitis).
  • Craniofacial syndromes, Down syndrome, cerebral palsy (strabismus, refractive errors, CVI).
  • IIH (adolescents), Vitamin A deficiency, JXG (iris lesions, hyphaema), tumours (neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma).
SY 3. Ocular manifestations of connective tissue (collagen vascular) disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Connective tissue disorders

  • Rheumatoid arthritis: keratoconjunctivitis sicca, scleritis (necrotising, scleromalacia perforans), PUK, episcleritis, Brown syndrome; hydroxychloroquine retinopathy screening.
  • SLE: dry eye, lupus retinopathy (cotton-wool spots, vaso-occlusive), choroidopathy, optic neuritis, discoid lid lesions.
  • Sjögren syndrome: aqueous-deficient dry eye, filamentary keratitis; lymphoma risk.
  • Systemic sclerosis: lid tightening, dry eye, retinopathy (hypertensive).
  • GPA: orbital inflammation, scleritis, PUK, nasolacrimal duct obstruction.
  • Polyarteritis nodosa: retinal vasculitis, PUK.
  • Dermatomyositis: heliotrope rash, retinopathy.
  • Spondyloarthropathies (HLA-B27): acute anterior uveitis.
  • Relapsing polychondritis: scleritis, episcleritis.

Management

  • Treat systemic disease with rheumatology; topical and systemic anti-inflammatory therapy; immunosuppression for scleritis and PUK.

SY 4. Outline the ocular lesions connected with specific systemic infections. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Bacterial

  • TB (phlyctenulosis, uveitis, choroiditis, Eales), syphilis (IK, uveitis, Argyll Robertson pupils), leprosy (lagophthalmos, uveitis, corneal anaesthesia), gonorrhoea (ophthalmia neonatorum, hyperacute conjunctivitis), chlamydia (trachoma, inclusion conjunctivitis), Lyme (uveitis, neuro-ophthalmic), Bartonella (neuroretinitis, Parinaud oculoglandular), endocarditis (Roth spots, emboli).

Viral

  • HSV and VZV (keratitis, uveitis, ARN, HZO), CMV (retinitis), HIV (microvasculopathy, opportunistic infection), measles (keratitis, SSPE), rubella (cataract, salt-and-pepper retinopathy, Fuchs uveitis), dengue (maculopathy), chikungunya (retinitis), COVID-19 (conjunctivitis, mucormycosis, vascular occlusions), mumps (dacryoadenitis).

Parasitic and fungal

  • Toxoplasmosis, toxocariasis, onchocerciasis (river blindness), cysticercosis, loiasis, malaria (retinopathy), candida (endogenous endophthalmitis), mucormycosis (orbital apex syndrome), cryptococcus (papilloedema), Acanthamoeba (keratitis, contact lens).

Revise from: The Eye in Systemic Disease for MBBS Students: Diabetes, Hypertension, Thyroid, TB, HIV, Rheumatology and More

SY 5. Haematological disorders in ophthalmology. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Anaemia

  • Pallor of conjunctiva, retinal haemorrhages and Roth spots, cotton-wool spots, venous tortuosity; B12 deficiency optic neuropathy.

Leukaemias

  • Retinal haemorrhages (white-centred), infiltrates, cotton-wool spots, optic nerve infiltration (emergency radiotherapy), iris infiltration and pseudohypopyon, orbital mass (granulocytic sarcoma), opportunistic infections; lymphoma (vitreoretinal).

Sickle cell disease

  • Comma-shaped conjunctival vessels, salmon-patch haemorrhages, black sunbursts, sea-fan neovascularisation (proliferative sickle retinopathy; HbSC), angioid streaks, hyphaema complications (avoid CAIs).

Others

  • Thrombocytopenia: haemorrhages; polycythaemia: dilated veins, CRVO-like; hyperviscosity (Waldenström, myeloma): sausage veins, haemorrhages; thalassaemia: desferrioxamine retinopathy; haemophilia: orbital and intraocular haemorrhage; coagulopathies and antiphospholipid syndrome: vascular occlusions.

Revise from: Sickle Cell Retinopathy: Goldberg Stages and Management

SY 6. Marfan syndrome; Hutchinson's triad and congenital syphilis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Marfan syndrome

  • Autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder (FBN1, fibrillin-1); diagnosed with the revised Ghent criteria (aortic root dilatation and ectopia lentis are cardinal).
  • Ocular: ectopia lentis (superotemporal, bilateral; zonules stretched), axial myopia, flat cornea, hypoplastic iris and dilator, RD, glaucoma, cataract, strabismus, amblyopia.
  • Systemic: tall stature, arachnodactyly (thumb and wrist signs), joint laxity, pectus, scoliosis, aortic root dilatation and dissection, mitral valve prolapse.
  • Management: refraction (aphakic correction through the aphakic part), amblyopia therapy, lens surgery (scleral-fixated IOL or CTS) when needed, RD surveillance; cardiology (beta-blockers or losartan, aortic surgery).

Congenital syphilis

  • Transplacental Treponema pallidum infection.
  • Hutchinson triad: interstitial keratitis (bilateral, 5–20 years), Hutchinson teeth (notched incisors), eighth nerve deafness.
  • Other: saddle nose, sabre shins, frontal bossing, rhagades, salt-and-pepper fundus, optic atrophy, Argyll Robertson pupils (late).
  • Management: IV penicillin; topical steroids and cycloplegics for interstitial keratitis; keratoplasty for scars.
SY 7. Inborn errors of metabolism — Wilson disease, galactosaemia, mucopolysaccharidoses. DNB / UNIV · outline

Wilson disease

  • Autosomal recessive ATP7B mutation; copper accumulates in liver, brain, cornea.
  • Eye: Kayser–Fleischer ring (copper in peripheral Descemet membrane, superior and inferior first; gonioscopy helps), sunflower cataract (anterior subcapsular), saccadic abnormalities.
  • Diagnosis: low serum caeruloplasmin, high 24-hour urinary copper, liver biopsy, genetic testing.
  • Treatment: chelation (penicillamine, trientine), zinc, liver transplant; KF ring fades with treatment (monitor).

Galactosaemia

  • Autosomal recessive (GALT deficiency, also galactokinase deficiency); galactitol accumulates in the lens.
  • Eye: oil-droplet central lens opacity (reversible early with diet), dense cataract.
  • Systemic (GALT): vomiting, failure to thrive, jaundice, hepatomegaly, sepsis (E. coli), intellectual disability.
  • Treatment: galactose-free diet (lactose-free milk); cataract surgery if dense.

Mucopolysaccharidoses

  • Lysosomal storage disorders; corneal clouding (Hurler, Scheie, Morquio, Maroteaux–Lamy; not Hunter or Sanfilippo), pigmentary retinopathy, optic nerve swelling or atrophy, glaucoma.
  • Treatment: enzyme replacement therapy, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, keratoplasty (DALK), glaucoma management.
SY 8. Non-retinal ocular manifestations of diabetes. DNB / UNIV · outline

Non-retinal ocular manifestations of diabetes

  • Lids: recurrent styes, xanthelasma.
  • Cornea: reduced sensation (neurotrophic keratopathy), delayed epithelial healing, recurrent erosions, endothelial changes, dry eye.
  • Iris: rubeosis iridis and neovascular glaucoma; ectropion uveae; poor dilatation; pigment release.
  • Lens: snowflake (metabolic) cataract in young, early senile cataract, transient refractive changes (myopic shift with hyperglycaemia).
  • Glaucoma: higher risk of POAG and NVG.
  • Optic nerve: diabetic papillopathy, NAION, optic atrophy (Wolfram syndrome: DIDMOAD).
  • Motility: cranial nerve palsies (third nerve pupil-sparing, sixth, fourth).
  • Infections: mucormycosis, endophthalmitis, orbital cellulitis.
  • Accommodation: early presbyopia, accommodative insufficiency.
SY 9. Ocular changes in pregnancy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Physiological changes in pregnancy

  • Cornea: increased thickness and curvature (contact lens intolerance; avoid refractive surgery), reduced sensitivity; Krukenberg spindle (rare).
  • IOP decreases (increased uveoscleral outflow).
  • Lids: chloasma, ptosis (transient).
  • Refractive changes, reduced accommodation, dry eye.

Pathological changes

  • Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia: hypertensive retinopathy, serous RD, cortical blindness (PRES), HELLP-associated haemorrhages.
  • CSCR (third trimester), worsening diabetic retinopathy (more frequent examination), Purtscher-like retinopathy (amniotic fluid embolism), pituitary apoplexy (Sheehan) and enlargement of pituitary adenomas (prolactinoma: field defects), IIH, cerebral venous thrombosis, meningioma growth, TED fluctuations, toxoplasmosis reactivation, uveitis often improves.

Drugs and procedures

  • Avoid teratogenic drugs (oral CAIs early, topiramate, tetracyclines, methotrexate, retinoids, anti-VEGF with caution); brimonidine late pregnancy (apnoea in neonate); fluorescein angiography generally avoided; vaginal delivery is not contraindicated by myopia or previous RD.
SY 10. Ocular manifestations of sexually transmitted diseases. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ocular manifestations of sexually transmitted diseases

  • Gonorrhoea: hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis (corneal perforation risk), ophthalmia neonatorum.
  • Chlamydia (serovars D–K): adult inclusion conjunctivitis (follicular, chronic), neonatal conjunctivitis; reactive arthritis (conjunctivitis, uveitis).
  • Syphilis: chancre on lids or conjunctiva, uveitis (any type), interstitial keratitis, placoid chorioretinitis, retinitis, optic neuritis, Argyll Robertson pupils, gumma.
  • HIV: microvasculopathy, CMV retinitis, Kaposi sarcoma, HZO, molluscum, OSSN, opportunistic infections.
  • Herpes simplex type 2: neonatal keratitis and ARN.
  • Hepatitis B and C (dry eye, retinopathy with interferon), HPV (conjunctival papillomas, OSSN), molluscum contagiosum, pediculosis (Phthirus pubis on lashes: phthiriasis palpebrarum).

Management

  • Treat the infection systemically, treat partners, screen for other STIs including HIV and syphilis.
SY 11. Eye in epidemic dropsy. · outline

Epidemic dropsy

  • Poisoning from mustard oil adulterated with argemone oil (Argemone mexicana seeds); toxic alkaloid sanguinarine; outbreaks in India (Delhi 1998).

Systemic features

  • Bilateral pitting oedema of legs, diarrhoea, erythema and skin changes, congestive heart failure, anaemia, breathlessness.

Ocular features

  • Epidemic dropsy glaucoma: open-angle glaucoma with high IOP, deep normal angles, often painless; mechanism: increased aqueous secretion (hypersecretion) and increased capillary permeability from raised histamine and prostaglandins.
  • Retinal haemorrhages, venous dilatation, disc oedema, telangiectasia, corneal oedema.

Management

  • Stop the contaminated oil, aqueous suppressants (beta-blockers, CAIs), filtering surgery in uncontrolled cases; public health action (food adulteration testing).
SY 12. Critically evaluate nutritional factors causing ocular morbidity. · outline

Nutritional causes of ocular morbidity

  • Vitamin A deficiency: night blindness, xerophthalmia, keratomalacia (leading cause of childhood blindness historically); now less common in India but persists in malnourished and malabsorption states, after bariatric surgery.
  • Vitamin B1 (thiamine): Wernicke encephalopathy (ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus), optic neuropathy.
  • Vitamin B12 and folate: nutritional optic neuropathy (centrocaecal scotomas), tobacco-alcohol amblyopia.
  • Vitamin B2 (riboflavin): angular blepharitis, corneal vascularisation.
  • Vitamin C: subconjunctival and orbital haemorrhages (scurvy), delayed wound healing.
  • Vitamin D: possible role in myopia and dry eye; deficiency with rickets (craniosynostosis).
  • Protein-energy malnutrition: keratomalacia with VAD, cataract.
  • Zinc and antioxidants: AMD (AREDS), night vision.
  • Excess: vitamin A (IIH), niacin (CME), obesity (IIH).

Critical evaluation

  • Most deficiency-related blindness is preventable; supplementation programmes, dietary diversification, food fortification and treating malabsorption are key; evidence for supplements in cataract and dry eye is weak.
SY 13. Ocular manifestations of COVID-19 and its treatment. · outline

Ocular manifestations of COVID-19

  • Conjunctivitis (follicular, mild; virus in tears), episcleritis, keratoconjunctivitis.
  • Retinal: cotton-wool spots, microhaemorrhages, retinal vein and artery occlusions (prothrombotic state), paracentral acute middle maculopathy, acute macular neuroretinopathy.
  • Neuro-ophthalmic: optic neuritis, MOGAD, cranial nerve palsies, Miller Fisher syndrome, papilloedema (venous sinus thrombosis).
  • Paediatric: MIS-C (conjunctival injection).

Treatment-related complications

  • Steroids: rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis (India 2021 epidemic: uncontrolled diabetes plus steroids), endogenous fungal endophthalmitis (Candida, Aspergillus), steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract, CSCR.
  • Prone positioning: periorbital oedema, exposure keratopathy, ischaemic optic neuropathy, CRAO.
  • Hydroxychloroquine (short course: low risk), tocilizumab (infections).
  • Vaccines: rare uveitis, optic neuritis, vascular occlusions (adenoviral vector thrombosis), VKH reactivation.

Practice changes

  • Tele-ophthalmology, slit-lamp breath shields, PPE, delays in care (glaucoma and anti-VEGF backlogs).
SY 26. The eye in chronic kidney disease and dialysis. NEW · outline

The eye in chronic kidney disease (CKD)

  • Shared risk factors: diabetes and hypertension (retinopathy is a marker of kidney disease); retinal vessel changes predict CKD progression.
  • Hypertensive and diabetic retinopathy, macular oedema (fluid overload), serous retinal detachment, CRVO and CRAO, uraemic optic neuropathy, NAION (anaemia, hypotension during dialysis).
  • Anterior segment: band keratopathy and conjunctival calcification (raised calcium-phosphate product: red eye of renal failure), dry eye, cataract (steroids).
  • Genetic and syndromic: Alport syndrome (anterior lenticonus, fleck retinopathy, temporal macular thinning), cystinosis (corneal crystals), Fabry disease (cornea verticillata), nephronophthisis with retinal dystrophy (Senior–Løken), Lowe syndrome, renal-coloboma syndrome, WAGR (aniridia), VHL (renal carcinoma), aHUS and C3 glomerulopathy (drusen).
  • Transplant patients: immunosuppressant effects (steroid cataract and glaucoma, CMV retinitis, calcineurin inhibitor optic neuropathy).

Dialysis

  • IOP rise during haemodialysis (in eyes with angle closure or glaucoma), transient blurred vision, NAION, exacerbation of macular oedema; timing of anti-VEGF injections; drug dosing in renal failure (acetazolamide contraindicated in severe CKD; mannitol caution).
SY 27. Obstructive sleep apnoea and the eye. NEW · outline

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)

  • Recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep with hypoxia, sleep fragmentation and BP surges; associated with obesity, hypertension, diabetes.

Ocular associations

  • Floppy eyelid syndrome (strong link), NAION (higher risk; treat OSA to protect the other eye), glaucoma (especially normal-tension glaucoma), papilloedema and IIH (hypercapnia raises ICP), CSCR, retinal vein occlusion, diabetic macular oedema, keratoconus (eye rubbing, floppy lids).
  • CPAP-related: dry eye, conjunctivitis from mask air leak.

Practice

  • Ask about snoring, daytime sleepiness, witnessed apnoeas (STOP-BANG questionnaire) in patients with these eye conditions; refer for polysomnography; CPAP therapy, weight loss.

Iatrogenic & Environmental

SY 14. Discuss iatrogenic disorders of the eye. · outline

Iatrogenic disorders of the eye

  • Drug-induced: steroids (cataract, glaucoma, CSCR, infection), hydroxychloroquine and chloroquine (maculopathy), ethambutol (optic neuropathy), amiodarone (vortex keratopathy, optic neuropathy), tamsulosin (IFIS), topiramate (angle closure, myopic shift), bisphosphonates (scleritis, uveitis), rifabutin (hypopyon uveitis), isotretinoin (dry eye, night vision), checkpoint inhibitors and MEK inhibitors (uveitis, serous retinopathy), vigabatrin (field loss), pentosan polysulfate (maculopathy), phenothiazines (pigmentary retinopathy), topical anaesthetic abuse (ulcers), BAK preservatives (ocular surface disease), anti-VEGF (endophthalmitis, inflammation).
  • Procedure-related: endophthalmitis after surgery and injections, PBK, CME, RD, suprachoroidal haemorrhage, TASS, wrong IOL, retrobulbar haemorrhage, globe perforation by injections, radiation retinopathy, laser burns, PRP field loss, cross-infection (adenovirus from tonometers).
  • Systemic procedures: prone surgery (POVL), cardiac surgery (emboli), general anaesthesia (corneal abrasions).

Prevention

  • Rational prescribing, screening for drug toxicity, safe surgery checklists, sterilisation, informed consent, monitoring.
SY 15. Indications for and hazards of radiation to the eye. · outline

Indications for radiation to the eye

  • Tumours: uveal melanoma (plaque brachytherapy, proton beam), retinoblastoma (plaque; external beam rarely), choroidal haemangioma, metastases, ocular adnexal lymphoma (low-dose external beam), conjunctival tumours (plaque), lid tumours (BCC: selected), orbital rhabdomyosarcoma, optic nerve sheath meningioma (stereotactic).
  • Non-tumour: thyroid eye disease (orbital radiotherapy), pterygium (beta irradiation: historical), AMD (historical, stereotactic trials), Graves disease (radioactive iodine affects eye indirectly).

Hazards

  • Lids and adnexa: madarosis, skin atrophy, telangiectasia, lid deformity, lacrimal gland damage (dry eye), canalicular stenosis.
  • Ocular surface: dry eye, keratopathy, corneal ulceration and melt, scleral necrosis (beta irradiation).
  • Lens: radiation cataract (posterior subcapsular; low threshold doses).
  • Retina: radiation retinopathy (microangiopathy, ischaemia, macular oedema, neovascularisation) months to years later.
  • Optic nerve: radiation optic neuropathy (acute vision loss; poor prognosis).
  • Others: neovascular glaucoma, orbital bone growth arrest in children, second cancers (heritable retinoblastoma).

Protection

  • Lens shielding, fractionation, proton beam for precision, monitoring and early treatment (anti-VEGF for retinopathy).
SY 16. Red eye and painful blind eye — causes and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Red eye: causes

  • Conjunctivitis (infectious, allergic), subconjunctival haemorrhage, episcleritis and scleritis, keratitis (infective, exposure), corneal abrasion and foreign body, anterior uveitis, acute angle closure, endophthalmitis, orbital cellulitis, CCF, trauma, dry eye, blepharitis.
  • Red flags: reduced vision, pain, photophobia, ciliary flush, corneal opacity, abnormal pupil, raised IOP, contact lens wear, trauma, neonate.

Painful blind eye: causes

  • Absolute glaucoma (NVG commonest), chronic uveitis, phthisis bulbi, endophthalmitis, corneal decompensation (bullous keratopathy), scleritis, after trauma or failed surgery, intraocular tumours (exclude melanoma on B-scan).

Management

  • Rule out treatable causes and tumours (B-scan).
  • Medical: topical steroids and cycloplegics, IOP lowering drops, bandage contact lens for bullae.
  • Procedures: cyclophotocoagulation (diode), retrobulbar alcohol or chlorpromazine injection, anterior stromal puncture or conjunctival flap for bullous keratopathy.
  • Surgical: evisceration (if no tumour) or enucleation with implant and prosthesis; cosmetic shells for phthisical eyes.
SY 28. Ergonomics and occupational health of ophthalmologists. NEW · outline

Ergonomic problems of ophthalmologists

  • Neck and back pain, shoulder and wrist strain, from slit lamp and microscope posture, indirect ophthalmoscopy (head load), long surgical lists, repetitive injections; work-related musculoskeletal disorders are common and may cause early retirement.
  • Other occupational hazards: infection (adenovirus, needle-stick, COVID-19), radiation (fluoroscopy rarely), laser exposure (eye protection), noise, eye strain, burnout and stress, medicolegal stress.

Prevention

  • Neutral posture: adjust chair, table and microscope height; eyepieces angled; use of heads-up 3D surgery; footswitch positioning; take micro-breaks and stretches between patients; exercise and core strengthening.
  • Lighter headsets, slit-lamp mounted imaging, assistants for scribing (EMR), scheduling to avoid long continuous sessions.
  • Infection control and vaccination; safe sharps handling; laser safety goggles.
  • Wellbeing: manageable workload, peer support, mentoring, leave.

Infection Control & Hospital Practice

SY 17. Describe disinfection and sterilisation in ophthalmology in relation to common investigations, sharp instruments and the operation theatre. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definitions

  • Disinfection: removal of most pathogenic micro-organisms (not spores); sterilisation: killing of all micro-organisms including spores; antisepsis: on living tissue.

Common investigations

  • Tonometer tips (Goldmann prisms): disposable covers or soaking in 70% isopropyl alcohol or dilute sodium hypochlorite (prion and adenovirus concerns); contact lenses for gonioscopy and laser: hypochlorite or hydrogen peroxide disinfection after each patient; B-scan probes: alcohol wipe; fundus contact lenses: similar.

Sharp instruments

  • Clean promptly (enzymatic detergents, ultrasonic cleaner), dry, pack and steam sterilise (autoclave: 121°C for 15–30 minutes or 134°C for 3–4 minutes); ethylene oxide or hydrogen peroxide plasma (Sterrad) for heat-sensitive instruments; protect tips; flash sterilisation only in emergency.

Operation theatre

  • Zoning (protective, clean, sterile, disposal), laminar air flow (HEPA filters), air changes (over 20 per hour), positive pressure, cleaning protocols, fogging alternatives (hydrogen peroxide with silver), regular swabs and microbiological surveillance, biomedical waste segregation, indicators (chemical, biological: Geobacillus stearothermophilus), TASS prevention (rinse OVD and detergents, avoid endotoxins).

Revise from: Infection Control in the Eye Clinic and Theatre: Tonometers, Drops, Sterilisation, Endophthalmitis Clusters and Waste, Autoclaves, Sterilisation Indicators and OT Fogging: Class N, S and B Autoclaves, Bowie-Dick and Biological Indicators, Fumigation

SY 18. Postoperative infections — prevention and management. · outline

Postoperative infections

  • Wound infections, suture abscesses, blebitis and bleb-related endophthalmitis, keratitis after refractive surgery or keratoplasty, endophthalmitis (acute and delayed: Propionibacterium acnes), orbital cellulitis after orbital surgery, implant infection (scleral buckle, tubes, orbital implants), canaliculitis.

Prevention

  • Pre-operative: treat blepharitis, dacryocystitis and conjunctivitis; control diabetes; povidone-iodine 5% to conjunctiva and 10% to skin (most important), proper draping of lashes.
  • Intraoperative: sterile technique, well-constructed wounds, intracameral antibiotics (cefuroxime or moxifloxacin), avoid vitreous loss, minimise surgery time, theatre hygiene and air quality.
  • Post-operative: topical antibiotics, good wound integrity, patient education on hygiene and warning symptoms (RSVP: redness, sensitivity to light, vision loss, pain).

Management

  • Culture and sensitivity; treat according to site: topical fortified antibiotics for keratitis, removal of infected sutures or implants, intravitreal antibiotics and vitrectomy for endophthalmitis (EVS), systemic antibiotics for orbital infections.
  • Outbreak investigation if clusters occur (instrument sterilisation, solutions, TASS vs infection).
SY 19. Discuss the aetiology and management perspective of advanced terminal situations on the verge of total blindness. · outline

Advanced terminal situations on the verge of total blindness

  • End-stage glaucoma (split fixation, tunnel vision), advanced diabetic retinopathy with TRD and NVG, end-stage AMD (bilateral GA or disciform scars), advanced RP and dystrophies, optic atrophy (LHON, compressive, toxic), multiple failed corneal grafts and severe ocular surface disease (SJS, chemical injuries), recurrent RD with PVR, uveitis with complications, myopic degeneration.

Management perspective

  • Preserve remaining vision: aggressive IOP control (surgery with care in glaucoma), control of diabetes and anti-VEGF, keratoprosthesis (Boston KPro, OOKP) for end-stage corneal blindness, vitreoretinal surgery for salvageable eyes.
  • Accept limits and avoid futile interventions; discuss realistic goals with the patient.
  • Rehabilitation: low vision aids, orientation and mobility training, Braille and assistive technology, vocational training, counselling (depression, anxiety), Charles Bonnet syndrome reassurance, disability certification and benefits, family support.
  • Emerging options: gene therapy (RPE65), retinal prostheses, optogenetics, stem cells.
SY 20. How would you organise an upgraded residency training programme in ophthalmology in your institute? · outline

Upgraded residency training programme

  • Competency-based curriculum (NMC CBME for PG), defined outcomes and milestones each year, entrustable professional activities, logbooks (e-logbooks) with graded surgical autonomy.

Components

  • Clinical: rotation through all subspecialties, adequate patient load, outreach and community work, emergency and trauma exposure.
  • Surgical: wet lab and simulation (Eyesi, artificial eyes) before live surgery; structured supervised progression (steps of phaco and MSICS); surgical skill assessment tools (ICO-OSCAR rubrics); video review.
  • Academic: seminars, journal clubs, case presentations, grand rounds, evidence-based medicine, research methodology and a thesis, audit and quality improvement.
  • Assessment: formative (workplace-based: mini-CEX, DOPS, case-based discussions) and summative; 360-degree feedback.
  • Soft skills: communication, counselling, ethics, medico-legal training, teamwork, wellbeing and mentorship.
  • Infrastructure: library, digital resources, tele-education, imaging and laser access.

Evaluation of the programme

  • Feedback from residents and faculty, outcomes (complication rates, exam results), periodic curriculum review, accreditation.
SY 21. Critically evaluate the organisation of an ideal (eye/blood) bank to meet the needs of your region. · outline

Ideal eye bank

  • Accredited by EBAI standards (Eye Bank Association of India); covering a region with collection centres, hospital cornea retrieval programmes and a central processing eye bank.

Organisation

  • Structure: medical director, eye bank manager, technicians, grief counsellors, quality officer; 24-hour retrieval team.
  • Functions: public awareness and pledges, retrieval (within about 6 hours of death; in situ corneoscleral excision preferred), donor screening (medical history, serology for HIV, HBV, HCV, syphilis), evaluation (slit lamp, specular microscopy), storage (McCarey–Kaufman medium: up to 4 days; Optisol and Cornisol: up to 14 days; organ culture: up to 4 weeks), distribution by need and urgency, records and traceability, quality control (microbiology), research and training tissue.
  • Advanced services: pre-cut tissue for DSAEK and DMEK, glycerol-preserved corneas for tectonic grafts, sclera for patch grafts, amniotic membrane processing.

Meeting regional needs

  • Estimate need (corneal blindness burden, keratoplasty capacity), increase supply through hospital cornea retrieval programmes (deaths in hospitals), integration with surgeons and transport, wastage reduction, government support (NPCBVI grants), monitoring indicators (collection, utilisation rate, graft outcomes).

Blood bank (if asked)

  • Licensed under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act; voluntary non-remunerated donors, screening (HIV, HBV, HCV, syphilis, malaria), component separation, cold chain, haemovigilance.
SY 22. Medico-legal aspects in ophthalmology — informed consent, documentation and the Consumer Protection Act. · outline

Informed consent

  • Voluntary, informed (nature of disease, procedure, risks including common and serious, benefits, alternatives, consequences of refusal), by a competent adult; written in the patient’s language; specific for the procedure and eye; separate consent for anaesthesia, photographs, teaching and research; guardians for minors; emergency exception.

Documentation

  • Accurate, legible, dated and timed records: history, examination findings (both eyes), investigations, diagnosis, plan, counselling, operative notes (IOL details, complications), discharge instructions; retain records (at least 3 years as per regulations; longer advisable); electronic records with audit trail.

Consumer Protection Act

  • Medical services for a fee are covered (Indian Medical Association vs V. P. Shantha, 1995); Consumer Protection Act 2019 with district, state and national commissions; negligence judged by the Bolam test (reasonable standard of a competent practitioner) and Jacob Mathew vs State of Punjab (criminal negligence needs gross negligence).

Common litigation areas in ophthalmology

  • Endophthalmitis, wrong IOL or wrong eye, retained lens matter, missed retinal detachment, ROP not screened, drug toxicity, LASIK complications, eye camp disasters, failure to refer.

Prevention

  • Good communication, consent, standard protocols, checklists (WHO surgical safety), indemnity insurance, follow-up arrangements, apology and disclosure policies.

Revise from: Informed Consent in Eye Practice: What Valid Consent Needs, Risks to Discuss for Cataract, LASIK and Injections, Minors and Language, Medico-Legal Basics for Eye Surgeons in India: Negligence, Consumer Complaints, Indemnity, Documentation and Handling an Unhappy Patient

SY 23. Simulation and wet-lab training in ophthalmic surgical education. · outline

Simulation and wet-lab training

  • Bridges the gap between theory and live surgery; improves skills and reduces complications in early surgeries.

Types

  • Wet lab: animal eyes (goat, pig), cadaver eyes, artificial eyes (Kitaro, Phillips Studio, SimulEYE) for incisions, capsulorhexis, phaco, MSICS, suturing, keratoplasty, trabeculectomy, strabismus.
  • Virtual reality simulators: Eyesi (cataract and vitreoretinal modules with scoring), HelpMeSee (MSICS simulator), indirect ophthalmoscopy simulators.
  • Video-based learning, 3D surgical videos, augmented reality.

Evidence

  • Eyesi training reduces posterior capsule rupture rates in residents (UK and US studies); HelpMeSee improves MSICS skills in LMICs.

Implementation

  • Structured curriculum with defined tasks, proficiency-based progression, assessment tools (OSACSS, ICO-OSCAR), dedicated wet-lab time and faculty, cost-sharing, integration into residency (NMC recommends skills labs).

Revise from: Wet Lab Training for Ophthalmology Residents: Eyes, Set-up and a Skill Ladder

SY 29. Patient safety in ophthalmology — the WHO surgical safety checklist and prevention of wrong-IOL and wrong-eye surgery. NEW · outline

Patient safety in ophthalmology

  • Common serious errors: wrong IOL (wrong power, wrong patient, wrong formula), wrong eye or site (injections, laser, surgery), drug errors (intracameral antibiotic dilution, mydriatic in infants), infection outbreaks (endophthalmitis clusters, TASS), retained instruments or materials, falls.

WHO surgical safety checklist (adapted for eye surgery)

  • Sign in (before anaesthesia): identity, procedure, site marking, consent, allergies, anticoagulants, anaesthesia check.
  • Time out (before incision): team introductions, confirm patient, eye and procedure, IOL type and power against the biometry and plan, critical steps, antibiotic and equipment availability.
  • Sign out (before leaving the theatre): procedure recorded, IOL details, instrument counts, specimens, concerns for recovery.
  • RCOphth and other bodies have published cataract-specific versions.

Preventing wrong-IOL and wrong-eye surgery

  • Mark the eye in the ward (indelible mark above the eye), single biometry print with the chosen IOL signed by the surgeon, IOL check against two identifiers, stocking IOLs in order, no last-minute changes without re-check, avoid “IOL bank” confusion, electronic systems (barcodes).

Safety culture

  • Incident reporting without blame, root-cause analysis, audit, duty of candour (disclosure), training, standard operating procedures.

Revise from: Informed Consent in Eye Practice: What Valid Consent Needs, Risks to Discuss for Cataract, LASIK and Injections, Minors and Language

SY 30. Biomedical waste management in an eye hospital. NEW · outline

Biomedical waste management (India)

  • Governed by the Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules 2016 (amended 2018, 2019); hospitals must segregate, store, transport and dispose through authorised common treatment facilities; maintain records and annual reports; train staff and immunise them (hepatitis B).

Colour coding

  • Yellow bags: anatomical waste (eviscerated contents, enucleated eyes after histopathology), soiled dressings and cotton, expired drugs, chemical waste: incineration.
  • Red bags: contaminated recyclable plastics (tubing, IV sets, gloves, catheters, syringes without needles): autoclaving and shredding.
  • White (translucent) puncture-proof containers: sharps (needles, blades, cautery tips, phaco tips).
  • Blue (cardboard boxes): glassware (broken vials, slides) and metallic implants.

Eye-hospital specifics

  • Sharps from surgery and intravitreal injections, used drapes and gowns, sponges, viscoelastic syringes, cytotoxic agents (MMC, 5-FU: yellow, special handling), silicone oil, chemicals from sterilisation (glutaraldehyde), mercury from old tonometers (phase out), liquid waste disinfection.

Good practice

  • Segregation at source, no mixing with general waste, needle cutters, spill kits, record keeping, barcoding (CPCB requirement), training and audit.

Revise from: Infection Control in the Eye Clinic and Theatre: Tonometers, Drops, Sterilisation, Endophthalmitis Clusters and Waste

SY 31. Ethics, and NMC regulations on professional conduct and advertising, for ophthalmologists. NEW · outline

Ethical principles

  • Autonomy (informed consent, respecting refusal), beneficence, non-maleficence, justice (fair access, camp patients treated equally), confidentiality, truthfulness (disclosure of complications), avoiding conflicts of interest (industry payments, commissions on IOLs or referrals).

Ethical issues in ophthalmology

  • Eye camps and targets (quality vs quantity), premium IOL and LASIK selling, unnecessary procedures, cost transparency, research ethics (ethics committee approval, CTRI registration), surgical training on patients (supervision and consent), telemedicine consent, AI and data privacy, organ (eye) donation and allocation, care of minors and patients without capacity.

Regulations

  • Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations 2002 and later NMC regulations and guidelines on professional conduct; Consumer Protection Act 2019; Clinical Establishments Act; Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023.

Advertising

  • Doctors may share factual information (name, qualifications, services, timings, fees) but must avoid self-promotion that is misleading, comparative claims, guarantees of results, patient testimonials and success counts where prohibited, and paid promotion that breaches the regulations; follow the current NMC advertising guidelines (check the latest notification, as rules have changed several times).

Revise from: Medico-Legal Basics for Eye Surgeons in India: Negligence, Consumer Complaints, Indemnity, Documentation and Handling an Unhappy Patient, Informed Consent in Eye Practice: What Valid Consent Needs, Risks to Discuss for Cataract, LASIK and Injections, Minors and Language

Telemedicine & Technology

SY 24. Role of telemedicine and computers in ophthalmology. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Telemedicine in ophthalmology

  • Store-and-forward (fundus photographs, OCT), real-time (video consultation), remote monitoring (home OCT, home tonometry), tele-education.
  • Uses: diabetic retinopathy screening, ROP screening (KIDROP model), glaucoma and cornea consultations, vision centres linked to tertiary hospitals (LV Prasad, Aravind), tele-optometry.
  • Indian framework: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (2020), eSanjeevani, NPCBVI vision centres.

Computers and AI in ophthalmology

  • Electronic medical records, PACS for imaging, automated perimetry, biometry formulas.
  • Artificial intelligence: FDA-cleared autonomous DR screening (IDx-DR, EyeArt), OCT analysis, glaucoma detection, ROP plus disease detection, predictions of systemic disease (oculomics).

Limitations

  • Image quality, internet access, data privacy (DPDP Act 2023), medico-legal issues, need for referral pathways.

Revise from: Online Eye Consultations in India: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, What Can Be Prescribed and When to See the Patient

SY 25. Artificial intelligence and deep learning in ophthalmology — current applications and limitations. · outline

Artificial intelligence in ophthalmology

  • Machine learning and deep learning (convolutional neural networks, transformers, foundation models such as RETFound) trained on images and data.

Current applications

  • Screening: diabetic retinopathy (autonomous FDA-cleared systems), ROP plus disease, glaucoma (disc photos and OCT), AMD (progression prediction), keratoconus (tomography), cataract grading, papilloedema detection.
  • Clinical: OCT fluid quantification for anti-VEGF decisions, visual field progression prediction, IOL power formulas (Hill-RBF, Kane), surgical video analysis, triage and tele-ophthalmology.
  • Oculomics: predicting cardiovascular risk, kidney disease, anaemia, Alzheimer disease from retinal images.
  • Education and documentation: large language models for patient information and notes.

Limitations

  • Data quality and bias (populations, cameras), generalisability, explainability (black box), regulatory approval, medicolegal liability, data privacy (DPDP Act), workflow integration, cost, overdiagnosis, need for human oversight; large language models can produce confident errors.

Revise from: AI in Ophthalmology: What Every Resident Should Know
