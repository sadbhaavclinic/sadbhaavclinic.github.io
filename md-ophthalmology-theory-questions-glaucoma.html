Aqueous Dynamics & Angle

GL 1. Describe the development, anatomy and anomalies of the anterior chamber angle and aqueous outflow channels (including uveoscleral outflow), with their application in health and disease. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Development

  • Angle structures form from neural crest mesenchyme in the 3rd–4th month; the trabecular meshwork differentiates and the iris root recedes posteriorly until after birth.
  • Failure of this posterior migration or of trabecular differentiation leaves a high iris insertion and an immature meshwork: the basis of primary congenital glaucoma and angle dysgenesis.

Anatomy (from behind forwards on gonioscopy)

  • Iris root → ciliary body band → scleral spur → trabecular meshwork (posterior pigmented, functional part) → Schwalbe’s line.
  • Trabecular meshwork: uveal, corneoscleral and juxtacanalicular (cribriform) layers; the juxtacanalicular tissue and inner wall of Schlemm’s canal give most outflow resistance.
  • Schlemm’s canal → 25–35 collector channels → aqueous and episcleral veins.

Outflow pathways

  • Conventional (trabecular): about 80–90%, pressure-dependent.
  • Uveoscleral: about 10–20% (higher in the young), largely pressure-independent; increased by prostaglandin analogues, reduced by pilocarpine.

Anomalies and application

  • Axenfeld–Rieger (prominent Schwalbe’s line with iris strands), Peters anomaly, aniridia, primary congenital glaucoma.
  • Applied: gonioscopy and grading, angle closure mechanisms, target sites of drugs (PGAs uveoscleral, ROCK inhibitors and netarsudil trabecular), laser trabeculoplasty and MIGS that bypass the juxtacanalicular resistance.

GL 2. Describe the dynamics of aqueous humour formation and circulation in health and disease, and the mechanisms of drugs affecting it. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Formation (about 2–3 µL/min)

  • Diffusion and ultrafiltration (passive) and active secretion by the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium (about 80%).
  • Key enzymes and pumps: Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, carbonic anhydrase II (HCO₃⁻ formation), Cl⁻ channels.
  • Composition differs from plasma: high ascorbate and lactate, low protein (blood-aqueous barrier).

Circulation

  • Posterior chamber → pupil → anterior chamber (thermal convection currents) → trabecular and uveoscleral outflow.
  • Goldmann equation: IOP = (F − U)/C + Pv.

In disease

  • Reduced outflow facility in POAG; pupil block in angle closure; reduced secretion in uveitis and cyclodialysis (hypotony); misdirection in malignant glaucoma.

Drugs and mechanisms

  • Reduce formation: β-blockers (cAMP), α2-agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
  • Increase uveoscleral outflow: prostaglandin analogues (matrix metalloproteinases), α2-agonists (partly).
  • Increase trabecular outflow: pilocarpine (ciliary muscle pull on the spur), ROCK inhibitors, NO-donating latanoprostene bunod.
  • Lower episcleral venous pressure: netarsudil.
  • Hyperosmotics: dehydrate the vitreous.

GL 3. Factors maintaining IOP and its diurnal variation; outflow facility (C-value) and tonography. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Factors maintaining IOP

  • Rate of aqueous formation, outflow facility (C), uveoscleral outflow and episcleral venous pressure (Goldmann equation).
  • Other influences: posture (higher lying down), age, BP, Valsalva, corneal thickness (measurement), drugs (steroids), season.

Diurnal variation

  • Normal fluctuation about 3–5 mmHg; often highest in early morning or on waking (supine nocturnal peaks).
  • Fluctuation over 8–10 mmHg suggests glaucoma; a diurnal curve or 24-hour monitoring is used in normal-tension glaucoma and progression despite “normal” IOP.

Outflow facility and tonography

  • C = ease of outflow; normal about 0.25–0.3 µL/min/mmHg; under 0.18 is abnormal.
  • Tonography (Grant): a Schiøtz-type tonometer rests on the cornea for 4 minutes; the fall in IOP is used to calculate C.
  • Pressure-outflow ratio Po/C over 100 suggests glaucoma.
  • Now largely of historical and research value; limited by ocular rigidity, pseudofacility and patient co-operation.

GL 4. Gonioscopy — optical principles, types of gonioscopes and angle-grading systems (Shaffer, Spaeth, RP Centre); other modalities of angle assessment. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optical principle

  • Angle structures are hidden by total internal reflection at the cornea–air interface (critical angle about 46°); a contact lens with similar refractive index eliminates the air interface.

Types

  • Direct (goniolenses): Koeppe, Barkan, Swan-Jacob (surgical); patient supine.
  • Indirect (gonioprisms/mirrors): Goldmann single- and three-mirror (coupling fluid), Zeiss/Posner/Sussman four-mirror (no fluid, allow indentation gonioscopy).

Grading systems

  • Shaffer: angle width grade 0–4 (0° closed to 35–45° wide open); grade 0–1 likely to close.
  • Spaeth: insertion of iris (A–E), angle width in degrees, iris configuration (r, s, q) and pigmentation.
  • Scheie: structures visible and pigment; RP Centre (AIIMS) grading in the Indian context.
  • Also van Herick (slit-lamp limbal depth) as a screening grade.

Other modalities

  • Anterior segment OCT (angle opening distance, trabecular-iris space area), UBM (ciliary body, plateau iris), Pentacam/Scheimpflug, gonio-photography.

Diagnosis & Evaluation

GL 5. Optic nerve head evaluation and nerve fibre layer, with special reference to glaucoma; causes of unilateral disc cupping. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optic disc evaluation

  • Disc size first (small discs hide cupping; large discs have large physiological cups).
  • ISNT rule (Inferior ≥ Superior ≥ Nasal ≥ Temporal neuroretinal rim) and its violation.
  • Glaucomatous signs: vertical cup elongation, notching, rim thinning, disc haemorrhage (Drance), bayoneting, baring of circumlinear vessels, laminar dot sign, beta-zone peripapillary atrophy, cup-disc asymmetry over 0.2.

Retinal nerve fibre layer

  • Red-free photography: wedge or slit defects, diffuse loss.
  • OCT RNFL thickness (inferior and superior quadrants first), ganglion cell complex; scanning laser polarimetry, confocal laser tomography (HRT) historically.

Unilateral disc cupping: causes

  • Asymmetric POAG, secondary glaucomas (pseudoexfoliation, pigmentary, traumatic angle recession, steroid, uveitic, neovascular).
  • Non-glaucomatous: compressive optic neuropathy, previous AION (especially arteritic), optic neuritis, physiological asymmetry (disc size), coloboma and optic pit.
  • Pallor out of proportion to cupping suggests a non-glaucomatous cause: image the brain.

GL 6. Humphrey visual field analysis — field changes in open-angle glaucoma, their pathogenesis, documentation and limitations; global indices. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Humphrey field analyser

  • Static automated threshold perimetry; 24-2 or 30-2 (and 10-2 for central defects), SITA strategies, size III white stimulus.
  • Reliability: fixation losses, false positives, false negatives.

Field changes in open-angle glaucoma

  • Early: generalised depression, paracentral scotomas, nasal step, temporal wedge.
  • Moderate: arcuate (Bjerrum) and Seidel scotomas, double arcuate (ring) defect.
  • Advanced: central and temporal islands; split fixation.
  • Defects respect the horizontal midline (arrangement of the RNFL at the raphe).

Pathogenesis

  • Selective loss of ganglion cell axons at the superior and inferior poles of the disc corresponds to arcuate bundles.

Global indices

  • MD (mean deviation), PSD (pattern standard deviation), VFI (visual field index, %), GHT (glaucoma hemifield test).

Documentation and limitations

  • Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson staging; Guided Progression Analysis (event) and VFI/MD trend analysis.
  • Limitations: learning effect, fatigue, cataract and small pupils (generalised depression), long-term fluctuation; about 25–35% of ganglion cells may be lost before a defect appears.

GL 7. Pre-perimetric glaucoma — diagnosis and factors in the progression of field defects. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Glaucomatous structural damage (disc or RNFL changes on examination or OCT) with a normal standard automated field.

Diagnosis

  • OCT RNFL and ganglion cell complex thinning, red-free RNFL defects, disc haemorrhage.
  • Function-specific tests: SWAP (short-wavelength), FDT (frequency doubling), 10-2 fields for early central involvement, pattern ERG.

Factors in progression of field defects

  • Higher IOP and greater IOP fluctuation, older age, thin central cornea, disc haemorrhage, beta-zone PPA, myopia.
  • Low ocular perfusion pressure, systemic hypotension (especially nocturnal), vasospasm, pseudoexfoliation, family history, poor adherence.

Management

  • Risk assessment (OHTS-type calculators); treat if progression or high risk; target 20–30% IOP reduction; serial OCT and fields.

GL 8. Concepts of glaucomatous optic nerve damage — mechanical and vascular theories. DNB / UNIV · outline

Mechanical theory

  • Raised IOP deforms the lamina cribrosa (posterior bowing, compression of laminar beams), blocking axoplasmic flow at the lamina and causing ganglion cell death by apoptosis; the larger superior and inferior laminar pores explain early arcuate defects.

Vascular theory

  • Reduced or unstable optic nerve head perfusion (low ocular perfusion pressure, nocturnal hypotension, vasospasm, faulty autoregulation) causes ischaemia; important in normal-tension glaucoma (disc haemorrhages, migraine, Raynaud).

Unifying concepts

  • Biomechanics (translaminar pressure gradient with low CSF pressure), neurotrophic factor deprivation (BDNF), glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, glial activation, mitochondrial dysfunction; both mechanisms interact.

Clinical relevance

  • IOP is the only proven modifiable factor (EMGT, OHTS); neuroprotection (brimonidine: LoGTS; nicotinamide trials) and blood pressure management are being studied.

GL 9. Target IOP. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • The upper limit of IOP, judged for each patient, below which further glaucomatous damage is unlikely to occur within the patient’s lifetime.

Setting it

  • Start from baseline (untreated) IOP; aim for at least 20–30% reduction in early disease and 40% or more (often low teens) in advanced disease.
  • Consider stage of damage, rate of progression, age and life expectancy, baseline IOP, central corneal thickness, risk factors (pseudoexfoliation, disc haemorrhage, family history).
  • Evidence: EMGT (each 1 mmHg ≈ 10% lower risk of progression), AGIS, CIGTS, CNTGS (30% reduction in NTG).

Using it

  • A range, not a fixed number; reassess at every visit against structural and functional progression; lower it if progression continues, relax it if stable for years.

GL 10. Structure-function correlation and detecting progression (event and trend analysis, OCT). · outline

Structure–function correlation

  • Structural loss (RNFL and GCL thinning on OCT) often precedes detectable field loss: 25–35% ganglion cells may be lost before standard perimetry shows a defect; in advanced disease, OCT floors out and fields are better for monitoring.
  • Map structure to function: superior RNFL to inferior field; macular GCL to central 10° field (use 10-2).

Detecting progression: fields

  • Event analysis (GPA in Humphrey: change from baseline at the same points; “likely progression”), trend analysis (rate of change of VFI or MD in dB per year; regression).
  • Need enough tests: about six fields in the first 2 years to detect fast progressors.

OCT

  • Guided progression analysis of RNFL and GCL; rates of thinning (µm per year); beware artefacts, segmentation errors, floor effect, ageing (about 0.5 µm per year).

Clinical use

  • Combine structure and function, confirm with repeat tests, judge rate against life expectancy, adjust target IOP.

GL 11. 24-hour IOP monitoring and home tonometry. · outline

Why monitor IOP over 24 hours

  • IOP fluctuates (peak often in early morning or at night when supine); single office readings miss peaks in a third or more of patients; fluctuation may matter in progression despite “normal” office IOP.

Methods

  • Diurnal phasing in hospital (every 2–3 hours, including supine readings), modified (office hours) phasing.
  • Home tonometry: rebound tonometer (iCare HOME), self-operated; good agreement with Goldmann for monitoring trends.
  • Continuous: contact lens sensor (Triggerfish: relative changes), implantable sensors (Eyemate) for research.

Indications

  • Progression despite controlled office IOP, normal-tension glaucoma, suspected nocturnal spikes, checking drug effect, before surgery decisions.

GL 57. Glaucoma in high myopia — diagnostic difficulties and management. NEW · outline

Diagnostic difficulties in high myopia

  • Disc: large, tilted, torted discs with peripapillary atrophy and shallow cups; difficult to judge cupping and rim.
  • OCT: RNFL measurements unreliable (displaced vessels, peripapillary atrophy, staphyloma, segmentation errors; myopic eyes outside the normative database); macular GCL analysis and BMO-MRW may help.
  • Fields: myopic defects (enlarged blind spot, defects from staphyloma or lacquer cracks), refractive scotomas; need correct refraction.
  • IOP: thin cornea and low scleral rigidity (Schiotz under-reads).
  • Overlap with myopic optic neuropathy; higher risk of normal-tension glaucoma.

Management

  • Establish baseline with photographs, OCT and fields; monitor progression rather than single measurements; target IOP like NTG; medical and laser therapy (SLT, pilocarpine avoided due to RD risk); surgery: trabeculectomy carries higher hypotony maculopathy risk (careful MMC use and tighter flap).

Primary Glaucomas

GL 12. POAG — minimum diagnostic criteria, severity staging with target pressure; modern diagnosis and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Minimum diagnostic criteria for POAG

  • Adult, open angle on gonioscopy, characteristic glaucomatous optic neuropathy (rim thinning, notching, RNFL defects, disc haemorrhages) with or without corresponding reproducible field defect, no secondary cause; IOP not part of the definition.

Severity staging

  • Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson (mild: MD better than −6 dB; moderate −6 to −12; severe worse than −12 or central involvement), ICD-10 staging.

Target pressure

  • The IOP range at which progression is unlikely to affect quality of life: start with 20–30% reduction (mild), 30–40% (moderate), 40% or more or IOP in low teens (severe); adjust for age, life expectancy, rate of progression, risk factors; reassess regularly.

Modern diagnosis and management

  • Diagnosis: OCT (RNFL, GCL, BMO-MRW), standard automated perimetry, pachymetry, gonioscopy, AI-assisted disc analysis.
  • Management: SLT first-line or prostaglandin analogues; add agents, consider MIGS, trabeculectomy with MMC or drainage devices for progression; adherence support; low-vision aids in advanced disease.

Revise from: POAG and Ocular Hypertension: Diagnosis, Risk and Treatment, Glaucoma Staging and Progression Rate Calculator: Hodapp-Parrish-Anderson Stage and MD Slope

GL 13. Glaucoma suspect and ocular hypertension — definition, management and follow-up. DNB / UNIV · outline

Definitions

  • Ocular hypertension: IOP above 21 mmHg on repeated measurement with normal discs, fields and open angles.
  • Glaucoma suspect: one or more of suspicious disc or RNFL, suspicious field, raised IOP, angle-closure suspect or strong family history, without definite damage.

Risk assessment (OHTS and EGPS)

  • Older age, higher IOP, thinner central cornea, larger cup-disc ratio, higher pattern standard deviation; disc haemorrhage; risk calculators estimate 5-year conversion.

Management

  • OHTS: treatment reduced 5-year conversion from 9.5% to 4.4%; treat high-risk patients (e.g. IOP over 30 mmHg, thin cornea, family history, single-eyed); low-risk may be observed.
  • Aim for 20% reduction; SLT or prostaglandin analogue.

Follow-up

  • Every 6–12 months: IOP, disc photographs or OCT, fields; more often if high risk.

GL 14. Angle-closure glaucoma — pathophysiology, modern classification (ISGEO/AIGS), clinical features and management; management of the acute attack. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathophysiology and mechanisms

  • Pupillary block (most common), plateau iris, lens-induced (phacomorphic) and retro-lenticular (malignant) mechanisms; often combined.
  • Risk factors: hypermetropia, short axial length, thick anteriorly placed lens, female sex, older age, Asian ethnicity, family history.

Classification (ISGEO / AIGS)

  • Primary angle closure suspect (PACS): 180° or more iridotrabecular contact, normal IOP and disc.
  • Primary angle closure (PAC): PACS plus raised IOP or PAS, normal disc and field.
  • Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG): PAC with glaucomatous optic neuropathy.
  • Acute angle closure (attack) is a presentation, not a separate stage.

Acute attack: features

  • Pain, vomiting, haloes, blurred vision; ciliary flush, corneal oedema, shallow AC, mid-dilated vertically oval fixed pupil, IOP often 40–70 mmHg; glaukomflecken and iris atrophy later.

Management of the acute attack

  • Lie supine; topical β-blocker, α2-agonist, steroid; IV acetazolamide 500 mg then oral; IV mannitol 1–2 g/kg if needed.
  • Pilocarpine 1–2% once IOP starts to fall; corneal indentation (Zeiss) can break the block.
  • Laser peripheral iridotomy when the cornea clears; prophylactic LPI in the fellow eye.
  • If unresponsive: laser iridoplasty, paracentesis, early lens extraction or trabeculectomy.

Chronic management

  • LPI for PAC and PACG, then medical therapy if needed; clear lens extraction (EAGLE trial) for PAC with IOP ≥ 30 mmHg or PACG; trabeculectomy or phaco-trab for uncontrolled disease.

GL 15. Plateau iris syndrome. DNB / UNIV · outline

Plateau iris

  • Anteriorly positioned ciliary processes push the peripheral iris forward against the trabecular meshwork; flat iris plane centrally with deep central AC; narrow angle remains after a patent iridotomy.

Diagnosis

  • Gonioscopy: “double hump” sign on indentation; UBM shows anterior ciliary processes with no ciliary sulcus.

Plateau iris configuration vs syndrome

  • Configuration: anatomical appearance; syndrome: angle closure and IOP rise despite a patent iridotomy (often on dilatation).

Management

  • Laser peripheral iridotomy first (to exclude pupil block), laser iridoplasty (ALPI), pilocarpine, lens extraction (with or without endocyclophotocoagulation), trabeculectomy for chronic disease.

GL 16. Provocative tests for angle-closure glaucoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Provocative tests for angle closure

  • Prone test (lie prone for 60 minutes; IOP rise of 8 mmHg or more is positive), dark room test, combined dark-room prone test, mydriatic test (weak mydriatics; risk of precipitating attack), pilocarpine-phenylephrine test, water drinking test (for open-angle outflow reserve).

Limitations

  • Low sensitivity and specificity, risk of inducing an attack; largely replaced by gonioscopy, dark-room gonioscopy, AS-OCT and UBM (dynamic imaging in light and dark).

Use today

  • Mostly research or in selected patients to decide on prophylactic iridotomy; ZAP showed low conversion rates in primary angle-closure suspects.

GL 17. Reverse pupillary block. DNB / UNIV · outline

Reverse pupillary block

  • Pressure in the anterior chamber exceeds that in the posterior chamber, pushing the mid-peripheral iris backwards against the zonules and lens (concave iris).

Settings

  • Pigment dispersion syndrome (blinking, accommodation), after phakic IOL, after silicone oil, after some vitrectomy (intraoperative lens-iris diaphragm retropulsion syndrome, LIDRS) with mydriasis and deep AC.

Consequences

  • Iris rubbing against zonules releases pigment (transillumination defects, Krukenberg spindle, pigmentary glaucoma).

Management

  • Laser peripheral iridotomy equalises pressures and flattens the iris (debated long-term benefit in PDS); lifting the iris during surgery for LIDRS.

GL 18. Pathogenesis and management of malignant glaucoma (aqueous misdirection) — correlate the management with its pathogenesis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathogenesis

  • Aqueous is misdirected posteriorly into or behind the vitreous; the expanding vitreous pushes the lens-iris (or IOL-iris) diaphragm forwards.
  • Contributing factors: anterior rotation of the ciliary body, ciliolenticular or ciliovitreal block, reduced vitreous conductivity, choroidal expansion. Typically follows filtering surgery in eyes with angle closure.

Features

  • Uniformly shallow or flat AC (central and peripheral) with patent iridotomy, normal or high IOP, no choroidal detachment or suprachoroidal haemorrhage on B-scan.

Management correlated with pathogenesis

  • Cycloplegics (atropine 1%) to tighten zonules and pull the lens back; stop miotics (which worsen it).
  • Aqueous suppressants and hyperosmotics to shrink the vitreous.
  • Nd:YAG laser hyaloidotomy/capsulotomy (pseudophakic or aphakic) to open a channel through the anterior hyaloid.
  • Pars plana vitrectomy with hyaloido-zonulectomy-iridectomy (creating a single chamber): definitive.
  • Diode cyclophotocoagulation in selected cases. Fellow eye: avoid miotics; consider prophylactic LPI and early lens surgery.

Revise from: Angle Closure Glaucoma Definitions

GL 19. Normal-tension glaucoma — clinical features, pathogenesis and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Clinical features

  • Glaucomatous disc changes and field defects with IOP never above 21 mmHg; disc haemorrhages common; dense paracentral field defects close to fixation; more common in East Asian populations, women, and with vasospasm.

Pathogenesis

  • IOP-dependent component (still benefits from lowering), vascular dysregulation (migraine, Raynaud, nocturnal hypotension, sleep apnoea), low CSF pressure (translaminar gradient), autoimmune factors, OPTN gene.

Differential diagnosis

  • Undetected high IOP (diurnal phasing), compressive optic neuropathy (MRI if atypical: young age, pallor more than cupping, field respecting vertical meridian), previous ischaemic events, myopic discs, congenital anomalies, burnt-out secondary glaucoma.

Management

  • CNTGS: 30% IOP reduction slowed progression; prostaglandin analogues, SLT, brimonidine (LoGTS), surgery if progressive; avoid nocturnal hypotension (adjust antihypertensive timing); manage sleep apnoea; regular fields.

Revise from: Normal-Tension Glaucoma: Diagnosis and the CNTGS Debate

GL 20. Advanced and absolute glaucoma — definition and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Definitions

  • Advanced glaucoma: severe field loss (MD worse than −12 dB or fixation threatened) or cup-disc ratio 0.9 or more.
  • Absolute glaucoma: no perception of light with high IOP; painful blind eye.

Management of advanced glaucoma

  • Low target IOP (low teens or less), early surgery (trabeculectomy with MMC; snuff-out risk is low), 10-2 fields, low-vision support.

Management of absolute glaucoma

  • Aim is comfort: topical steroids and cycloplegics, IOP-lowering drops if needed, cyclophotocoagulation (diode), retrobulbar alcohol or chlorpromazine injection, evisceration or enucleation for intractable pain; cosmetic shell or prosthesis.

Revise from: Glaucoma Notes for Residents: Topics and Syllabus

GL 21. ZAP and EAGLE trials — lens extraction in angle closure. · outline

ZAP trial (Zhongshan Angle Closure Prevention)

  • 889 Chinese primary angle-closure suspects: laser iridotomy in one eye, the other as control.
  • Low incidence of progression to angle closure (about 4–5 per 1000 eye-years); LPI halved the risk but number needed to treat is high; routine prophylactic LPI not recommended for all suspects (similar in ANA-LIG).

EAGLE trial

  • 419 patients aged 50 or more with primary angle closure (IOP 30 mmHg or more) or PACG: clear lens extraction vs LPI with drops.
  • Lens extraction gave better quality of life, lower IOP, fewer medications, fewer surgeries, and was cost-effective.

Clinical implications

  • Lens extraction is a first-line option in PAC with raised IOP and PACG in people aged 50 or more; LPI still used for acute attacks and selected suspects.

Revise from: Landmark Trials in Glaucoma: OHTS, EMGT, AGIS, UKGTS, LiGHT, TVT and EAGLE

GL 58. Juvenile open-angle glaucoma — genetics (MYOC), features and management. NEW · outline

Juvenile open-angle glaucoma (JOAG)

  • Open-angle glaucoma with onset between 4 and 40 years (typically teenage and young adults); often strong family history (autosomal dominant).

Genetics

  • MYOC (myocilin; GLC1A on chromosome 1q) mutations in about 10–30%; misfolded myocilin accumulates in trabecular cells; CYP1B1 in some.

Features

  • Very high IOP (often over 40 mmHg), myopia, open angle with high iris insertion or prominent iris processes, rapid cupping and field loss, often asymmetric; diagnosed late.

Management

  • Family screening (genetic testing of relatives where available).
  • Medical therapy usually inadequate; SLT less effective.
  • Surgery early: trabeculectomy with MMC, angle surgery (trabeculotomy, GATT: good results in young eyes), drainage devices; careful lifelong follow-up.

GL 22. Primary congenital glaucoma (buphthalmos) — genetics, pathophysiology, differential diagnosis and surgical management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Genetics

  • Mostly sporadic; recessive forms. CYP1B1 (GLC3A locus, chromosome 2p) most common, especially in consanguineous populations; also LTBP2, MYOC.

Pathophysiology

  • Trabeculodysgenesis: high, flat iris insertion and failure of the meshwork to mature (Barkan’s membrane theory is largely historical).

Features

  • Classic triad: epiphora, photophobia, blepharospasm.
  • Corneal enlargement (> 12 mm in the first year), Haab striae (horizontal breaks in Descemet’s), corneal oedema, deep AC, myopia, cupping (reversible in infants).

Differential diagnosis

  • Megalocornea, nasolacrimal duct obstruction, birth-trauma Descemet’s tears (vertical), congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy, mucopolysaccharidoses, keratitis.

Surgical management

  • Surgery is primary; medical treatment only bridges.
  • Goniotomy (clear cornea) or trabeculotomy (ab externo, or 360° microcatheter); combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy for severe cases or late presenters in India.
  • Refractory: trabeculectomy with MMC, drainage devices, cyclophotocoagulation.
  • Lifelong follow-up and amblyopia therapy; examination under anaesthesia for IOP, corneal diameter, axial length and disc.

GL 23. Phakomatoses — Sturge-Weber syndrome: classification, signs and management of associated glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Sturge–Weber syndrome

  • Sporadic; somatic GNAQ mutation. Facial port-wine stain (V1/V2 distribution), ipsilateral leptomeningeal angioma (seizures), and ocular involvement.
  • Roach classification: type I (facial and leptomeningeal, may have glaucoma), type II (facial only, may have glaucoma), type III (leptomeningeal only, usually no glaucoma).

Ocular signs

  • Glaucoma (30–70%; ipsilateral), diffuse choroidal haemangioma (“tomato-ketchup” fundus), episcleral haemangioma, iris heterochromia.

Mechanisms of glaucoma

  • Early onset: angle anomaly (like congenital glaucoma). Late onset: raised episcleral venous pressure.

Management

  • Medical first in later onset (aqueous suppressants, PGAs).
  • Goniotomy or trabeculotomy for early onset.
  • Trabeculectomy carries a high risk of choroidal effusion and suprachoroidal haemorrhage: lower IOP slowly, consider prophylactic sclerotomies; drainage devices are often preferred.

GL 24. Aniridia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Aniridia

  • Bilateral partial or complete absence of iris; PAX6 gene (11p13); autosomal dominant (two-thirds) or sporadic.

WAGR syndrome

  • Sporadic deletion including WT1: Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, mental retardation: renal ultrasound every 3 months until 8 years.

Ocular features

  • Photophobia, nystagmus, foveal hypoplasia, aniridia-associated keratopathy (limbal stem cell deficiency), cataract, glaucoma (about 50%: angle closure by iris stump rotating forward), optic nerve hypoplasia, ectopia lentis.

Management

  • Tinted or iris contact lenses, refractive correction, low-vision aids; glaucoma: medications, goniotomy (prophylactic), trabeculectomy, drainage devices; keratopathy: lubricants, limbal stem cell transplant, keratoprosthesis; careful cataract surgery (artificial iris implants).

GL 25. Childhood Glaucoma Research Network classification. · outline

Childhood Glaucoma Research Network (CGRN) classification (2013)

  • Primary: primary congenital glaucoma (neonatal, infantile, late-recognised; spontaneously arrested), juvenile open-angle glaucoma.
  • Secondary: glaucoma with non-acquired ocular anomalies (Axenfeld–Rieger, Peters, aniridia, ectropion uveae), glaucoma with non-acquired systemic disease (Sturge–Weber, NF1, Marfan, Lowe, rubella), glaucoma with acquired conditions (uveitis, trauma, steroids, tumours, ROP), glaucoma following cataract surgery.

GL 26. Classify secondary glaucoma. Outline, in a flow chart, the steps to diagnose and manage the various types of lens-induced glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification of secondary glaucoma

  • Open angle: pretrabecular (neovascular membrane), trabecular (pseudoexfoliation, pigment, red cells, steroids, uveitis, lens proteins), post-trabecular (raised episcleral venous pressure).
  • Closed angle: anterior pulling (PAS from neovascular, ICE, uveitis) or posterior pushing (intumescent lens, malignant glaucoma, tumours, choroidal effusion), pupil block (seclusio pupillae, phacomorphic).

Lens-induced glaucoma: flow chart

Raised IOP with a cataract → is the angle open or closed?

  • Closed: phacomorphic (intumescent lens, unilateral shallow AC) → lower IOP medically, LPI, then cataract extraction; ectopia lentis / anterior dislocation → pupil block → LPI, lens removal.
  • Open: phacolytic (hypermature cataract, protein-laden macrophages, no KPs) → control IOP, early cataract surgery; lens particle (after surgery or trauma) → steroids, washout of fragments; phacoanaphylactic (granulomatous uveitis after lens injury) → steroids, removal of lens matter.
  • In all: IOP control (aqueous suppressants, hyperosmotics), steroids, then definitive lens removal; check fellow eye and the angle afterwards.

GL 27. Phacolytic glaucoma and its management. · outline

Phacolytic glaucoma

  • Open-angle glaucoma caused by leakage of high-molecular-weight lens proteins through an intact capsule of a hypermature cataract; macrophages and proteins block the trabecular meshwork.

Features

  • Elderly, poor vision for long, sudden pain and redness, very high IOP, corneal oedema, deep AC with floating white particles (no KPs), pseudohypopyon, open angle.

Management

  • Lower IOP (topical and systemic, IV mannitol), topical steroids; cataract extraction (SICS or ECCE) with thorough AC wash; trabeculectomy rarely needed.

Differential

  • Phacomorphic (swollen lens, shallow AC, angle closure), lens particle glaucoma (after trauma or surgery), phacoanaphylactic uveitis.

GL 28. Neovascular glaucoma — causes of rubeosis, aetiopathogenesis, features and management (e.g. PDR with dense cataract and high IOP). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of rubeosis

  • Ischaemic retinopathies: PDR, ischaemic CRVO, ocular ischaemic syndrome (carotid disease), CRAO, chronic RD, radiation, tumours, uveitis.

Pathogenesis

  • Retinal ischaemia releases VEGF → new vessels on the iris and angle → fibrovascular membrane closes the angle (open-angle stage then synechial closure).

Stages

  • Rubeosis iridis → open-angle neovascular glaucoma → angle-closure (zipper-like PAS, ectropion uveae).

Management

  • Treat the cause: intravitreal anti-VEGF (rapid regression) and pan-retinal photocoagulation (definitive); carotid evaluation if no retinal cause.
  • IOP: aqueous suppressants, atropine and steroids for comfort; avoid miotics and (often) PGAs.
  • Surgery: drainage devices (Ahmed/Baerveldt) preferred; trabeculectomy with MMC after anti-VEGF; cyclophotocoagulation in poor visual potential.
  • PDR with dense cataract and high IOP: anti-VEGF, IOP control, B-scan; then combined phaco + vitrectomy with endolaser (± drainage device or cyclophotocoagulation), or phaco with early indirect PRP.

GL 29. Steroid-induced glaucoma. · outline

Steroid-induced glaucoma

  • IOP rise after corticosteroids by any route (topical, periocular, intravitreal, systemic, inhaled, skin); about 30% are moderate responders and 5% high responders; higher in POAG, children, high myopes, connective tissue disease.

Mechanism

  • Increased extracellular matrix in the trabecular meshwork, reduced phagocytosis, cytoskeletal changes (cross-linked actin networks), myocilin induction.

Features

  • Usually 2–6 weeks after starting; open angle; mimics POAG; vernal keratoconjunctivitis in boys using steroids unsupervised is a classic Indian presentation.

Management

  • Stop or reduce steroid, use lower-potency agents (loteprednol, fluorometholone), steroid-sparing drugs; IOP-lowering drops, SLT (works well), surgery (trabeculectomy) for persistent cases; remove depot steroids (sub-Tenon triamcinolone, implants) if needed.

GL 30. Causes of secondary glaucoma; pattern of IOP rise in post-uveitic glaucoma. · outline

Causes of secondary glaucoma

  • Open angle: pigmentary, pseudoexfoliation, steroid, uveitic (trabeculitis), lens-induced (phacolytic, lens particle), traumatic (angle recession, hyphaema, ghost cell), raised episcleral venous pressure (CCF, Sturge–Weber, TED), tumours.
  • Angle closure with pupil block: phacomorphic, ectopia lentis, seclusio pupillae (iris bombé), aphakic and pseudophakic pupil block.
  • Angle closure without pupil block: NVG, ICE syndrome, epithelial downgrowth, aqueous misdirection, choroidal effusion, posterior segment tumours, scleral buckle, PAS after inflammation.

Pattern of IOP rise in uveitic glaucoma

  • Acute inflammation: IOP often low (ciliary body shutdown) or high (trabeculitis: herpetic, Posner–Schlossman, toxoplasma).
  • Treatment phase: steroid response.
  • Chronic: PAS, trabecular scarring, seclusio pupillae; IOP rises as inflammation subsides and the ciliary body recovers.

GL 31. Glaucomatocyclitic crisis (Posner-Schlossman syndrome). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Posner–Schlossman syndrome (glaucomatocyclitic crisis)

  • Recurrent unilateral episodes of very high IOP (40–60 mmHg) with mild anterior uveitis; usually young to middle-aged adults.
  • Associated with cytomegalovirus in many cases (aqueous PCR).

Features

  • Mild discomfort, haloes; minimal ciliary flush, few fine KPs, open angle, no synechiae, corneal oedema; resolves in days to weeks; normal between attacks.

Management

  • Topical steroids and aqueous suppressants; avoid miotics and (often) PGAs during attacks.
  • If CMV positive: topical ganciclovir gel 0.15% or oral valganciclovir.
  • Long-term: monitor for chronic glaucoma (cumulative damage); trabeculectomy or drainage surgery if uncontrolled.

GL 32. Pigment dispersion syndrome and pigmentary glaucoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pigment dispersion syndrome (PDS)

  • Young myopic men; posterior iris bowing (reverse pupil block) rubs pigment epithelium against zonules.

Features

  • Krukenberg spindle, mid-peripheral radial transillumination defects, heavily pigmented trabecular meshwork (homogeneous band), Zentmayer ring (pigment on zonules), Scheie line (posterior lens capsule pigment), concave iris on gonioscopy; IOP spikes after exercise or dilatation.

Pigmentary glaucoma

  • 10–35% of PDS patients over time; may become less active after 50 years (pigment burn-out).

Management

  • Prostaglandin analogues, pilocarpine (reduces contact but myopic RD risk), SLT or ALT (lower energy: heavy pigmentation causes spikes), laser iridotomy (flattens iris; uncertain benefit), trabeculectomy; check peripheral retina (lattice).

GL 33. Inflammatory (uveitic) glaucoma — classification and treatment options. DNB / UNIV · outline

Classification

  • Open angle: trabeculitis, trabecular obstruction by inflammatory cells and proteins, steroid response.
  • Angle closure: with pupil block (seclusio pupillae, iris bombé), without pupil block (PAS, ciliary body swelling and rotation).
  • Specific: Posner–Schlossman, Fuchs uveitis syndrome, herpetic and CMV anterior uveitis, JIA.

Treatment options

  • Control inflammation (topical, periocular or systemic steroids; immunosuppression; antivirals for herpetic and CMV uveitis).
  • Aqueous suppressants first (beta-blockers, CAIs, alpha-agonists); prostaglandins can be used when inflammation is controlled; avoid pilocarpine.
  • Laser iridotomy for pupil block (often needs surgical iridectomy).
  • Surgery: trabeculectomy with MMC (higher failure), drainage devices (Ahmed, Baerveldt: preferred in many), cyclophotocoagulation for refractory cases; perioperative steroid cover.

GL 34. Angle recession and traumatic glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Mechanisms of traumatic glaucoma

  • Early: hyphaema, trabecular inflammation, angle closure, lens-induced, ghost cell (vitreous haemorrhage).
  • Late: angle recession, PAS, epithelial downgrowth, siderosis, retained foreign body.

Angle recession

  • Tear between the longitudinal and circular fibres of the ciliary muscle; gonioscopy shows a widened ciliary body band, torn iris processes, a whitened scleral spur.
  • Glaucoma risk rises with more than 180° of recession; may occur years later (scarring of the meshwork); the fellow eye has a higher risk of POAG.

Management

  • Lifelong annual IOP checks; aqueous suppressants and PGAs; miotics are often ineffective and may paradoxically raise IOP.
  • Laser trabeculoplasty works poorly; trabeculectomy with antimetabolite or a drainage device for uncontrolled IOP.

GL 35. ICE syndrome — definition, types, features and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Iridocorneal endothelial syndrome: abnormal corneal endothelium (ICE cells) proliferates and migrates over the angle and iris; unilateral, usually young to middle-aged women; HSV implicated.

Types

  • Chandler syndrome: corneal oedema, mild iris changes (most common).
  • Progressive (essential) iris atrophy: corectopia, iris holes, ectropion uveae.
  • Cogan–Reese (iris naevus) syndrome: pigmented iris nodules.

Features

  • “Beaten-metal” endothelium on slit lamp; specular microscopy shows pleomorphic ICE cells with dark-light reversal; high PAS beyond Schwalbe’s line; glaucoma in about half.

Management

  • Corneal oedema: hypertonic saline, IOP lowering, endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK/DMEK).
  • Glaucoma: aqueous suppressants; drainage devices (trabeculectomy often fails because the membrane grows over the ostium).

GL 36. Pseudoexfoliation syndrome — pathology, features and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathology

  • Age-related systemic microfibrillopathy: fibrillary material deposited on the lens, zonules, iris, trabecular meshwork and other tissues; LOXL1 gene variants.
  • Associated with weak zonules, poor dilation, pigment release and trabecular obstruction.

Clinical features

  • Central disc and peripheral band of material on the anterior capsule with a clear zone; pupillary ruff loss and transillumination defects; Sampaolesi line; phacodonesis.
  • Glaucoma: higher IOP, larger fluctuation, faster progression than POAG; often asymmetric.

Management

  • Medical: PGAs and combinations; lower target IOP.
  • SLT works well initially (heavy angle pigment).
  • Trabeculectomy with MMC; combined surgery; lens extraction may lower IOP.
  • Cataract surgery: poor dilation (iris hooks, rings), capsular tension ring for weak zonules, risk of late in-the-bag IOL dislocation.

GL 37. Aphakic and pseudophakic glaucoma. · outline

Aphakic and pseudophakic glaucoma

  • Open angle: steroid response, inflammation, retained lens material, hyphaema, ghost cells, viscoelastic retention (early), vitreous in AC, epithelial downgrowth.
  • Angle closure: pupil block (AC IOL without iridotomy, vitreous in pupil), PAS, aqueous misdirection, NVG.

Evaluation

  • Gonioscopy, UBM (IOL position, ciliary body), B-scan.

Management

  • Medications, laser iridotomy (pupil block), Nd:YAG hyaloidotomy, IOL repositioning or exchange, vitrectomy for vitreous-related cases, drainage devices (preferred over trabeculectomy in aphakia), cyclophotocoagulation.

GL 38. Medical management of glaucoma — classification of antiglaucoma drugs; prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide) and newer drugs. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification of antiglaucoma drugs

  • Prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprost, tafluprost); β-blockers (timolol, betaxolol); α2-agonists (brimonidine, apraclonidine); carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (topical dorzolamide, brinzolamide; oral acetazolamide); parasympathomimetics (pilocarpine); ROCK inhibitors (netarsudil, ripasudil); NO-donating PGA (latanoprostene bunod); EP2 agonist (omidenepag isopropyl); hyperosmotics (mannitol, glycerol).

Prostaglandin analogues (latanoprost 0.005% once at night)

  • Increase uveoscleral (and some trabecular) outflow via matrix metalloproteinases; 25–35% IOP reduction; first line.
  • Side effects: conjunctival hyperaemia, iris and periocular pigmentation, eyelash growth, prostaglandin-associated periorbitopathy, CME and uveitis reactivation (caution in aphakia).

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide 2% three times daily)

  • Reduce bicarbonate and aqueous formation; 15–20% reduction; stinging, bitter taste, corneal decompensation in endothelial disease; sulfonamide.

Newer drugs

  • Netarsudil (trabecular outflow and episcleral venous pressure; conjunctival haemorrhages, cornea verticillata).
  • Latanoprostene bunod, omidenepag, preservative-free and fixed combinations, sustained-release implants (bimatoprost SR).

GL 39. Critically analyse the side effects of various antiglaucoma medications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Prostaglandin analogues

  • Local: hyperaemia, iris pigmentation (irreversible), periocular skin darkening, hypertrichosis, periorbitopathy (deep sulcus), CME, reactivation of HSV keratitis and uveitis. Systemic: minimal.

β-blockers

  • Systemic: bradycardia, heart block, bronchospasm (non-selective), masking of hypoglycaemia, fatigue, depression, reduced exercise tolerance, impotence. Local: dry eye, punctate keratopathy.

α2-agonists

  • Brimonidine: allergic follicular conjunctivitis (up to 15–20%), dry mouth, fatigue; contraindicated in infants (CNS depression, apnoea). Apraclonidine: tachyphylaxis, lid retraction.

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors

  • Topical: stinging, bitter taste, corneal decompensation.
  • Oral: paraesthesia, metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia, renal stones, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, aplastic anaemia; caution in sulfa allergy and sickle cell disease.

Miotics

  • Brow ache, myopic shift, dim vision, retinal detachment, pupil block in angle closure, cataract.

Others and preservatives

  • BAK: ocular surface disease, failure of later trabeculectomy.
  • Hyperosmotics: heart failure, urinary retention, hyperglycaemia (glycerol in diabetics).

GL 40. Neuroprotection in glaucoma and its mechanism of action. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Concept

  • Protecting retinal ganglion cells and their axons from death independent of IOP lowering, since some patients progress at low IOP.

Mechanisms of ganglion cell death

  • Excitotoxicity (glutamate), oxidative stress, neurotrophin deprivation, mitochondrial dysfunction, ischaemia, glial activation, apoptosis.

Agents and their mechanisms

  • Brimonidine: α2-agonist; up-regulates neurotrophic factors and anti-apoptotic genes (LoGTS trial: less field progression than timolol in NTG).
  • Memantine: NMDA antagonist; failed in phase III trials.
  • Nicotinamide (vitamin B3): supports mitochondrial NAD⁺; promising early trials.
  • Citicoline, calcium channel blockers, ginkgo biloba, neurotrophic factors (ciliary neurotrophic factor implant), stem cells: under study.

Status

  • No neuroprotective drug is yet proven in routine practice; IOP lowering remains the only established treatment.

GL 41. Newer agents — rho-kinase inhibitors, latanoprostene bunod, omidenepag; preservative-free therapy. · outline

Rho-kinase inhibitors

  • Netarsudil 0.02% (also norepinephrine transporter inhibition) and ripasudil: increase trabecular outflow by relaxing the meshwork, reduce episcleral venous pressure; once daily; side effects: conjunctival hyperaemia, corneal verticillata, subconjunctival haemorrhage; fixed combination with latanoprost.

Latanoprostene bunod 0.024%

  • Prostaglandin analogue linked to a nitric oxide-donating group: dual action (uveoscleral and trabecular outflow); slightly greater IOP lowering than latanoprost.

Omidenepag isopropyl 0.002%

  • Selective EP2 receptor agonist (non-prostaglandin FP); increases both outflows; less periorbitopathy; avoid in pseudophakic eyes (macular oedema) and with tafluprost.

Preservative-free therapy

  • Benzalkonium chloride causes ocular surface disease, poor adherence and surgical failure; preservative-free or alternative preservatives (Polyquad, SofZia); unit-dose and multi-dose PF bottles; sustained-release implants (bimatoprost SR, travoprost intracameral implant).

GL 59. Adherence to glaucoma medication — barriers and strategies. NEW · outline

Adherence problems

  • Up to half of patients do not take drops as prescribed; poor adherence contributes to progression and blindness.

Barriers

  • Patient: lack of understanding (asymptomatic disease), forgetfulness, poor technique (missing the eye), arthritis, poor vision, depression, side effects (red eyes), cost, multiple drops.
  • Doctor and system: poor communication, complex regimens, insufficient follow-up, drug availability.

Detection

  • Ask non-judgementally, check pharmacy refills, watch drop instillation, electronic monitors (research).

Strategies

  • Education about glaucoma and the purpose of drops, written instructions, demonstration of drop technique, simplify (once-daily, fixed combinations), preservative-free options for side effects, reminders (alarms, apps, SMS), involve family, link drops to daily routines, review at each visit, low-cost generics, SLT as first-line (LiGHT), sustained-release implants (bimatoprost implant, travoprost intracameral implant), early surgery when adherence cannot be achieved.

GL 60. Glaucoma in pregnancy and breastfeeding — choice of treatment. NEW · outline

Principles

  • Balance maternal vision and fetal safety; discuss with the obstetrician; plan before pregnancy if possible; consider stopping drops in stable early disease with close monitoring (IOP falls in pregnancy).

Drug choices

  • Brimonidine: often preferred in pregnancy (FDA category B), but stop before delivery and in breastfeeding (risk of central nervous system depression and apnoea in infants).
  • Beta-blockers (timolol 0.25% gel): usable, use the lowest dose; monitor fetal heart rate (bradycardia) near delivery; secreted in breast milk.
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors: topical acceptable; oral acetazolamide avoided in the first trimester (teratogenic in animals).
  • Prostaglandin analogues: theoretical risk of uterine contraction; generally avoided in pregnancy, acceptable in breastfeeding.
  • Pilocarpine: limited data; avoid near term.
  • Reduce systemic absorption with punctal occlusion and eyelid closure.

Non-drug options

  • SLT (safe, avoids medication), surgery if needed (trabeculectomy without antimetabolites in pregnancy; avoid MMC), local anaesthesia.

GL 43. Role of lasers in glaucoma — laser peripheral iridotomy, iridoplasty, ALT/SLT. DNB / UNIV · outline

Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI)

  • Nd:YAG (with or without argon pre-treatment), superior or temporal peripheral iris; indications: angle closure with pupil block, fellow eye of acute angle closure, PAC; complications: IOP spike, bleeding, dysphotopsia, cataract.

Laser iridoplasty (ALPI)

  • Contraction burns to the peripheral iris; for plateau iris and when LPI fails to open the angle, acute attack not responding to medication.

ALT and SLT

  • ALT (argon, 50 µm burns, thermal damage), largely replaced by SLT (532 nm Q-switched frequency-doubled Nd:YAG, 400 µm spot, selectively targets pigmented cells, repeatable).
  • Indications: POAG, ocular hypertension, PXF, pigmentary glaucoma; first-line (LiGHT).
  • Effect: 20–30% IOP reduction in responders; lasts about 2–5 years.

Others

  • Cyclophotocoagulation (diode, micropulse), laser suture lysis, YAG hyaloidotomy, goniopuncture after deep sclerectomy.

GL 44. Trabeculectomy — indications, steps, factors affecting success and failure, and wound modulation (antimetabolites). DNB / UNIV · outline

Indications

  • Progression or uncontrolled IOP despite maximum tolerated therapy, advanced disease needing low targets, poor adherence or intolerance, primary surgery in selected cases.

Steps

  • Traction suture, fornix or limbus-based conjunctival flap, antimetabolite (MMC 0.2–0.4 mg/mL for 1–3 minutes, or injected), partial-thickness scleral flap, sclerostomy (punch), peripheral iridectomy, flap closure with adjustable or releasable sutures, watertight conjunctival closure, AC maintenance.

Factors affecting success

  • Better: older patients, Caucasian, primary surgery, no previous conjunctival surgery.
  • Worse: young age, Black or Indian ethnicity, previous surgery, uveitis, NVG, aphakia, long-term topical medication, active inflammation.

Wound modulation

  • Antimetabolites: MMC (stronger, longer), 5-FU (injections post-operatively); anti-VEGF, collagen matrix implants (Ologen) as alternatives; intensive post-operative steroids; needling with antimetabolites; bleb massage and suture lysis.

Complications

  • Early: hypotony, shallow AC, choroidal effusion, bleb leak, hyphaema, aqueous misdirection; late: bleb failure, encapsulation, blebitis and endophthalmitis, cataract, hypotony maculopathy.

GL 45. Surgical management of failed trabeculectomy, failed bleb and refractory (intractable) glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Failed trabeculectomy: assess

  • Cause: encapsulation, episcleral fibrosis, internal ostium blockage (iris, Descemet’s, fibrin), scleral flap fibrosis; gonioscopy and AS-OCT of the bleb.

Options

  • Early failure: digital massage, suture lysis or release, 5-FU injections, bleb needling with 5-FU or MMC.
  • Internal block: Nd:YAG laser to the ostium.
  • Revision of trabeculectomy at a new site with MMC.
  • Glaucoma drainage device (Ahmed, Baerveldt; Ahmed Baerveldt Comparison and TVT studies).
  • Cyclophotocoagulation (diode or micropulse) for poor visual potential.

Refractory (intractable) glaucoma

  • Neovascular, uveitic, ICE, post-keratoplasty, previous failed surgery, aphakic and paediatric glaucomas.
  • Drainage devices are first choice in most; antimetabolite-augmented trabeculectomy in selected eyes; cyclodestruction or cyclophotocoagulation when sight is poor; treat the underlying cause (anti-VEGF, inflammation control).

GL 46. Differential diagnosis and management of shallow anterior chamber (on day 1) following filtering surgery. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Shallow AC on day 1 after filtering surgery: approach by IOP

Low IOP

  • Bleb leak (Seidel positive): pressure patch, bandage contact lens, aqueous suppressants; resuture if persistent.
  • Over-filtration (Seidel negative, large bleb): cycloplegics, observation, reformation with viscoelastic if lens-cornea touch.
  • Choroidal detachment (serous): cycloplegics, steroids; drainage if kissing or flat AC.

High IOP

  • Pupil block (non-patent iridectomy, iris bombé): laser iridotomy.
  • Malignant glaucoma (aqueous misdirection, patent PI, flat AC centrally and peripherally): cycloplegics, suppressants, YAG hyaloidotomy, vitrectomy.
  • Suprachoroidal haemorrhage (pain, dark choroidal elevation on B-scan): control pain and IOP, drain later if needed.

Grading of flat AC

  • Grade I: peripheral iris–cornea touch; II: whole iris touches the cornea; III: lens or IOL touches the cornea (urgent reformation).

GL 47. Management of encapsulated bleb following filtering surgery. · outline

Encapsulated bleb (Tenon cyst)

  • Dome-shaped, tense, elevated, vascularised bleb with thick wall; occurs 2–8 weeks after surgery; IOP rises.

Risk factors

  • Previous topical medication (especially sympathomimetics), young age, male, previous surgery, no antimetabolite use, laser trabeculoplasty.

Management

  • Aqueous suppressants, digital massage, observation (many improve over months).
  • Needling (bleb revision) with 5-FU or MMC injection.
  • Surgical excision of the cyst wall with antimetabolites if needling fails.

Revise from: Bleb Grading and Hypotony After Trabeculectomy

GL 48. Releasable sutures. · outline

Releasable sutures

  • Scleral flap sutures that can be removed at the slit lamp without laser, by pulling an externalised knot in the cornea or conjunctiva.

Advantages

  • Tight closure at surgery lowers early hypotony and shallow AC; controlled titration of flow after surgery (days 7–21); no laser needed; can be removed through thick Tenon.

Disadvantages

  • Exposed suture end can irritate or cause infection; difficult if covered by epithelium; timing affects outcome (earlier with 5-FU, up to several weeks with MMC).

Alternatives

  • Laser suture lysis (Hoskins or Mermoud lens), adjustable sutures.

Revise from: Bleb Grading and Hypotony After Trabeculectomy

GL 49. Glaucoma drainage devices (setons, Ahmed, Baerveldt) — types, mechanism, indications and complications. DNB / UNIV · outline

Types

  • Non-valved (Molteno, Baerveldt 250 and 350 mm²): need temporary tube ligation or ripcord; lower long-term IOP.
  • Valved (Ahmed FP7): flow-restrictive valve; immediate IOP control; higher hypertensive phase.
  • Newer: PAUL glaucoma implant, Ahmed ClearPath; Aurolab aqueous drainage implant (low cost, India).

Mechanism

  • Tube in the AC, sulcus or pars plana drains aqueous to a plate placed 8–10 mm behind the limbus; fibrous capsule forms around the plate and controls absorption.

Indications

  • Failed trabeculectomy, NVG, uveitic glaucoma, ICE syndrome, aphakia, extensive conjunctival scarring, after PK or keratoprosthesis, childhood glaucoma after failed angle surgery.

Evidence

  • TVT (tube vs trab after previous surgery), PTVT (primary), ABC and AVB (Ahmed vs Baerveldt).

Complications

  • Hypotony, hypertensive phase, tube exposure (patch graft), tube–corneal touch and endothelial loss, diplopia (motility), plate extrusion, endophthalmitis, tube blockage.

Revise from: New Glaucoma Treatments: Netarsudil, Sustained-Release Implants and MIGS

GL 50. Cyclophotocoagulation — diode and micropulse. DNB / UNIV · outline

Diode cyclophotocoagulation (transscleral)

  • 810 nm laser applied 1.2 mm behind the limbus with G-probe, about 1.5–2 W for 1.5–2 seconds; destroys ciliary epithelium to reduce aqueous secretion.
  • Indications: refractory glaucoma, painful blind eyes, poor surgical candidates, NVG.
  • Complications: inflammation, hypotony, phthisis, pain, scleral thinning, loss of vision (in seeing eyes).

Micropulse transscleral cyclophotocoagulation

  • Repeated short on-off cycles (micropulse) allow tissue cooling; may also increase uveoscleral outflow.
  • Fewer complications, can be used in seeing eyes and earlier disease; effect moderate, may need repeating.

Endocyclophotocoagulation (ECP)

  • Direct visualisation through an endoscope; often combined with phaco.

Revise from: Glaucoma Notes for Residents: Topics and Syllabus

GL 51. Non-penetrating glaucoma surgery — viscocanalostomy and deep sclerectomy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Non-penetrating glaucoma surgery

  • Remove the inner wall of Schlemm canal and juxtacanalicular meshwork without entering the AC; aqueous filters through the trabeculo-Descemet window.

Deep sclerectomy

  • Superficial and deep scleral flaps; deep flap removed with unroofing of Schlemm canal; collagen or other implants to keep the space; subconjunctival bleb forms; Nd:YAG goniopuncture later to enhance flow.

Viscocanalostomy

  • Injection of high-viscosity viscoelastic into Schlemm canal to dilate it; superficial flap sutured tight; no bleb intended.

Advantages and limits

  • Fewer complications (hypotony, shallow AC, choroidal effusion, cataract) than trabeculectomy; less IOP lowering; steep learning curve; not for angle-closure eyes.

Revise from: Glaucoma Notes for Residents: Topics and Syllabus

GL 52. Combined phaco-trabeculectomy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Combined phaco-trabeculectomy

  • One-site or two-site surgery: phacoemulsification with trabeculectomy (MMC).

Indications

  • Visually significant cataract with uncontrolled glaucoma or advanced disease needing low target IOP, patients who cannot attend two operations, angle closure with PAS.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • One anaesthesia and recovery; avoids post-cataract IOP spike; protects trabeculectomy from later cataract surgery.
  • IOP lowering less than trabeculectomy alone; more inflammation; higher failure than staged surgery.

Alternatives

  • Phaco alone (in PACG lowers IOP), phaco with MIGS, phaco with goniosynechialysis, staged surgery (trabeculectomy first, phaco 6 months later).

Revise from: Glaucoma Long Case for MS and DNB Practicals: History, Examination, Presentation and Viva Questions

GL 53. Goniotomy and trabeculotomy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Goniotomy

  • Incision of the trabecular meshwork from inside under direct gonioscopic view (Barkan lens); needs a clear cornea; incise 90–120° in one session.

Trabeculotomy

  • External approach (find Schlemm canal under a scleral flap, rotate a trabeculotome into the AC) or 360° circumferential trabeculotomy with an illuminated microcatheter or suture (better IOP outcomes); usable with cloudy corneas.

Indications

  • Primary congenital glaucoma (first-line), some secondary childhood glaucomas, juvenile open-angle glaucoma; combined trabeculotomy-trabeculectomy for severe or Indian presentations (high success in India).

Complications

  • Hyphaema, iris or lens injury, Descemet detachment, false passage, failure.

Revise from: Primary Congenital Glaucoma

GL 54. Selective laser trabeculoplasty as first-line therapy (LiGHT trial). · outline

LiGHT trial (Laser in Glaucoma and ocular HyperTension)

  • 718 newly diagnosed untreated OAG and OHT patients in the UK: SLT first vs eye drops first.
  • At 3 years: 74% of SLT patients were drop-free with similar quality of life, better IOP control and fewer surgeries, lower cost; at 6 years: fewer patients progressed and fewer needed trabeculectomy in the SLT group.

Implications

  • SLT is a first-line option (NICE 2022); avoids drop side effects and adherence problems.

Practical points

  • 360° treatment, energy titrated to bubble formation, check IOP at 1 hour; repeat SLT works; less effective in angle closure and some secondary glaucomas.

Revise from: SLT as First-Line Glaucoma Treatment: The LiGHT Trial Explained

GL 55. Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) — types, indications and outcomes. · outline

MIGS (minimally invasive glaucoma surgery)

  • Ab interno, minimal tissue trauma, high safety, rapid recovery, modest IOP lowering; often combined with cataract surgery.

Types

  • Trabecular bypass: iStent, iStent inject, Hydrus (Schlemm canal scaffold).
  • Trabecular excision or incision: Kahook Dual Blade, trabectome, GATT (gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy).
  • Canal dilatation: OMNI, iTrack (canaloplasty ab interno).
  • Suprachoroidal: CyPass (withdrawn: endothelial loss), iStent supra.
  • Subconjunctival (bleb-forming, less minimally invasive): XEN gel stent, PreserFlo microshunt.

Indications

  • Mild to moderate open-angle glaucoma, reducing medications, combined with cataract surgery; bleb-forming devices for moderate disease.

Outcomes

  • IOP reduction modest (about 2–5 mmHg beyond phaco), fewer medications (HORIZON for Hydrus, iStent pivotal trials); cost and access limit use in India.

Revise from: New Glaucoma Treatments: Netarsudil, Sustained-Release Implants and MIGS

GL 61. Bleb-related infection — blebitis and bleb-related endophthalmitis. NEW · outline

Bleb-related infection

  • Blebitis: infection confined to the bleb (milky bleb, surrounding conjunctival injection, mucopurulent discharge, mild AC cells, no vitreous involvement).
  • Bleb-related endophthalmitis: spread into the vitreous; severe vision loss.

Risk factors

  • Thin, avascular, cystic blebs (MMC), inferior or nasal blebs, bleb leaks, releasable sutures, blepharitis, conjunctivitis, contact lens use, young age, diabetes.

Organisms

  • Streptococcus species, Haemophilus influenzae (more virulent than post-cataract organisms), Staphylococcus, Enterococcus.

Management

  • Blebitis: intensive topical fortified antibiotics (or fluoroquinolones) every hour, with systemic antibiotics; topical steroids after response; close review.
  • Endophthalmitis: vitreous tap and intravitreal vancomycin and ceftazidime, early vitrectomy, systemic antibiotics.

Prevention

  • Avoid inferior blebs, use diffuse MMC application to produce diffuse blebs, repair leaks and thin blebs (bleb revision, conjunctival advancement), educate patients about warning signs (red eye, discharge), treat blepharitis.

Revise from: Bleb Grading and Hypotony After Trabeculectomy, Endophthalmitis: Types, Organisms and Management

Public Health Aspects

GL 56. Strategies to decrease glaucoma morbidity in the country; ideal model of glaucoma services in a tertiary care centre; glaucoma screening. · outline

Strategies to decrease glaucoma morbidity

  • Awareness (World Glaucoma Week), opportunistic case detection (IOP and disc assessment for adults over 40 at every visit), family screening of glaucoma patients, training of optometrists and general ophthalmologists in gonioscopy and disc evaluation, affordable medication and laser access, adherence counselling, low-vision rehabilitation.

Ideal tertiary glaucoma service

  • Dedicated clinic with OCT, perimetry, gonioscopy, pachymetry, UBM and AS-OCT; laser suite (SLT, LPI); surgical expertise (trabeculectomy, drainage devices, MIGS, childhood glaucoma); electronic records and progression software; counsellors; links to vision centres and tele-glaucoma; research and training.

Screening

  • Population screening is not cost-effective (low prevalence, poor test accuracy); targeted screening of high-risk groups (first-degree relatives, people over 40 with myopia, diabetes, steroid users) and opportunistic case finding are preferred; AI disc photograph grading is promising.
