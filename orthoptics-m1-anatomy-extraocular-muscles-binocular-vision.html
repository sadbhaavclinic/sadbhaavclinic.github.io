Orthoptics Module 1: Anatomy of Eye Movements and Binocular Vision

For: Optometrists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Six extraocular muscles per eye; nerve supply LR6, SO4, all others III.
  • Hering: yoke muscles get equal innervation. Sherrington: when an agonist contracts, its antagonist relaxes.
  • Binocular single vision needs normal retinal correspondence, overlapping fields, and motor and sensory fusion.
  • Stereopsis is the highest grade of binocular vision and develops in the first months of life.

The distances of the rectus insertions from the limbus increase in a spiral (the spiral of Tillaux: MR, IR, LR, SR). The vertical recti act best as elevators and depressors when the eye is abducted about 23°; the obliques act best as depressor (SO) and elevator (IO) when the eye is adducted about 51°. This is why the clinical test positions are: SR and IR in abduction, SO and IO in adduction.

Laws of innervation

  • Hering’s law: equal and simultaneous innervation flows to yoke muscles. In a paralytic squint, fixing with the paretic eye needs extra innervation, which also goes to the yoke of the other eye, so the secondary deviation is larger than the primary.
  • Sherrington’s law: reciprocal innervation: increased innervation to a muscle is matched by decreased innervation to its direct antagonist.

Cranial nerve pathways

  • III: nucleus in the midbrain; supplies SR (crossed), MR, IR, IO, levator, and the pupil sphincter and ciliary muscle (parasympathetic). Pupil fibres run on the outside of the nerve, so compression (aneurysm) usually affects the pupil early.
  • IV: the only nerve to leave the back of the brainstem and to cross completely; longest intracranial course, so often damaged in head injury.
  • VI: long course over the petrous apex, so affected by raised intracranial pressure (a “false localising sign”).
  • Supranuclear control: saccades (frontal eye fields, paramedian pontine reticular formation for horizontal), smooth pursuit (parieto-occipital), vergence, and the vestibulo-ocular reflex. The medial longitudinal fasciculus links the VI nucleus to the opposite III subnucleus for medial rectus: damage causes internuclear ophthalmoplegia.

Binocular single vision (BSV)

BSV is the use of both eyes together to form one percept. It needs: clear similar images in each eye, overlapping visual fields, normal retinal correspondence, a normal motor system, and an intact visual pathway and cortex.

Grade (Worth)MeaningTested with
1. Simultaneous perceptionSeeing two dissimilar images at onceSynoptophore SP slides
2. FusionCombining similar images into one, with a range (fusional amplitude)Synoptophore fusion slides, prism bars, Bagolini, Worth four-dot
3. StereopsisDepth from horizontal disparityFrisby, TNO, Lang, Titmus, synoptophore
  • Retinal correspondence: points in each retina that share a visual direction. The fovea of each eye shares the straight-ahead direction.
  • Horopter: the set of points in space seen singly. Panum’s fusional area is a small zone around it where slightly disparate images are still fused, giving stereopsis.
  • Physiological diplopia: objects well in front of or behind fixation are seen double; this is normal and is used in some exercises.
  • Motor fusion: vergence movements that keep images on corresponding points. Fusional reserves (convergent and divergent, vertical, cyclo) are measured with prisms.

Development

  • Fixation and following develop in the first 2–3 months; accurate alignment by about 3–4 months; stereopsis emerges about 3–6 months and refines for years.
  • The sensitive period for amblyopia lasts through early childhood (roughly to 7–8 years, with some plasticity later), which is why early detection matters.
  • Intermittent misalignment in the first 3 months can be normal; a constant squint at any age, or any squint after 4 months, needs assessment.

Adults with a new squint lack these adaptations and get diplopia. Children adapt by suppression, which explains why a child with squint rarely complains of double vision.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

State Hering’s law and its clinical use.

Yoke muscles receive equal innervation; it explains why the secondary deviation (fixing with the paretic eye) exceeds the primary deviation.

In which gaze is the superior oblique tested?

Depression in adduction (down and in).

Why is VI nerve palsy a false localising sign?

Its long intracranial course makes it vulnerable to raised intracranial pressure anywhere.

List Worth’s three grades of binocular vision.

Simultaneous perception, fusion, stereopsis.

Why do children with squint rarely have diplopia?

They develop suppression (and sometimes ARC) during the sensitive period.

Self-test: 8 MCQs

Answer all, then press “Check my score”. Option order is mixed.

1. The yoke muscle of the right superior rectus is the:

Show answer

Answer: Left inferior oblique. Both elevate in right-up gaze.

2. The muscle tested in abduction for depression is the:

Show answer

Answer: Inferior rectus. Vertical recti act best in abduction.

3. Which nerve crosses completely and leaves the brainstem dorsally?

Show answer

Answer: IV. Trochlear.

4. Secondary deviation larger than primary is explained by:

Show answer

Answer: Hering's law. Paralytic squint.

5. The highest grade of binocular vision is:

Show answer

Answer: Stereopsis. Worth's grades.

6. The medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion causes:

Show answer

Answer: Internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Limited adduction.

7. The closest rectus insertion to the limbus is the:

Show answer

Answer: Medial rectus. About 5.5 mm.

8. A child with a constant squint usually avoids diplopia by:

Show answer

Answer: Suppression. Sensory adaptation.
