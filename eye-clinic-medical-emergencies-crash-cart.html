Medical Emergencies in the Eye Clinic: Crash Cart Checklist, Anaphylaxis, Fainting, Local Anaesthetic Toxicity and Fluorescein Reactions

For: Practising ophthalmologists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Every eye clinic needs oxygen, a bag-valve-mask, suction, an AED, adrenaline and a trained team, checked daily.
  • Anaphylaxis: call for help, lie the patient flat, give adrenaline 0.5 mg IM in adults (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL) into the outer thigh, repeat after 5 minutes if needed.
  • Fainting is the commonest emergency: lie flat, raise the legs, check pulse and glucose.
  • After a peribulbar or retrobulbar block, watch for local anaesthetic toxicity and brainstem spread; 20% lipid emulsion should be in the theatre.
  • Run a mock drill every few months so every staff member knows their role.
Print tool: Printable registers (daily crash cart check sheet).

Serious medical emergencies are rare in an eye clinic, but they happen: an elderly patient faints after dilating drops, a diabetic becomes hypoglycaemic during a long wait, a block causes a seizure, fluorescein causes anaphylaxis. The difference between a scare and a tragedy is preparation. This page gives a crash cart checklist and first-response steps. Doses are for adults unless stated; follow your national resuscitation guidelines and local protocols.

The first minute, for any collapse

  1. Shout for help and note the time.
  2. Check response, airway and breathing (look, listen, feel for no more than 10 seconds).
  3. If not breathing normally: start chest compressions (100 to 120 a minute, 5 to 6 cm deep), call an ambulance, and attach the AED as soon as it arrives.
  4. If breathing: lie the patient flat (or in the recovery position if unconscious), give oxygen, check pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and blood glucose.
  5. One person leads, one manages the airway, one fetches the crash cart, one calls the ambulance and records times and drugs.

Seal the cart with a numbered tag. Check it daily (oxygen, suction, AED light) and fully every month (every expiry date); record both in a log.

Common emergencies and what to do

SituationRecogniseAct
Fainting (vasovagal)Pale, sweaty, slow pulse, brief loss of consciousness, often during drops, blocks or injectionsLie flat, raise the legs, loosen tight clothing, oxygen. Check glucose. If the pulse stays slow and BP low: atropine 0.6 mg IV. Recovery should be quick; if not, think of other causes
Anaphylaxis (drugs, fluorescein, latex, antibiotics)Sudden breathing difficulty, wheeze, stridor, low BP, collapse, often with hives or swellingCall for help. Adrenaline IM into the outer thigh: adult and over 12 years 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL); 6 to 12 years 0.3 mg; under 6 years 0.15 mg. Repeat after 5 minutes if no better. Lie flat (sit up if breathing is the main problem), high-flow oxygen, IV fluid bolus (adult 500 to 1,000 mL; child 10 mL/kg). Antihistamine once stable. Transfer to hospital; reactions can return
HypoglycaemiaSweating, confusion, odd behaviour, seizure; glucose below about 4 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)If awake and able to swallow: 15 to 20 g of fast sugar (glucose tablets, juice), recheck after 15 minutes. If not: IV dextrose (for example 25% dextrose 50 to 100 mL into a large vein) or glucagon 1 mg IM
SeizureJerking, unresponsiveProtect from injury, do not put anything in the mouth, recovery position after, oxygen, check glucose. If longer than 5 minutes: midazolam 10 mg buccal, intranasal or IM (adult), or IV benzodiazepine as per protocol
Chest painCentral chest pain, sweating, breathlessnessStop the procedure, sit up, oxygen if saturation is low, ECG if available. Aspirin 300 mg to chew (if not allergic). GTN under the tongue if systolic BP is above 90 mmHg. Call an ambulance
Asthma or bronchospasmWheeze, breathlessness (note: beta-blocker eye drops can trigger it)Sit up, oxygen, salbutamol 4 to 10 puffs via a spacer, repeat as needed. If severe or not improving, treat as anaphylaxis if that is possible and transfer
Very high blood pressure before surgeryOften anxiety; check symptomsDo not lower it fast with sublingual drugs. Repeat after rest; if still very high or with symptoms, postpone surgery and refer

Eye-specific emergencies

SituationRecogniseAct
Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)Within minutes of a block: metallic taste, tingling lips, tinnitus, agitation, then seizures, arrhythmia or cardiac arrestStop injecting, call for help, airway and 100% oxygen, treat seizures with a benzodiazepine. Give 20% lipid emulsion: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 1 minute, then infusion at 15 mL/kg/hour; repeat the bolus up to twice at 5-minute intervals if needed; maximum total about 12 mL/kg. CPR may need to continue for a long time
Brainstem anaesthesia after a retrobulbar or peribulbar blockWithin minutes: drowsiness, confusion, apnoea, blindness or paralysis of the other eye, unstable BP or heart rateSupport breathing with a bag-valve-mask and oxygen, support circulation, call for anaesthetic help. It usually wears off as the drug is cleared, provided breathing is supported
Oculocardiac reflexSudden slowing of the heart during muscle traction or globe pressureAsk the surgeon to stop traction at once. If it persists: atropine 0.6 mg IV (child 0.02 mg/kg)
Retrobulbar haemorrhage after a blockRapid proptosis, tight lids, rising pressurePostpone surgery, measure IOP; if very high, lateral canthotomy and cantholysis
Fluorescein reactionsNausea and vomiting are common; itching and hives occasional; severe reactions rareStop injecting, keep the cannula in, treat by severity: antiemetic, antihistamine, or adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Ask about previous reactions before every angiogram

Prevention

  • Ask about allergies, previous reactions, diabetes, heart and lung disease, and blood thinners before procedures; see medical fitness before eye surgery.
  • Ask diabetic patients to eat normally before OPD procedures; keep sugar sweets at reception.
  • Keep an IV cannula in place for angiography and for blocks in high-risk patients.
  • Monitor ECG, saturation and BP during blocks and surgery, with someone trained in airway management available.
  • Train all staff in basic life support every 2 years; at least one person per shift should hold advanced life support training if you do blocks or sedation.
  • Run a mock drill every 3 to 6 months: collapse in the waiting area, anaphylaxis after fluorescein, LAST in theatre.

This page is general information for clinicians, not a substitute for resuscitation training or your local protocols. Rules differ between states and change over time; check with your State authorities, your indemnity insurer or a lawyer for your situation.
