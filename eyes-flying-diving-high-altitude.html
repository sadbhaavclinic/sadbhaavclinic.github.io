Flying, Scuba Diving and High Altitude with Eye Conditions: Gas Bubbles, Eye Surgery, Pilots, Divers and Mountaineers

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • After retinal surgery with a gas bubble, do not fly, drive over mountain passes or have nitrous oxide until the gas has gone; it can expand and cause blindness.
  • Flying after most other eye surgery (cataract, LASIK, SMILE, glaucoma laser) is usually fine within days; cabin air is dry, so use lubricant drops.
  • Pilots must report eye surgery and meet their regulator's standards; refractive surgery is usually acceptable once vision is stable.
  • Divers should wait for wounds to heal after eye surgery and avoid diving after glaucoma filtering surgery; prescription masks avoid lens loss.
  • At high altitude, protect against UV (snow blindness) and know that retinal bleeding and blurred vision can be early signs of altitude illness.

Changes in air pressure, oxygen and light affect the eye. For most people, flying, diving and mountain trips are safe. A few eye conditions and recent operations need care, and one, a gas bubble in the eye, makes flying dangerous.

Gas bubble in the eye = no flying. After retinal detachment or macular hole surgery, surgeons often fill the eye with gas (air, SF6 or C3F8). At altitude the gas expands, pressure in the eye rises sharply and the optic nerve can be permanently damaged. Do not fly, travel over high mountain roads, or have nitrous oxide anaesthesia until your surgeon confirms the gas has gone. Air lasts a few days, SF6 about 2 weeks, and C3F8 about 6 to 8 weeks. Wear the wristband you are given. Silicone oil does not expand and does not stop you flying.

Flying as a passenger

SituationUsual advice
Cataract surgeryFlying is usually fine after the first check, often the next day; carry your drops in hand luggage
LASIK, SMILE, PRK, ICLUsually fine within days; use lubricants because cabin air is very dry
GlaucomaFlying is safe; carry drops in hand luggage and keep to your timetable across time zones
Corneal transplantFlying is safe once your surgeon is happy with the wound
Retinal surgery with gasNo flying until the gas has gone (see above)
Retinal surgery with silicone oil, or a buckle onlyUsually safe to fly; check with your surgeon
Contact lensesDry cabin air: wear glasses on long flights, or use rewetting drops; never sleep in lenses on the plane

Pilots and air crew

  • Aviation regulators (such as the FAA in the USA, the UK CAA, EASA in Europe and the DGCA in India) set standards for distance, intermediate and near vision, colour vision, fields and binocular vision for each class of medical certificate.
  • Glasses and contact lenses are allowed for most classes, often with a requirement to carry a spare pair.
  • Refractive surgery (LASIK, SMILE, PRK) and cataract surgery are generally acceptable once vision is stable and there are no troublesome glare or halos, but they must be declared, and a waiting period and report are usually required.
  • Colour vision is often tested with Ishihara plates first, and with further tests (such as the CAD test or lantern tests) if plates are failed.
  • Monovision correction is usually not accepted for flying.
  • Always check the current rules of your own regulator and speak to your aviation medical examiner before any eye operation.

Scuba diving and snorkelling

SituationTypical advice (confirm with your surgeon)
Mask squeezeBreathe out through the nose into the mask while descending; mask squeeze can cause bleeding under the conjunctiva and lid bruising
Contact lensesSoft lenses can be worn under a mask but may be lost if the mask floods; a prescription mask is safer. Avoid getting water in the eye with lenses in
After cataract surgeryUsually no diving for about 2 to 4 weeks, until the wound has healed
After LASIKUsually about 2 to 4 weeks
After PRK or SMILEUsually about 4 weeks, once the surface has healed
After corneal transplantSeveral months; ask your surgeon
After glaucoma filtering surgery (trabeculectomy, tubes)Diving is generally discouraged because of infection risk and pressure changes
Gas bubble in the eyeNo diving until the gas has fully gone

Mountains and high altitude

  • Snow blindness (photokeratitis): UV reflected from snow and ice burns the cornea within hours, causing severe pain and watering. Wear category 4 glacier glasses or goggles with side protection at altitude (category 4 lenses are too dark for driving).
  • High-altitude retinopathy: retinal haemorrhages and swollen veins are common above about 5,000 metres. Most cause no symptoms, but blurred vision at altitude can accompany serious altitude illness: descend and seek help.
  • Old radial keratotomy (RK): vision can shift towards long-sightedness at altitude; carry spare glasses.
  • LASIK and SMILE are generally stable at altitude.
  • Contact lenses in cold, dry, dusty camps carry a higher infection risk; daily disposables and glasses as backup are safest.
  • Cold and wind dry the eyes; use preservative-free lubricants that will not freeze in an inner pocket.

Before you go

  • Pack spare glasses, a copy of your prescription, and enough drops for the trip plus a week.
  • Keep drops in hand luggage; liquids rules usually allow medicines with a prescription.
  • Know where to find eye care at your destination.
  • If you have had eye surgery recently, ask your surgeon specifically about flying, diving and altitude.
