Pupils Made Easy: Anisocoria, Horner, Adie and RAPD

Last updated: October 2026

Pupils confuse people because several pathways and four eye drops are taught at once. Untangle them with three questions: is there an afferent problem, is there anisocoria, and does the pupil react better to near than to light?

Three rules that solve most pupil cases

1. An afferent defect never makes the pupils unequalLight from either eye drives both pupils equally, so a blind eye still has a pupil the same size as the other. Afferent disease shows only as a RAPD.
2. Find the abnormal pupil with the lightsAnisocoria bigger in the dark = the small pupil cannot dilate (Horner or physiological). Bigger in bright light = the large pupil cannot constrict (III palsy, Adie, drugs, iris damage).
3. Light–near dissociation has few causesThe near pathway runs more ventrally and the ciliary muscle has 30 times more nerve fibres than the sphincter. So the pupil may react to near but not light in dorsal midbrain lesions, Argyll Robertson pupils, Adie pupils (after regrowth) and severe afferent loss.

The anisocoria algorithm

StepFindingMeaning
1Anisocoria the same in light and dark, ≤1 mmPhysiological (about 1 in 5 people); check old photographs
2Greater in the dark; small pupil dilates slowly (dilation lag)Horner syndrome: confirm with drops
3Greater in bright light + ptosis and motility deficitThird nerve palsy: urgent imaging if the pupil is involved
4Greater in bright light, poor light reaction, slow tonic near reaction, sectoral palsyAdie tonic pupil
5Greater in bright light, fixed, no ptosis or motility problemPharmacological (atropine, scopolamine patch, plants) or iris damage: use 1% pilocarpine

Memory hook: Dark exaggerates the Defective dilator; Light exaggerates the Lazy constrictor.

The eye drops, sorted

DropUseResult
Cocaine 4–10%Confirm Horner (blocks noradrenaline reuptake)Normal pupil dilates, Horner pupil does not: anisocoria ≥0.8 mm after drops is positive
Apraclonidine 0.5–1%Confirm Horner (weak α1 agonist)Reversal: the Horner pupil dilates (denervation supersensitivity) and the ptosis improves. Can be falsely negative in the first days to weeks
Hydroxyamphetamine 1%Localise a Horner (releases noradrenaline from the 3rd neuron)Dilates if the 3rd neuron is intact (1st or 2nd order lesion); fails if postganglionic (3rd order)
Pilocarpine 0.1% (or 0.125%)Adie pupil (cholinergic supersensitivity)Adie pupil constricts, a normal pupil barely does
Pilocarpine 1%Fixed dilated pupilConstricts in III palsy and Adie; does not constrict after atropine-like drugs or with iris sphincter damage

Memory hook: Hydroxyamphetamine needs a living third neuron to work, so it fails only with a third-order (postganglionic) lesion.

Horner syndrome by neuron

OrderWhereSweating lossCauses to think of
1st (central)Hypothalamus to C8–T2Whole side of the bodyLateral medullary (Wallenberg) stroke, syrinx, MS, cord tumours
2nd (preganglionic)Lung apex, neckFacePancoast tumour, neck or thyroid surgery, chest drains, neuroblastoma in children
3rd (postganglionic)Internal carotid to the orbitNone, or a small patch of the foreheadCarotid dissection (painful Horner: urgent), cavernous sinus lesions, cluster headache
  • The triad is mild ptosis (1–2 mm, Müller's muscle), miosis with dilation lag, and anhidrosis; the lower lid may sit higher (inverse ptosis), giving apparent enophthalmos.
  • Congenital Horner: the affected iris is lighter (heterochromia), because sympathetic input is needed for iris pigment in early life.
  • New painful Horner = carotid dissection until proven otherwise: same-day CT or MR angiography of head and neck.
  • Horner in a child without a clear cause: look for neuroblastoma (abdomen, chest, neck imaging and urine catecholamines).

  • Pupil sparing: pupil fibres run on the outside of the nerve, so compression hits them early, while ischaemia (diabetes, hypertension) damages the core and usually spares the pupil. A complete III palsy with a normal pupil is reassuring; a partial palsy with a normal pupil still needs imaging and close follow-up.
  • Adie pupil starts large and becomes smaller over years ("little old Adie"). Vermiform movements of the iris border are seen at the slit lamp.

Memory hook: ARP: Accommodation Reflex Present, light reflex absent.

RAPD in practice

  • Swing the light every 2–3 seconds; when it reaches the bad eye, both pupils dilate.
  • Grade with neutral density filters over the good eye (0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2 log units) or in a simple 1+ to 4+ scale.
  • If one pupil is fixed or dilated, watch only the working pupil (reverse RAPD): it still tells you which eye is bad.
  • Gives a RAPD: optic nerve disease, large retinal lesions (CRAO, RD, extensive PRP), asymmetric chiasmal compression, optic tract (opposite eye), dense amblyopia (mild).
  • Does not: cataract, corneal scars, refractive error, macular hole or small macular lesions, functional loss. A dense cataract may even give a small RAPD in the other eye.

Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome

Mid-dilated pupils with light–near dissociation, upgaze palsy, convergence–retraction nystagmus on attempted upgaze, eyelid retraction (Collier sign) and skew deviation. Think pinealoma in a young man, stroke or MS in adults, and hydrocephalus or shunt failure in children.

Where residents slip

  • Looking for anisocoria in a blind eye: an afferent defect alone never makes the pupils unequal.
  • Testing anisocoria only in room light: always compare dim and bright light.
  • Using 1% pilocarpine to diagnose Adie: only the dilute drop (0.1%) shows supersensitivity.
  • Calling a painful new Horner a migraine: image the carotid the same day.
  • Doing an apraclonidine test the day after a carotid dissection: supersensitivity takes time, so cocaine is more reliable early.
