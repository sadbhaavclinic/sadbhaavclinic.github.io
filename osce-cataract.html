Cataract OSCE Stations: 19 Spot Stations on Biometry, Complications, Devices and Theatre Events

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 19 spot stations, 76 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 19 original stations cover the cataract topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. A-scan biometer, 2. IOL power formulas, 3. Wound burn, 4. Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome, 5. Lens–iris diaphragm retropulsion syndrome, 6. IOL opacification, 7. Dysphotopsia, 8. Cystoid macular oedema after cataract surgery, 9. Anaesthesia for cataract surgery, 10. Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery, 11. Toric IOL, 12. Ophthalmic viscosurgical devices, 13. Pseudoexfoliation, 14. Phaco fluidics, 15. Posterior polar cataract, 16. Descemet membrane detachment, 17. Dislocated IOL, 18. Endophthalmitis after cataract surgery, 19. Posterior capsule rupture

Station 1: A-scan biometer

The examiner shows you: A table-top ultrasound unit with a hand-held probe and a printout of axial length readings.

  1. Identify the instrument and two uses.
    A-scan ultrasound biometer. Uses: axial length for IOL power calculation, and measuring anterior chamber depth and lens thickness; also helps in eyes too dense for optical biometry.
  2. Give two drawbacks of contact A-scan.
    Pressing on the cornea shortens the axial length (roughly 1 mm of error gives about 2.5–3 D of refractive error), and the result depends on the operator, with a learning curve.
  3. How can these be overcome?
    Immersion A-scan (a scleral shell filled with saline avoids corneal compression), or optical biometry.
  4. Optical or ultrasound biometry: which is more accurate, and when does optical fail?
    Optical biometry is more accurate and repeatable: it is non-contact and measures along the visual axis to the retinal pigment epithelium. It fails with dense cataract, posterior subcapsular opacity on the axis, poor fixation or corneal scarring, when ultrasound is needed.

Station 2: IOL power formulas

The examiner shows you: A biometry printout with axial length 21.2 mm and average K 46.5 D, and a list of formulas.

  1. Write the SRK formula.
    P = A − 2.5L − 0.9K, where P is IOL power, A the lens constant, L axial length (mm) and K average keratometry (D). A first-generation regression formula, no longer used alone.
  2. Which formulas suit short and long eyes?
    Modern formulas (Barrett Universal II, Kane, Hill-RBF) work well across all lengths. Of the older ones, Hoffer Q or Haigis for short eyes (under 22 mm) and SRK/T for long eyes (over 26 mm).
  3. Why are short eyes difficult?
    A small error in predicting the effective lens position causes a large refractive error because the IOL power is high.
  4. How do you calculate IOL power after LASIK or PRK?
    Standard K readings overestimate corneal power after myopic laser, causing hyperopic surprises. Use post-refractive formulas (Barrett True-K, Haigis-L, the ASCRS online calculator) or total keratometry from optical biometry; the clinical history method needs pre-operative data and is unreliable.

Station 3: Wound burn

The examiner shows you: An intraoperative photograph: a white, opaque area at the main phaco incision with the wound edges pulled apart.

  1. Name the complication.
    Thermal (phaco) wound burn.
  2. What are the signs?
    Whitening of the corneal stroma around the incision, wound contraction and gaping, and a leaking incision that will not self-seal.
  3. Give risk factors.
    Dense nucleus needing high energy, continuous ultrasound, occluded aspiration or irrigation (for example dispersive OVD blocking the tip or a tight incision squeezing the sleeve), and low flow settings.
  4. How do you manage and prevent it?
    Close the wound with sutures (radial, mattress or cross-stitch), sometimes tissue adhesive or a patch graft; induced astigmatism is common. Prevent with pulsed, burst or torsional modes, adequate flow, an incision that fits the sleeve, and clearing OVD around the tip before starting phaco.

Station 4: Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome

The examiner shows you: A video still: a poorly dilated iris billowing in the irrigation flow and prolapsing out of the main incision.

  1. Name the condition and its signs.
    Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS): a flaccid iris that billows in normal flow, a tendency to prolapse through the incisions, and progressive pupil constriction during surgery.
  2. Which drugs are associated?
    Alpha-1A blockers, especially tamsulosin; also alfuzosin, doxazosin, terazosin, and less often finasteride and some antipsychotics.
  3. Should the drug be stopped before surgery?
    Stopping it does not reliably prevent IFIS, because the effect on the iris dilator persists. The key is to ask about it and plan.
  4. How do you manage it?
    Strong pre-operative dilation, intracameral phenylephrine or adrenaline (epinephrine), a dispersive or viscoadaptive OVD, well-constructed incisions, low flow and bottle height, and pupil expansion devices (Malyugin ring or iris hooks) if needed.

Station 5: Lens–iris diaphragm retropulsion syndrome

The examiner shows you: During phaco, the anterior chamber suddenly becomes very deep, the pupil dilates and the patient complains of pain.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Lens–iris diaphragm retropulsion syndrome (LIDRS), also called reverse pupillary block.
  2. What is the mechanism?
    Irrigation fluid passes into the posterior chamber; the iris then acts as a one-way valve against the lens, so pressure in the anterior chamber exceeds that behind the iris and pushes the diaphragm backwards.
  3. Which eyes are prone to it?
    High myopia, previous vitrectomy, and eyes with weak zonules or a large pupil-to-lens contact.
  4. How do you manage it?
    Lift the pupil margin with a second instrument to break the block, and lower the bottle height or infusion pressure.

Station 6: IOL opacification

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph of a pseudophakic eye: the IOL optic is diffusely hazy, with fine granular deposits within it, years after uneventful surgery.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    IOL opacification.
  2. Give causes by lens material.
    Hydrophilic acrylic: calcification, especially after air or gas in the anterior chamber (for example after DMEK) or in diabetes. Hydrophobic acrylic: glistenings (fluid vacuoles) and subsurface nanoglistenings. Silicone: coating by silicone oil. Older PMMA: snowflake degeneration.
  3. How is it prevented?
    Choose a well-proven hydrophobic acrylic IOL in eyes likely to need later air or gas tamponade, and avoid silicone IOLs in eyes likely to need silicone oil.
  4. How is it treated?
    IOL exchange if vision is significantly affected; laser cannot clear opacification within the optic.

Station 7: Dysphotopsia

The examiner shows you: A pseudophakic patient draws a dark crescent on the temporal side of their vision; another describes arcs and streaks of light at night.

  1. Name the two types.
    Negative dysphotopsia (a dark temporal crescent or shadow) and positive dysphotopsia (arcs, streaks, starbursts or flashes of light).
  2. Which IOL features are linked?
    Square-edged, high-refractive-index acrylic IOLs and multifocal designs. Positive dysphotopsia relates to reflections from the IOL edge; negative dysphotopsia to a gap in light reaching the nasal retina at the edge of the optic.
  3. What is the first-line management?
    Reassurance: most symptoms fade with time and neuroadaptation within months.
  4. What if negative dysphotopsia persists?
    Reverse optic capture (optic in front of the anterior capsule), a sulcus piggyback IOL, or IOL exchange with sulcus fixation. Nd:YAG nasal anterior capsulotomy has been reported.

Station 8: Cystoid macular oedema after cataract surgery

The examiner shows you: Six weeks after uneventful surgery, vision has dropped from 6/6 to 6/18. The OCT shows petaloid cystic spaces in the outer plexiform layer with central thickening.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Pseudophakic cystoid macular oedema (Irvine–Gass syndrome), typically peaking 4–8 weeks after surgery.
  2. What is the mechanism?
    Surgery-induced release of prostaglandins and other inflammatory mediators increases perifoveal capillary permeability.
  3. Give risk factors.
    Diabetes, uveitis, epiretinal membrane, previous CME or vein occlusion, posterior capsule rupture with vitreous loss, and possibly topical prostaglandin analogues.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Topical NSAID plus topical steroid as first line. If it persists: sub-Tenon or intravitreal steroid (triamcinolone or dexamethasone implant). Treat vitreous incarceration in the wound if present.

Station 9: Anaesthesia for cataract surgery

The examiner shows you: A tray with vials labelled 2% lignocaine, 0.5% bupivacaine and hyaluronidase, a 25 G needle and a blunt sub-Tenon cannula.

  1. Name the types of local anaesthesia for eye surgery.
    Topical, topical with intracameral, sub-Tenon, peribulbar and retrobulbar blocks; a facial nerve block (for example van Lint or O'Brien) is now rarely needed.
  2. What does each drug do?
    Lignocaine (lidocaine) 2%: fast onset, shorter action. Bupivacaine 0.5%: slower onset, longer action. Hyaluronidase: breaks down tissue hyaluronic acid so the anaesthetic spreads faster and better.
  3. What is used intracamerally?
    Preservative-free lidocaine 1%, sometimes combined with phenylephrine in a commercial preparation.
  4. Name serious complications of needle blocks.
    Retrobulbar haemorrhage, globe perforation (higher risk in long myopic eyes), optic nerve injury, and brainstem anaesthesia from injection into the optic nerve sheath.

Station 10: Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery

The examiner shows you: A large laser console with a patient interface and docking cone beside an operating microscope.

  1. Identify the machine.
    A femtosecond laser for laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS).
  2. Which steps can it perform?
    Anterior capsulotomy, lens fragmentation, corneal incisions and arcuate (astigmatic) incisions.
  3. Give advantages.
    A precise, round, well-centred capsulotomy, less phaco energy for dense lenses, and reproducible incisions.
  4. Give disadvantages, and what did trials show?
    Cost, extra time, docking discomfort, pupil constriction after the laser, and capsular block syndrome if hydrodissection is forceful. The FACT and FEMCAT trials found outcomes equivalent to standard phaco and the laser not cost-effective.

Station 11: Toric IOL

The examiner shows you: An intraoperative image with a toric IOL in the bag and axis marks on the cornea, and a topography showing 2.5 D with-the-rule astigmatism.

  1. What is the procedure?
    Implantation of a toric IOL to correct regular corneal astigmatism.
  2. How else can astigmatism be reduced at cataract surgery?
    Placing the main incision on the steep axis, limbal or corneal relaxing incisions, opposite clear corneal incisions, and femtosecond arcuate incisions.
  3. How is the axis marked?
    Manual reference marks at the limbus with the patient sitting up (to avoid cyclotorsion when lying down), then intraoperative axis marking; or image-guided systems that register iris and limbal landmarks.
  4. Why does rotation matter?
    Each degree of misalignment loses about 3.3% of the cylinder correction; at 30° there is no net correction.

Station 12: Ophthalmic viscosurgical devices

The examiner shows you: Three syringes of OVD with different labels: sodium hyaluronate, a chondroitin sulphate and hyaluronate combination, and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose.

  1. What are the main OVD components and their sources?
    Sodium hyaluronate (from rooster combs or bacterial fermentation), chondroitin sulphate (from shark cartilage) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (from plant cellulose).
  2. Contrast cohesive and dispersive OVDs.
    Cohesive (high molecular weight hyaluronate): maintains space well and is removed easily in one piece; good for inflating the bag and IOL insertion. Dispersive (lower molecular weight, for example chondroitin sulphate with hyaluronate): coats and protects the endothelium and stays in place, but is harder to remove.
  3. What is the soft-shell technique?
    A dispersive OVD is placed first to coat the endothelium, then a cohesive OVD is injected beneath it to pressurise the chamber (described by Arshinoff).
  4. Why remove OVD at the end of surgery?
    Retained OVD blocks the trabecular meshwork and causes a pressure spike within hours.

Station 13: Pseudoexfoliation

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph after dilation: grey-white flaky material in a central disc and a peripheral band on the anterior lens capsule, with a clear zone between.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Pseudoexfoliation syndrome.
  2. Why is there a clear zone?
    The moving pupil margin rubs the material off the mid-periphery of the capsule.
  3. What may gonioscopy show?
    Heavy, patchy trabecular pigmentation and a pigmented line anterior to Schwalbe's line (Sampaolesi line).
  4. What are the implications for cataract surgery?
    Poor dilation, weak zonules (phacodonesis, risk of zonular dialysis and later IOL-bag dislocation), raised risk of IOP spikes. Plan pupil expansion, a capsular tension ring, gentle manoeuvres and careful post-operative IOP checks.

Station 14: Phaco fluidics

The examiner shows you: An irrigation–aspiration handpiece removing cortex, and a phaco machine screen showing bottle height, aspiration flow and vacuum.

  1. Which step is shown?
    Irrigation and aspiration of the cortex.
  2. Name the pump types.
    Peristaltic (flow-based: vacuum builds only on occlusion) and venturi or diaphragm (vacuum-based: vacuum is always present, so material is drawn to the tip). Some machines offer both.
  3. What is surge, and how is it reduced?
    When an occluding fragment is suddenly cleared, fluid rushes into the tip and the chamber shallows. Reduce it with adequate bottle height or active pressurised infusion, lower vacuum, less compliant tubing and machine surge-control features.
  4. How does bottle height relate to IOP?
    Each 10 cm of bottle height adds roughly 7–8 mmHg of infusion pressure.

Station 15: Posterior polar cataract

The examiner shows you: A retroillumination photograph: a dense, round, white central posterior plaque with concentric rings, in a young adult with a family history.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Posterior polar cataract.
  2. Why is surgery risky?
    The posterior capsule may be thin or already open beneath the opacity, so there is a high risk of posterior capsule rupture and dropped nucleus.
  3. Name other types of congenital cataract.
    Lamellar (zonular, the commonest), nuclear, anterior polar, sutural, cerulean (blue dot), coronary and total cataract.
  4. What surgical precautions do you take?
    Avoid hydrodissection (use hydrodelineation only), keep a stable, well-formed chamber with low flow and bottle height, use gentle manoeuvres, and have anterior vitrectomy ready.

Station 16: Descemet membrane detachment

The examiner shows you: A day-one post-operative eye with a localised, sharply demarcated area of corneal oedema; anterior segment OCT shows a detached, scrolled membrane near the incision.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Descemet membrane detachment.
  2. What causes it?
    Blunt instruments or a poorly constructed incision engaging Descemet membrane, inadvertent injection of fluid or OVD behind it, and pre-existing endothelial disease.
  3. How does anterior segment OCT help?
    It confirms the diagnosis when the cornea is too hazy to see through, measures the extent and separation, and guides and monitors treatment.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Small, peripheral, planar detachments may settle on their own. Larger or central ones: descemetopexy with an air or gas (SF6 or C3F8) bubble in the anterior chamber; sometimes full-thickness sutures; endothelial keratoplasty if it fails.

Station 17: Dislocated IOL

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph of a pseudophakic eye with the IOL decentred inferiorly and its edge visible in the pupil; the patient describes double vision and glare.

  1. What has happened?
    IOL subluxation or dislocation, either early (from posterior capsule rupture or IOL placed outside the bag) or late (in-the-bag dislocation from progressive zonular weakness).
  2. Give causes of late in-the-bag dislocation.
    Pseudoexfoliation (commonest), high myopia, trauma, retinitis pigmentosa, previous vitrectomy and uveitis.
  3. How does the patient present?
    Blurred vision, glare, haloes, monocular diplopia, a change in refraction, and sometimes an IOL edge visible in the pupil.
  4. What are the management options?
    Observation if minimal; repositioning and suture or scleral fixation of the existing IOL; or exchange for an anterior chamber, iris-fixated (iris-claw) or scleral-fixated IOL (for example flanged intrascleral haptic fixation).

Station 18: Endophthalmitis after cataract surgery

The examiner shows you: Three days after surgery: pain, reduced vision to hand movements, lid swelling, hypopyon and vitreous haze.

  1. What is the diagnosis, and how common is it?
    Acute post-operative endophthalmitis. With intracameral antibiotic prophylaxis the rate is under about 1 in 1,000 cases.
  2. What is the main differential?
    Toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS): onset within 12–24 hours, limbus-to-limbus corneal oedema, less pain and no vitreous involvement; it is sterile and responds to steroids.
  3. How do you manage it?
    Urgent vitreous tap (and aqueous) for culture, intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg/0.1 mL and ceftazidime 2.25 mg/0.1 mL (or amikacin). By EVS, immediate vitrectomy if vision is light perception only.
  4. Name causes of late-onset endophthalmitis.
    Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes in the capsular bag, fungi, and bleb-related infection.

Station 19: Posterior capsule rupture

The examiner shows you: During cortex aspiration, a nuclear fragment suddenly sinks and the chamber deepens; a clear strand of vitreous is seen in the pupil.

  1. What has happened?
    Posterior capsule rupture with vitreous presentation.
  2. What are the early signs?
    Sudden deepening of the chamber, a fragment that falls back or will not follow the tip, a visible capsule edge or a red reflex change, and vitreous strands.
  3. What are your first steps?
    Stop, keep the instrument still, inject dispersive OVD before withdrawing to keep the chamber formed, lower flow and bottle height, and assess the size of the tear.
  4. How do you manage vitreous and the IOL?
    Bimanual anterior vitrectomy (triamcinolone helps show vitreous), then place the IOL in the sulcus with optic capture through an intact anterior capsulorhexis, or in the bag if the tear is small. Refer for vitrectomy if nuclear fragments fall into the vitreous.
