MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Oculoplasty, Orbit and Lacrimal (51 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 51 oculoplasty, orbit and lacrimal theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Eyelids

OL 1. Anatomy of the eyelid with a labelled vertical section; lid margin. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Vertical section of the eyelid (front to back)

  • Skin (thinnest in the body), subcutaneous tissue (no fat), orbicularis oculi (pretarsal, preseptal, orbital), submuscular areolar tissue, tarsal plate with orbital septum above, levator aponeurosis and Müller muscle (upper lid), palpebral conjunctiva.
  • Upper lid crease formed by levator fibres to the skin; tarsus about 10 mm high in the upper lid, 4–5 mm in the lower.

Lid margin

  • Anterior lamella (lashes, Zeis and Moll glands), grey line (muscle of Riolan; surgical plane), posterior lamella (meibomian gland orifices), mucocutaneous junction; lacrimal punctum medially.

Blood and nerve supply

  • Marginal and peripheral arcades from the ophthalmic and facial arteries; lymphatics to pre-auricular (lateral) and submandibular (medial) nodes; sensation from V1 and V2; motor: facial (orbicularis), oculomotor (levator), sympathetic (Müller).

Revise from: Eyelid Malpositions: Entropion, Ectropion, Trichiasis and Chalazion

OL 2. Describe and discuss the principles and techniques of lid reconstruction (including upper lid) — Cutler-Beard, Mustardé flap and Z-plasty. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principles of lid reconstruction

  • Reconstruct both lamellae (posterior lamella with a lining and rigid support; anterior with a vascular flap); one lamella must have a blood supply; maintain lid height, contour, closure, a smooth margin and lateral canthal fixation; protect the cornea.

Size of defect

  • Under 25%: direct closure (pentagonal wedge).
  • 25–50%: direct closure with lateral cantholysis, Tenzel semicircular flap.
  • Over 50%: upper lid: Cutler–Beard (bridge flap from lower lid, two stages), lower lid to upper lid switch flap; lower lid: Hughes tarsoconjunctival flap (two stages), Mustardé cheek rotation flap, free tarsal grafts with skin muscle flaps.

Z-plasty

  • Two triangular flaps transposed to lengthen and re-orient scars; used to release vertical scar traction and in epicanthus (Mustardé double Z) and cicatricial ectropion.

Revise from: Eyelid Tumours: BCC, SCC, Sebaceous Gland Carcinoma and Melanoma

OL 3. Entropion — aetiology, classification and correction (congenital, senile, cicatricial, trachomatous). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Congenital (rare), involutional (senile: lower lid, horizontal laxity, disinsertion of lower lid retractors, overriding preseptal orbicularis), cicatricial (trachoma, SJS, OCP, chemical burns: posterior lamellar shortening), spastic (acute, irritation).

Correction

  • Involutional: temporary taping, Quickert everting sutures, Weis procedure (transverse lid split with sutures), Jones (retractor plication), lateral tarsal strip for laxity; combined procedures.
  • Cicatricial upper lid (trachomatous): tarsal fracture or terminal tarsal rotation, Jaesche–Arlt (lid split with graft), anterior lamellar reposition, grafts of mucous membrane or hard palate to lengthen the posterior lamella; WHO bilamellar tarsal rotation for trachomatous trichiasis.
  • Congenital: excise skin and muscle fold, lid crease formation.

Revise from: Eyelid Malpositions: Entropion, Ectropion, Trichiasis and Chalazion

OL 4. Ectropion — classification and surgical management (senile, paralytic, cicatricial; Kuhnt-Szymanowski); lagophthalmos. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Involutional (horizontal laxity, medial canthal tendon laxity), paralytic (facial nerve palsy), cicatricial (anterior lamellar shortening: burns, sun damage, scars), mechanical (tumours, oedema), congenital.

Surgical management

  • Involutional: lateral tarsal strip, wedge excision with lateral canthal tendon tightening (Bick), Kuhnt–Szymanowski (Smith modification: lid shortening combined with skin muscle blepharoplasty), medial spindle (punctal ectropion), Lazy-T procedure (medial ectropion).
  • Paralytic: lateral tarsal strip, medial canthoplasty, fascia sling, gold weight for upper lid, tarsorrhaphy.
  • Cicatricial: release scar, full-thickness skin graft (retroauricular or upper lid), Z-plasty, mid-face lift.

Lagophthalmos

  • Incomplete closure: facial palsy, proptosis, scarring, leprosy, after ptosis surgery.
  • Management: lubricants, taping, moisture chambers, botulinum-induced ptosis, gold or platinum weights, tarsorrhaphy, lateral tarsal strip, facial reanimation.

Revise from: Eyelid Malpositions: Entropion, Ectropion, Trichiasis and Chalazion

OL 5. Tarsorrhaphy. DNB / UNIV · outline

Tarsorrhaphy

  • Partial or complete surgical fusion of the upper and lower lids to protect the cornea.

Types

  • Temporary: suture tarsorrhaphy (bolsters), glue tarsorrhaphy, botulinum-induced ptosis (protective ptosis).
  • Permanent: lateral, medial or central; lid margins de-epithelialised (posterior lamella split from grey line) and sutured; pillar tarsorrhaphy.

Indications

  • Exposure keratopathy (facial palsy, proptosis), neurotrophic keratopathy, persistent epithelial defects, after keratoplasty in high-risk eyes, severe dry eye, chemical burns, to protect grafts.

Complications

  • Cosmesis, restricted visual field, trichiasis, lid margin notching, premature opening, difficulty examining the eye.
OL 6. Floppy eyelid syndrome; distichiasis; telecanthus and epicanthus. DNB / UNIV · outline

Floppy eyelid syndrome

  • Easily everted, rubbery upper lids with papillary conjunctivitis; overweight middle-aged men; strong association with obstructive sleep apnoea (refer for sleep study), keratoconus, glaucoma, NAION.
  • Management: lid shields or taping at night, treat OSA (CPAP), lubricants, horizontal lid shortening (wedge resection, lateral tarsal strip).

Distichiasis

  • Extra row of lashes emerging from the meibomian gland orifices; congenital (lymphoedema-distichiasis syndrome: FOXC2) or acquired (cicatricial: SJS, OCP, chemical injury).
  • Management: lubricants, bandage lens, electrolysis, cryotherapy (to the posterior lamella), lid splitting with excision of follicles, radiofrequency ablation.

Telecanthus and epicanthus

  • Telecanthus: increased intercanthal distance with normal interpupillary distance (blepharophimosis, trauma, fetal alcohol syndrome); vs hypertelorism (wide bony orbits).
  • Epicanthus: vertical skin fold at the medial canthus (palpebralis, tarsalis, inversus, supraciliaris).
  • Management: medial canthoplasty (transnasal wiring for traumatic telecanthus), Mustardé double Z-plasty or Y-V plasty for epicanthus.
OL 7. Blepharitis — clinical types and management; chalazion (differential diagnosis and histopathology). DNB / UNIV · outline

Blepharitis

  • Anterior: staphylococcal (crusts, collarettes, madarosis, marginal keratitis), seborrhoeic (greasy scales), Demodex (cylindrical dandruff at lash base).
  • Posterior: meibomian gland dysfunction (capped glands, frothy tears, lid margin telangiectasia), rosacea.
  • Management: lid hygiene and warm compresses, antibiotic ointment, tea tree oil or lotilaner for Demodex, oral doxycycline or azithromycin for MGD and rosacea, short course of topical steroids for inflammation, lubricants.

Chalazion

  • Chronic lipogranulomatous inflammation of a meibomian gland (blocked duct, extravasated lipid).
  • Histopathology: lipogranuloma with epithelioid cells, giant cells around lipid vacuoles, lymphocytes, plasma cells; no caseation.
  • Differential diagnosis: hordeolum, sebaceous gland carcinoma (recurrent chalazion in elderly: biopsy), BCC, Merkel cell carcinoma, granulomas (TB, sarcoid), cyst of Moll or Zeis, pyogenic granuloma.
  • Management: warm compresses, intralesional triamcinolone, incision and curettage; histopathology for recurrent or atypical lesions.
OL 8. Differential diagnosis of a painless, nodular, progressively increasing swelling of the lower lid in a 60-year-old male. · outline

Painless nodular progressive lower lid swelling in a 60-year-old man: differential diagnosis

  • Basal cell carcinoma (commonest: pearly nodule, rolled edges, telangiectasia, ulcer), squamous cell carcinoma, sebaceous gland carcinoma (upper lid more often; recurrent chalazion), Merkel cell carcinoma (violaceous, rapid), melanoma, keratoacanthoma (rapid, crater), lymphoma, metastases.
  • Benign: chalazion, epidermal inclusion cyst, cyst of Moll (hidrocystoma), seborrhoeic keratosis, papilloma, xanthelasma (plaque), naevus.

Approach

  • History (duration, growth rate, bleeding, prior lesions), examination (loss of lashes: malignancy, ulceration, fixation, lid margin distortion, lymph nodes), photograph.
  • Biopsy (incisional or excisional), map biopsies for sebaceous carcinoma.

Management

  • Excision with margin control (frozen section or Mohs), reconstruction by defect size, adjuvant therapy for high-risk tumours.
OL 9. Differential diagnosis and management of a growth of the lateral part of the eyelids. · outline

Growths of the lateral eyelid region: differential diagnosis

  • Benign: dermoid cyst (at the zygomaticofrontal suture: young), lacrimal gland prolapse or tumour (upper lateral lid: S-shaped lid), chalazion, papilloma, seborrhoeic keratosis, keratoacanthoma, xanthelasma (rare laterally), haemangioma (children).
  • Malignant: BCC (lateral canthus less common than medial), SCC, sebaceous carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, melanoma, lymphoma (salmon patch in the fornix).
  • Inflammatory: dacryoadenitis, orbital cellulitis, preseptal cellulitis.

Evaluation

  • Palpation (mobility, bony attachment), globe displacement, imaging (CT for dermoids and lacrimal lesions), biopsy.

Management

  • Excision (intact for dermoids and pleomorphic adenomas), malignant: excision with clear margins and reconstruction (lateral canthal reconstruction, Tenzel flap), radiotherapy for lymphoma.
OL 10. Malignant lid tumours — basal cell, squamous cell, sebaceous (meibomian) gland and Merkel cell carcinoma: clinical and pathological features and treatment. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Basal cell carcinoma

  • Commonest lid malignancy (about 90%); lower lid and medial canthus; nodular (pearly rolled edges, telangiectasia, ulcer: rodent ulcer), morphoeic (infiltrative, ill-defined), superficial; locally invasive, rarely metastasises.
  • Histology: palisading basaloid cells, retraction artefact.

Squamous cell carcinoma

  • Keratinising, ulcerated lesion; can spread via nerves (perineural) and to lymph nodes.

Sebaceous gland carcinoma

  • Meibomian or Zeis glands; upper lid, older women, Asian populations (common in India); masquerades as recurrent chalazion or chronic blepharoconjunctivitis; pagetoid spread; aggressive with lymph node and distant metastasis.
  • Histology: foamy vacuolated cells, oil red O stain (fresh tissue); map biopsies of conjunctiva.

Merkel cell carcinoma

  • Rapidly growing violaceous nodule in older patients; neuroendocrine; high metastatic risk; Merkel cell polyomavirus.

Treatment

  • Excision with margin control (Mohs or frozen-section), reconstruction; sentinel node biopsy for sebaceous and Merkel; radiotherapy, topical chemotherapy (mitomycin for pagetoid spread), vismodegib for advanced BCC, immunotherapy (avelumab for Merkel); exenteration for orbital invasion.

Revise from: Eyelid Tumours: BCC, SCC, Sebaceous Gland Carcinoma and Melanoma

OL 11. Symblepharon; xanthelasma; molluscum contagiosum. DNB / UNIV · outline

Symblepharon

  • Adhesion between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva; causes: chemical burns, SJS, OCP, trachoma, trauma, surgery, radiation.
  • Management: prevention (symblepharon rings, amniotic membrane early), excision with mucous membrane or amniotic membrane grafts, fornix reconstruction, treat underlying disease (immunosuppression in OCP).

Xanthelasma

  • Yellow plaques at the medial upper and lower lids (lipid-laden foam cells); half have dyslipidaemia (check lipids).
  • Management: excision with direct closure, laser (CO₂, erbium, argon), trichloroacetic acid peels, cryotherapy; recurrence common.

Molluscum contagiosum

  • Poxvirus; pearly umbilicated nodules on the lid margin; children and HIV patients (large multiple lesions); shedding of virus causes chronic follicular conjunctivitis and keratitis.
  • Management: curettage, excision, cryotherapy, cautery, expression; resolves spontaneously in immunocompetent; HIV test in extensive disease.
OL 49. Cosmetic oculoplasty — blepharoplasty, botulinum toxin and fillers; complications including filler-related blindness. NEW · outline

Cosmetic oculoplasty

  • Upper blepharoplasty (dermatochalasis: excess skin, sometimes fat; also functional when it obstructs the field), lower blepharoplasty (fat prolapse, tear trough: transconjunctival approach, fat repositioning), brow lift, botulinum toxin for crow’s feet and glabellar lines, hyaluronic acid fillers (tear trough, temples), laser resurfacing.

Complications

  • Blepharoplasty: dry eye and lagophthalmos (excess skin removal), lower lid retraction and ectropion, asymmetry, haematoma, retrobulbar haemorrhage with vision loss (orbital compartment syndrome: emergency canthotomy), diplopia (inferior oblique injury), scarring, infection.
  • Botulinum toxin: ptosis (spread to levator: apraclonidine drops help), brow ptosis, diplopia, dry eye, ectropion.
  • Fillers: lumps, Tyndall effect (bluish discolouration), oedema (malar), infection, granulomas.
  • Filler-related blindness: inadvertent intra-arterial injection with retrograde flow into the ophthalmic artery causes CRAO or ophthalmic artery occlusion (glabella, nose, forehead most risky); often with skin necrosis, ophthalmoplegia, stroke.

Prevention and management

  • Know vascular anatomy, aspirate, inject slowly with low pressure and small volumes, use cannulas in high-risk areas; keep hyaluronidase available.
  • Vision loss after filler: emergency; stop injection, high-dose hyaluronidase (retrobulbar and local, debated), lower IOP (ocular massage, paracentesis, acetazolamide), urgent ophthalmology and stroke assessment; prognosis poor.
  • Pre-operative assessment: dry eye, Bell phenomenon, lid laxity, realistic expectations, consent.

Revise from: Botulinum Toxin in Ophthalmology: Reconstitution, Units per Site for Blepharospasm, Strabismus Doses and Side Effects, Eyelid Malpositions: Entropion, Ectropion, Trichiasis and Chalazion

Ptosis

OL 12. Classification of blepharoptosis, its evaluation and relevance to management and complications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Congenital (simple, with SR weakness, blepharophimosis syndrome, Marcus Gunn jaw-winking, third nerve palsy) and acquired: aponeurotic (commonest acquired), myogenic (myasthenia, CPEO, myotonic dystrophy), neurogenic (third nerve, Horner), mechanical (tumours, scarring), traumatic; pseudoptosis (dermatochalasis, contralateral lid retraction, hypotropia, enophthalmos).

Evaluation

  • Margin reflex distances (MRD1 normal about 4–5 mm), vertical fissure height, levator function (excursion: good over 8 mm, fair 5–7 mm, poor under 4 mm), lid crease height, Bell phenomenon, corneal sensation, jaw-winking, fatiguability, phenylephrine test, pupils, ocular motility, visual field.

Relevance to management

  • Levator function guides the procedure: good → levator advancement or Müller muscle conjunctival resection; fair → levator resection; poor → frontalis sling.

Complications

  • Under- or over-correction, lid contour abnormalities, lagophthalmos and exposure keratopathy, lid crease asymmetry, entropion or ectropion, conjunctival prolapse, infection.

Revise from: Ptosis Examination: MRD1, Levator Function and Tests

OL 13. Outline the surgical and functional anatomy of the levator palpebrae superioris. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Levator palpebrae superioris

  • Origin: lesser wing of the sphenoid above the annulus of Zinn; runs forward above SR; at Whitnall ligament (superior transverse ligament) it becomes the aponeurosis (about 14–20 mm long).
  • Insertion: anterior surface of the tarsus and skin (lid crease), horns medially and laterally (lateral horn divides the lacrimal gland).
  • Nerve supply: superior division of the third nerve (from the single central caudal subnucleus); Müller muscle (sympathetic) beneath it.

Surgical relevance

  • Whitnall ligament as a suspension point (Whitnall sling), aponeurosis disinsertion causes involutional ptosis with high lid crease, preaponeurotic fat as a landmark, preserving lateral horn avoids lacrimal gland damage.

Revise from: Ptosis Examination: MRD1, Levator Function and Tests

OL 14. Discuss the modalities of management of ptosis and their indications; critically evaluate the operative procedures and the prevention and treatment of postoperative problems. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Modalities of management

  • Non-surgical: treat the cause (myasthenia), ptosis crutches, oxymetazoline 0.1% drops (acquired).
  • Surgical: Müller muscle conjunctival resection (Putterman: good levator function, positive phenylephrine test), Fasanella–Servat, levator advancement or plication (aponeurotic), levator resection (fair function), frontalis suspension (poor function: silicone, autogenous fascia lata, Mersilene).

Critical evaluation

  • Anterior approach allows contour control and lid crease formation; posterior approach is predictable for minimal ptosis; frontalis sling depends on brow use and lagophthalmos is common.

Prevention and treatment of postoperative problems

  • Under-correction: early massage or re-operation; over-correction: massage, early release; lagophthalmos: lubricants, night taping; contour problems: early revision; exposure keratopathy in poor Bell phenomenon (aim for under-correction); conjunctival prolapse: suture or excise.

Revise from: Ptosis Examination: MRD1, Levator Function and Tests

OL 15. Congenital ptosis — investigations, surgical management and your criteria for decision making; frontalis sling. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Investigations

  • Fixation and amblyopia (occlusion, astigmatism), refraction, levator function, Bell phenomenon, ocular motility (SR weakness), jaw-winking, family history (blepharophimosis), photographs.

Decision making

  • Early surgery for occlusion amblyopia or compensatory head posture; otherwise at 3–5 years (better measurements).
  • Levator function: good (over 8 mm) → levator resection (small); fair (5–7 mm) → larger levator resection (Beard nomogram); poor (under 4 mm) → frontalis sling.
  • Marcus Gunn: mild → levator resection; severe → levator excision with bilateral frontalis sling.

Frontalis sling

  • Connects the tarsus to the frontalis muscle; materials: autogenous fascia lata (best, over 3 years), silicone rods (adjustable, temporary), Mersilene mesh, banked fascia; Crawford, Fox pentagon techniques; complications: lagophthalmos, granuloma, infection, recurrence.

Revise from: Ptosis Examination: MRD1, Levator Function and Tests, Ptosis Case for MS and DNB Practicals: History, Lid Measurements, Presentation and Viva Questions

OL 16. Acquired (including myogenic and synkinetic) ptosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Acquired ptosis

  • Aponeurotic (commonest): age, contact lens wear, after eye surgery; high lid crease, good levator function; levator advancement or Müller muscle resection.
  • Myogenic: myasthenia gravis (variable, fatigable: medical treatment first), CPEO and Kearns–Sayre (bilateral, symmetric, poor Bell phenomenon: conservative surgery), oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy, myotonic dystrophy.
  • Neurogenic: third nerve palsy, Horner syndrome (mild ptosis, miosis), ophthalmoplegic migraine.
  • Mechanical: tumours, scarring, oedema; traumatic.

Synkinetic ptosis

  • Marcus Gunn jaw-winking (trigeminal–oculomotor synkinesis), aberrant regeneration of the third nerve, Duane syndrome; management depends on severity of synkinesis.

OL 17. Blepharophimosis syndrome; Marcus Gunn jaw-winking ptosis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Blepharophimosis-ptosis-epicanthus inversus syndrome (BPES)

  • Autosomal dominant (FOXL2); type I with premature ovarian failure, type II without.
  • Features: blepharophimosis (shortened horizontal fissure), ptosis (poor levator function), epicanthus inversus, telecanthus, lower lid ectropion, flat nasal bridge, amblyopia.
  • Management: staged surgery: medial canthoplasty (Mustardé, Y-V plasty) first, then frontalis sling (or combined in one stage), amblyopia treatment, endocrinology and fertility counselling for girls.

Marcus Gunn jaw-winking ptosis

  • Congenital synkinesis between the trigeminal nerve (pterygoid muscles) and the levator: eyelid elevates with jaw movement (chewing, sucking, moving jaw to the opposite side); unilateral ptosis.
  • Associated: strabismus (double elevator palsy, superior rectus weakness), amblyopia.
  • Management: observation if mild (wink may lessen with age); mild ptosis with minimal wink: levator resection; marked wink: levator excision with bilateral frontalis sling.
OL 18. Outline your approach to the evaluation and management of failed ptosis surgery and ptosis in special (complicated) situations. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Evaluation of failed ptosis surgery

  • Previous records, levator function now, lid crease, contour, lagophthalmos, cornea, Bell phenomenon, wrong diagnosis (myasthenia, CPEO, Horner), wrong procedure for levator function, slippage of suture or sling.

Management

  • Under-correction: re-advancement or sling; over-correction: recession or release, levator lengthening (spacer grafts); contour defects: revision; recurrent sling ptosis: sling replacement.

Ptosis in special situations

  • Poor Bell phenomenon or dry eye: conservative lift.
  • Myasthenia and CPEO: slings with adjustment (silicone), conservative lift.
  • Third nerve palsy: correct squint first; sling.
  • Jaw-winking, blepharophimosis (staged: telecanthus correction, then sling).
  • Anophthalmic socket: prosthesis or implant volume adjustment first.
  • After trauma: wait for 6 months.

Lacrimal System

OL 19. Describe tear formation and drainage, and the lacrimal pump mechanism (structural and functional aspects). How would you evaluate a watering eye and manage different types of lacrimal obstruction? HIGH-YIELD · outline

Tear formation

  • Basal secretion (accessory lacrimal glands of Krause and Wolfring, goblet cells, meibomian glands) and reflex secretion (main lacrimal gland; parasympathetic from the superior salivatory nucleus via the greater petrosal nerve and pterygopalatine ganglion).

Drainage and lacrimal pump

  • Puncta → canaliculi (vertical 2 mm, horizontal 8 mm) → common canaliculus → sac → nasolacrimal duct (valve of Hasner) into the inferior meatus.
  • Pump: blinking compresses the pretarsal orbicularis around the canaliculi (Horner muscle); sac dilates on closure, collapses on opening (Jones theory); gravity, capillary attraction.

Evaluation of a watering eye

  • Hypersecretion vs epiphora; lid position and laxity, puncta, tear meniscus, fluorescein dye disappearance test, regurgitation on pressure over the sac, syringing and probing, Jones dye tests I and II, dacryocystography, dacryoscintigraphy, CT, nasal endoscopy.

Management of obstruction

  • Punctal stenosis: punctoplasty (snip).
  • Canalicular: probing with intubation, canaliculo-DCR, Jones tube (conjunctivo-DCR).
  • Nasolacrimal duct: DCR (external or endoscopic); functional obstruction: lower lid tightening, DCR with intubation.

Revise from: Watering Eye in Adults: Approach to Epiphora, Syringing, Dye Tests and Treatment, Congenital NLD Obstruction: When to Wait, When to Act

OL 20. Acute dacryocystitis; classification of dacryocystitis; mucocele. DNB / UNIV · outline

Acute dacryocystitis

  • Acute infection of the lacrimal sac secondary to nasolacrimal duct obstruction; painful, red, tender swelling below the medial canthal tendon; may spread to preseptal or orbital cellulitis; can form a lacrimal abscess and fistula.
  • Organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Pseudomonas, Haemophilus (children).
  • Management: systemic antibiotics (oral amoxicillin-clavulanate; IV if severe), warm compresses, analgesics, incision and drainage of a pointing abscess; avoid probing during acute stage; DCR after resolution (early endoscopic DCR in selected cases).

Classification of dacryocystitis

  • Congenital (neonatal) vs acquired; acute, chronic (catarrhal, lacrimal mucocele, chronic suppurative), acute on chronic; specific causes (TB, syphilis, fungal, rhinosporidiosis).

Mucocele

  • Distended sac filled with mucoid fluid due to obstruction of both the NLD and the common canalicular valve (Rosenmüller); non-tender swelling; regurgitation may not occur; treatment: DCR.
OL 21. Chronic dacryocystitis — aetiopathogenesis and management; DCR (indications, steps, recent modifications). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiopathogenesis

  • Nasolacrimal duct obstruction (primary acquired: inflammation and fibrosis, more common in women) → stasis and infection (Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Pneumococcus); chronic mucocele, pyocoele, fistula, acute attacks.

Management

  • Acute: systemic antibiotics, warm compresses, incision and drainage of abscess; DCR later.
  • Chronic: DCR (definitive); dacryocystectomy in elderly, frail patients, TB or tumours; treat before intraocular surgery (endophthalmitis risk).

DCR: indications and steps

  • Indications: primary acquired NLD obstruction, chronic dacryocystitis, mucocele, recurrent acute dacryocystitis, failed probing in older children.
  • External DCR steps: skin incision medial to the angular vein, expose anterior lacrimal crest, raise periosteum, create a bony ostium (about 15 mm), make anterior and posterior flaps of sac and nasal mucosa, suture anterior flaps, close in layers.

Recent modifications

  • Endoscopic DCR (no skin scar), powered drills and lasers, mitomycin C, silicone intubation, balloon dacryoplasty, anterior flap only suturing, Ultrasonic bone removal.

Revise from: Congenital NLD Obstruction: When to Wait, When to Act

OL 22. Management of failed DCR — causes and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of failed DCR

  • Small or high ostium, granulation tissue and scarring (synechiae with the middle turbinate or septum), common canalicular obstruction, sump syndrome (low ostium), soft tissue closure, untreated nasal disease, wrong diagnosis (functional epiphora, lid laxity).

Evaluation

  • Syringing, probing, nasal endoscopy (look at the ostium), dacryocystography, CT.

Management

  • Endoscopic revision with enlargement of ostium, removal of scar tissue and adhesions, mitomycin C, silicone intubation; treat canalicular blockage (trephination, Lester Jones tube); manage nasal disease (septoplasty).

Revise from: Congenital NLD Obstruction: When to Wait, When to Act

OL 23. Outline the differential diagnosis and management of a watering eye in children; congenital NLD obstruction. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Differential diagnosis of watering in a child

  • Congenital NLD obstruction (commonest), congenital glaucoma (photophobia, large cornea: urgent), conjunctivitis, keratitis, foreign body, trichiasis or epiblepharon, punctal agenesis, dacryocele.

Congenital NLD obstruction

  • Membrane at the valve of Hasner; about 6% of newborns; epiphora and discharge from weeks of age; most resolve spontaneously by 1 year (about 90%).

Management

  • Crigler massage and lid hygiene until 9–12 months; probing (high success up to 1–2 years, PEDIG); repeat probing with intubation or balloon dacryoplasty after failure; DCR (endoscopic) in older children or complex cases.
  • Congenital dacryocele: risk of infection and respiratory obstruction; massage, early probing, endoscopic marsupialisation.

OL 24. Evaluate the management of functional epiphora; Jones dye tests. · outline

Functional epiphora

  • Watering with a patent lacrimal system on syringing but delayed drainage.

Causes

  • Lacrimal pump failure (lower lid laxity, facial palsy, ectropion), punctal malposition, partial NLD stenosis, canalicular stenosis (partial), conjunctivochalasis, hypersecretion (reflex: dry eye, blepharitis).

Evaluation

  • Fluorescein dye disappearance test, Jones dye tests, dacryoscintigraphy (delayed transit), DCG, nasal endoscopy.

Jones dye tests

  • Jones I (primary): fluorescein in the conjunctival sac; recovery from the inferior meatus with a cotton bud after 5 minutes: positive (dye recovered) = normal patency.
  • Jones II (secondary): if Jones I negative, irrigate the sac after washing out fluorescein from the eye; dye recovered from the nose = partial NLD obstruction (dye entered the sac); clear fluid = upper system (punctal or canalicular) failure or pump failure; no fluid = complete obstruction.

Management

  • Treat dry eye and blepharitis, lid tightening (lateral tarsal strip), punctoplasty, conjunctivochalasis excision, DCR with intubation for partial NLD obstruction (good success in selected patients).

OL 25. Lacrimal gland lesions and tumours (pleomorphic adenoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma) — clinical picture, histopathology, differential diagnosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification of lacrimal gland lesions

  • Inflammatory (dacryoadenitis: viral, bacterial, IgG4-related disease, sarcoid, Sjögren), lymphoproliferative (reactive lymphoid hyperplasia, lymphoma), epithelial tumours (benign and malignant), cysts (dacryops), dermoids.

Pleomorphic adenoma (benign mixed tumour)

  • Middle-aged adults; painless slowly progressive mass in the superotemporal orbit (over 12 months); globe displaced down and medially; CT: well-defined round mass with fossa expansion (scalloping).
  • Histology: epithelial and mesenchymal elements with a pseudocapsule.
  • Management: complete excision with capsule intact (lateral orbitotomy); incision biopsy contraindicated (recurrence, malignant change).

Adenoid cystic carcinoma

  • Commonest malignant epithelial tumour; pain (perineural invasion), short history, bone erosion and calcification; Swiss cheese histology (cribriform pattern).
  • Management: wide excision or exenteration with radiotherapy; intra-arterial chemotherapy studied; poor prognosis.

Differential diagnosis

  • Dacryoadenitis (acute painful, S-shaped lid), lymphoma (moulding, older patients), dermoid, metastases.

OL 26. Endoscopic DCR, lacrimal intubation and balloon dacryoplasty; types of DCR and their complications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Endoscopic DCR

  • Ostium made via the nose: mucosal flap, bone removal from the frontal process of maxilla and lacrimal bone with punch or drill, marsupialisation of the sac, mucosal apposition.
  • Advantages: no skin scar, preservation of the pump function, treats nasal disease, quick recovery; disadvantages: learning curve, equipment.

Lacrimal intubation

  • Silicone stents (bicanalicular or monocanalicular) left for 6 weeks to 6 months; uses: canalicular stenosis, congenital NLD obstruction after failed probing, DCR (selected).

Balloon dacryoplasty

  • Balloon catheter dilates the NLD; congenital NLDO after failed probing, partial adult obstruction.

Types of DCR

  • External, endoscopic, endocanalicular laser DCR, conjunctivo-DCR (Jones tube), canaliculo-DCR.

Complications

  • Bleeding, CSF leak (rare), infection, scarring of ostium, synechiae, tube extrusion and cheese-wiring of the puncta, skin scar (external), wound gaping.

OL 27. Canalicular obstruction — surgical management and Lester Jones tube; dacryocele and dacryolith. DNB / UNIV · outline

Canalicular obstruction

  • Causes: idiopathic, chronic infection (canaliculitis: Actinomyces), herpetic, drugs (5-FU, docetaxel, topical antiglaucoma), trauma, radiotherapy, punctal plugs.

Surgical management

  • Partial: probing, silicone intubation, balloon canaliculoplasty, trephination with stent.
  • Common canalicular obstruction: DCR with canalicular trephination and intubation.
  • Complete obstruction of both canaliculi: conjunctivodacryocystorhinostomy (CDCR) with Lester Jones tube (Pyrex glass tube from the medial canthus to the middle meatus); complications: tube extrusion, malposition, blockage, granuloma; frosted tubes and StopLoss tubes reduce extrusion.
  • Canaliculitis: curettage of concretions (sulphur granules), canaliculotomy, topical and oral antibiotics.

Dacryocele (dacryocystocele)

  • Congenital distension of the lacrimal sac with mucus or amniotic fluid (obstruction at both ends); bluish swelling below the medial canthal tendon at birth; risk of dacryocystitis and intranasal cyst (respiratory distress).
  • Management: massage, early probing, endoscopic marsupialisation of the intranasal cyst.

Dacryolith

  • Stone in the sac (mucus, cells, calcium); intermittent obstruction; DCR with removal.

Orbit: Anatomy

OL 28. Describe, with diagrams, the functional and surgical anatomy of the bony orbit (walls and weak spots), its spaces and their clinical significance, along with development and important disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Bony orbit

  • Pyramid with volume about 30 mL; seven bones.
  • Roof: frontal and lesser wing of sphenoid; lacrimal fossa, trochlear fovea.
  • Lateral wall: zygomatic and greater wing of sphenoid; strongest; Whitnall tubercle.
  • Floor: maxilla, zygoma and palatine; infraorbital groove; weakest posteromedially (blow-out fractures).
  • Medial wall: maxilla, lacrimal, ethmoid (lamina papyracea: thinnest; spread of ethmoid sinusitis), sphenoid.

Spaces and their clinical significance

  • Subperiosteal space (abscess), peripheral (extraconal) space (lacrimal gland tumours, dermoids), central (intraconal) space (cavernous haemangioma, optic nerve tumours), sub-Tenon space (injections).

Openings

  • Optic canal (optic nerve, ophthalmic artery), superior and inferior orbital fissures, ethmoidal foramina, nasolacrimal canal.

Development

  • From the mesenchyme surrounding the optic vesicle (neural crest); grows until puberty (enucleation in childhood causes orbital hypoplasia).

Important disorders

  • Thyroid eye disease, orbital cellulitis, fractures, tumours, craniofacial anomalies.

OL 29. Anatomy of the superior orbital fissure (with labelled diagram); orbital apex syndrome. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Superior orbital fissure

  • Between the greater and lesser wings of the sphenoid; divided by the annulus of Zinn.
  • Above the annulus (lateral): lacrimal, frontal and trochlear nerves, superior ophthalmic vein.
  • Within the annulus (oculomotor foramen): superior and inferior divisions of the third nerve, nasociliary nerve, sixth nerve, sympathetic roots.
  • Below: inferior ophthalmic vein.

Superior orbital fissure syndrome

  • Ophthalmoplegia (3, 4, 6), V1 sensory loss, proptosis, dilated pupil; optic nerve spared.

Orbital apex syndrome

  • SOF syndrome plus optic nerve involvement (vision loss, RAPD).
  • Causes: fungal infection (mucormycosis, aspergillosis), tumours, inflammation (Tolosa–Hunt, IgG4), trauma, metastases.
  • Management: imaging (MRI, CT), biopsy, treat cause (antifungals and debridement, steroids, radiotherapy).

OL 30. Applied anatomy of the orbital apex. · outline

Orbital apex

  • Posterior end of the orbit where the optic canal, superior orbital fissure and inferior orbital fissure converge; site of the annulus of Zinn.

Contents

  • Optic nerve and ophthalmic artery (optic canal); third, fourth, sixth nerves, V1 branches (lacrimal, frontal, nasociliary), superior ophthalmic vein, sympathetic fibres (superior orbital fissure); V2 branches (infraorbital, zygomatic) and inferior ophthalmic vein connections (inferior orbital fissure).

Relations

  • Sphenoid and posterior ethmoid sinuses medially (spread of infection, mucocele), cavernous sinus behind, middle cranial fossa, pituitary.

Applied anatomy

  • Orbital apex syndrome (optic neuropathy with ophthalmoplegia and V1 sensory loss): mucormycosis, aspergillosis, tumours, IOI, Tolosa–Hunt, trauma, thyroid eye disease compressive optic neuropathy.
  • Surgical approaches: endoscopic medial apex decompression, transcranial, lateral orbitotomy; risk of optic nerve injury.
  • Retrobulbar injections and compression at the apex in proptosis.

OL 31. Describe the aetiology, clinical features and management of unilateral proptosis; your approach to unilateral axial proptosis in an adult. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiology of unilateral proptosis

  • Thyroid eye disease (commonest in adults, also bilateral), orbital tumours (cavernous haemangioma, meningioma, lymphoma, lacrimal tumours, metastases), inflammation (orbital cellulitis, idiopathic orbital inflammation), vascular (varices, CCF), trauma (haematoma), sinus disease (mucocele).

Clinical features

  • Axial vs non-axial displacement, reducibility, pulsation, change with Valsalva, pain, vision, optic nerve function, motility, palpable mass.

Approach to unilateral axial proptosis in an adult

  • Intraconal lesions: cavernous haemangioma (commonest benign), optic nerve sheath meningioma, glioma, schwannoma, lymphoma, metastases, TED (muscle enlargement).
  • Investigations: thyroid function and antibodies, exophthalmometry, CT and MRI, B-scan, biopsy if needed.

Management

  • Treat TED, excise benign tumours (lateral or transconjunctival orbitotomy), biopsy and radiotherapy or chemotherapy for lymphoma, radiotherapy for meningioma.

OL 32. How would you manage a case of proptosis in a child? Differential diagnosis of proptosis and acute proptosis in a 3-year-old. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes of proptosis in a child

  • Orbital cellulitis (commonest acute), dermoid cyst, capillary haemangioma (infantile), lymphangioma (sudden enlargement with bleed), rhabdomyosarcoma (commonest primary malignancy), optic nerve glioma (NF1), neuroblastoma metastases (periorbital ecchymosis, raccoon eyes), leukaemia (chloroma), Langerhans cell histiocytosis, encephalocele.

Acute proptosis in a 3-year-old

  • Orbital cellulitis, rhabdomyosarcoma (rapid, upper orbit), haemorrhage into lymphangioma, neuroblastoma, leukaemia, trauma.

Management

  • Urgent assessment: vision, pupils, motility, systemic examination; CT or MRI; blood counts, urine catecholamines (neuroblastoma); biopsy for suspected malignancy (rhabdomyosarcoma: IRS protocol with chemotherapy and radiotherapy); IV antibiotics for cellulitis; propranolol for infantile haemangioma; sclerotherapy (bleomycin, doxycycline) for lymphangioma.

OL 33. Causes of bilateral proptosis; enophthalmos — causes and investigations. DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes of bilateral proptosis

  • Thyroid eye disease (commonest), orbital inflammation (bilateral IOI, GPA, IgG4-related disease, sarcoid), lymphoma, leukaemia, metastatic neuroblastoma (children), cavernous sinus thrombosis, bilateral CCF, craniofacial syndromes (Crouzon: shallow orbits), Cushing syndrome, obesity, high myopia (pseudoproptosis), bilateral orbital cellulitis.

Enophthalmos

  • Posterior displacement of the globe.
  • Causes: orbital fractures (blow-out: commonest), atrophy of orbital fat (ageing, HIV lipodystrophy, scleroderma, prostaglandin analogues: periorbitopathy), silent sinus syndrome (maxillary sinus collapse), scirrhous breast cancer metastases, Parry–Romberg syndrome, microphthalmos (pseudo-enophthalmos), contralateral proptosis (relative), Horner (apparent).

Investigations

  • Exophthalmometry (difference over 2 mm significant), CT orbit and sinuses (fracture, silent sinus, tumours), MRI, thyroid function, biopsy where needed.
OL 34. Differential diagnosis and management of unilateral orbital cellulitis, with special reference to children. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Differential diagnosis

  • Preseptal cellulitis (no proptosis, normal motility and vision), orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal or orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, idiopathic orbital inflammation, rhabdomyosarcoma, ruptured dermoid, TED (acute), mucormycosis (diabetes, COVID-19 era).

Chandler classification

  • Preseptal cellulitis, orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis.

Children

  • Commonly from ethmoid sinusitis; subperiosteal abscess common; most respond to IV antibiotics; younger children (under 9 years) with small medial abscess often avoid surgery.

Management

  • Admission, IV antibiotics (third-generation cephalosporin with anti-staphylococcal cover, metronidazole for anaerobes), nasal decongestants, CT orbits and sinuses, ENT referral; surgical drainage for large abscess, vision loss or poor response in 48 hours; monitor vision, pupils and colour; watch for intracranial spread.

OL 35. Discuss the differential diagnosis of pulsatile proptosis and its investigation and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pulsatile proptosis

  • Vascular: carotid-cavernous fistula (with bruit), arteriovenous malformations, aneurysms of the ophthalmic artery.
  • Bony defects: orbital roof defects (neurofibromatosis type 1: sphenoid wing dysplasia), meningoencephalocele, after surgery or trauma (brain pulsations transmitted, no bruit).

Investigations

  • Auscultation for bruit, CT (bone defects, dilated superior ophthalmic vein), MRI and MRA, Doppler, digital subtraction angiography.

Management

  • CCF: endovascular occlusion; bony defects: neurosurgical repair; AVM: embolisation or surgery.
OL 36. Exophthalmometry. · outline

Exophthalmometry

  • Measurement of the anteroposterior position of the corneal apex from the lateral orbital rim.

Instruments

  • Hertel exophthalmometer (commonest): mirrors with scales; record base reading (intercanthal distance between lateral rims) for repeat measurements; normal values about 12–20 mm (varies by race: higher in Black populations); difference between eyes over 2 mm is significant.
  • Luedde (single-eye ruler), Naugle (uses superior and inferior rims: useful when lateral rim is fractured), CT measurement (most accurate), photography.

Uses

  • Diagnose and monitor proptosis (thyroid eye disease activity, tumours), enophthalmos after fractures, orbital decompression results.

Limitations

  • Lateral rim abnormalities, inter-observer variability, need same base reading.
OL 37. Pseudotumour of the orbit (idiopathic orbital inflammation) — describe and discuss management; Tolosa-Hunt syndrome; painful orbital swelling. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Idiopathic orbital inflammation (orbital pseudotumour)

  • Non-specific inflammation of orbital tissues without a local or systemic cause; diagnosis of exclusion; subtypes: anterior, diffuse, myositis, dacryoadenitis, apical.

Features

  • Acute onset of pain, proptosis, lid swelling, red eye, restricted motility, diplopia; unilateral in adults; bilateral in children (with systemic features).
  • CT or MRI: diffuse infiltration, enlarged muscle including tendon (vs TED: tendon spared).

Management

  • Rule out infections, lymphoma, IgG4-related disease, sarcoid, GPA (biopsy for atypical or recurrent cases).
  • High-dose oral steroids (rapid response), slow taper; radiotherapy, methotrexate or rituximab for recurrent or resistant cases.

Tolosa–Hunt syndrome

  • Granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus or SOF; painful ophthalmoplegia; responds dramatically to steroids; diagnosis of exclusion (MRI, rule out tumour, aneurysm).

Painful orbital swelling: differential diagnosis

  • Orbital cellulitis, IOI, TED (acute), dacryoadenitis, ruptured dermoid, haemorrhage, CCF, rhabdomyosarcoma, metastases.

OL 38. Mucormycosis — rhino-orbital-cerebral. · outline

Rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM)

  • Angioinvasive fungal infection by Mucorales (Rhizopus, Mucor); uncontrolled diabetes (especially ketoacidosis), steroids, haematological malignancy, transplant, iron overload; surge in India during COVID-19 (2021).

Clinical features

  • Facial pain and swelling, nasal blockage, black eschar on the nasal mucosa or palate, periorbital oedema, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, loss of vision (CRAO, orbital apex syndrome), cavernous sinus thrombosis, cerebral involvement.

Diagnosis

  • Urgent nasal endoscopy with biopsy (KOH: broad aseptate ribbon-like hyphae with right-angle branching), culture, MRI with contrast (black turbinate sign, orbital and intracranial spread), CT sinuses.

Management

  • Control diabetes and acidosis, reduce immunosuppression, IV liposomal amphotericin B (5–10 mg/kg/day), followed by posaconazole or isavuconazole.
  • Aggressive surgical debridement (endoscopic sinus surgery), retrobulbar liposomal amphotericin B (TRAMB) for limited orbital disease, orbital exenteration for extensive orbital involvement with no vision.
  • High mortality (30–50%; worse with cerebral involvement).
OL 39. Classification of orbital tumours; common central-space tumours (cavernous haemangioma, dermoid); metastatic orbital tumours; lymphoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification of orbital tumours

  • Vascular (cavernous haemangioma, infantile haemangioma, lymphangioma, varix), neural (glioma, meningioma, schwannoma, neurofibroma), mesenchymal (rhabdomyosarcoma, fibrous histiocytoma, osteoma), lymphoproliferative (lymphoma), lacrimal gland, cystic (dermoid, mucocele), secondary (from sinuses, eyelid, globe), metastatic.

Cavernous haemangioma (cavernous venous malformation)

  • Commonest benign orbital tumour in adults; middle-aged women; intraconal, axial proptosis; well-defined on CT and MRI; progressive enhancement; surgical removal if symptomatic.

Dermoid cyst

  • Choristoma; superotemporal near the zygomaticofrontal suture; smooth, painless; excise intact (rupture causes inflammation); deep dermoids may extend through bone.

Metastatic orbital tumours

  • Adults: breast (enophthalmos in scirrhous type), lung, prostate; children: neuroblastoma; treat with radiotherapy and systemic therapy.

Lymphoma

  • Mostly B-cell non-Hodgkin (extranodal marginal zone: MALT); older adults; painless salmon-patch or moulding mass; biopsy; radiotherapy (low-grade), chemotherapy, rituximab; systemic staging.

OL 50. IgG4-related ophthalmic disease. NEW · outline

IgG4-related ophthalmic disease (IgG4-ROD)

  • Part of IgG4-related disease: fibroinflammatory condition with IgG4-positive plasma cells; middle-aged and older adults.

Ocular adnexal involvement

  • Lacrimal gland enlargement (bilateral painless dacryoadenitis: commonest; previously Mikulicz disease), extraocular muscle enlargement (lateral rectus often), infraorbital and supraorbital nerve enlargement (characteristic), orbital masses, eyelid swelling, scleritis; associated sialadenitis, autoimmune pancreatitis, retroperitoneal fibrosis, lymphadenopathy.

Diagnosis

  • Biopsy: dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, IgG4-positive plasma cells (over 40% of IgG-positive cells; over 50 per high-power field in the orbit), storiform fibrosis, obliterative phlebitis; serum IgG4 raised in many (not essential); imaging (MRI, CT); exclude lymphoma (risk of MALT lymphoma), sarcoid, GPA.

Management

  • Oral prednisolone (good initial response), steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate), rituximab for relapsing or refractory disease; long-term follow-up for relapse and lymphoma.
OL 51. Orbital lymphatic and venous malformations (lymphangioma, varix) — diagnosis and sclerotherapy. NEW · outline

Orbital lymphatic malformation (lymphangioma)

  • Congenital low-flow malformation of lymphatic channels; children; slowly progressive proptosis, may enlarge with upper respiratory infection; sudden painful proptosis from intralesional haemorrhage (chocolate cysts); conjunctival or lid lesions.
  • Imaging: MRI (multicystic lesion with fluid–fluid levels), ultrasound; not encapsulated, infiltrates tissues.

Orbital venous malformation (varix)

  • Distensible venous malformation; intermittent proptosis increasing with Valsalva, bending forward or crying; enophthalmos at rest (fat atrophy); risk of thrombosis and haemorrhage.
  • Imaging: CT or MRI with Valsalva (enlarges), phleboliths (calcification).

Management

  • Observation for mild cases; treat amblyopia.
  • Sclerotherapy (image-guided intralesional injection): bleomycin, doxycycline, sodium tetradecyl sulphate or OK-432 for macrocystic lymphatic malformations; sirolimus (oral) for extensive lymphatic malformations.
  • Surgery: drainage of haemorrhagic cysts in acute vision-threatening cases (with orbital compartment risk); partial excision (difficult, recurrence); for varix, embolisation with glue or coils followed by excision.
  • Avoid surgery when possible (risk of vision loss).

Orbital Surgery

OL 40. Surgical approaches to the orbit — lateral orbitotomy (indications, steps, complications) and transfrontal orbitotomy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Lateral orbitotomy (Krönlein, modified Stallard–Wright)

  • Indications: lacrimal gland tumours, intraconal tumours lateral or posterior to the globe, cavernous haemangioma, optic nerve sheath biopsy.
  • Steps: lateral canthal or lid crease incision, expose and remove the lateral orbital rim (osteotomy), open periorbita, dissect between LR and other structures, remove tumour, replace rim with plates or wires, close.
  • Complications: haemorrhage, vision loss, diplopia (LR injury), ptosis, lacrimal damage, pupillary dilatation (ciliary ganglion), CSF leak.

Transfrontal (transcranial) orbitotomy

  • Neurosurgical approach through the roof: tumours of the orbital apex, optic canal, intracranial extension (optic nerve glioma, meningioma).

Others

  • Anterior (transcutaneous, transconjunctival), medial (transcaruncular), endoscopic endonasal (medial apex lesions).
OL 41. Orbital implants; enucleation, evisceration and exenteration — indications and techniques. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Enucleation

  • Removal of the whole globe; indications: intraocular tumours (retinoblastoma, melanoma), blind painful eye, severe trauma (risk of sympathetic ophthalmia), phthisis.
  • Technique: 360° peritomy, isolate and tag recti, cut optic nerve, haemostasis, orbital implant, attach muscles, close Tenon and conjunctiva.

Evisceration

  • Removal of the contents leaving the sclera and extraocular muscles; indications: endophthalmitis (panophthalmitis), blind painful eye (when malignancy excluded); better motility.

Exenteration

  • Removal of the entire orbital contents with or without eyelids; indications: malignant orbital tumours, mucormycosis, extensive eyelid or conjunctival malignancies.

Orbital implants

  • Non-integrated (silicone, PMMA spheres), integrated or porous (hydroxyapatite, porous polyethylene), dermis-fat grafts (children, socket contraction); complications: exposure, extrusion, infection, migration.

OL 42. Contracted socket. DNB / UNIV · outline

Contracted socket

  • Shrinkage of the fornices and conjunctival lining of the anophthalmic socket so that a prosthesis cannot be retained.

Causes

  • Chemical and thermal burns, radiotherapy, repeated surgeries, chronic infection or inflammation (ill-fitting prosthesis), cicatrising conjunctival diseases, implant exposure or extrusion, childhood enucleation without implant.

Grading

  • Grade 0 (healthy socket) to grade 5 (recurrent contraction after repeated reconstruction), based on fornix depth (Krishna classification).

Management

  • Mild: fornix-deepening sutures, larger conformer.
  • Moderate: mucous membrane grafts (buccal mucosa), amniotic membrane, hard palate grafts.
  • Severe: dermis-fat grafts, temporalis muscle or radial forearm flaps, orbital implants; exenteration with a spectacle-mounted or osseointegrated prosthesis in extreme cases.
OL 43. Artificial eye. · outline

Artificial eye (ocular prosthesis)

  • Ready-made (stock) or custom-made (impression technique) shell of PMMA (acrylic) or glass; painted iris matched to the fellow eye.

Types

  • Shell prosthesis (over a phthisical eye or eviscerated socket), full prosthesis (anophthalmic socket), scleral shells, cosmetic contact lenses for disfigured eyes with vision.

Fitting

  • 6–8 weeks after enucleation or evisceration with a conformer in the meantime; motility improved by integrated (porous) implants with pegging.

Care

  • Remove and clean regularly with mild soap, polish every 6–12 months, replace every 3–5 years (children more often as the orbit grows); lubricants for socket dryness; treat giant papillary conjunctivitis and discharge.

Problems

  • Discharge, socket infection, contracted socket, ptosis and lower lid laxity, superior sulcus deformity (post-enucleation socket syndrome), poor motility, implant exposure.

Thyroid Eye Disease

OL 44. Describe the progressive clinical features of dysthyroid ophthalmopathy, grading, diagnostic modalities, indications for intervention and surgical management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Progressive features of thyroid eye disease

  • Active phase (6–18 months): lid retraction, soft tissue swelling, conjunctival injection and chemosis, proptosis, restricted motility; then inactive (fibrotic) phase.
  • Rundle curve: active inflammation then plateau.
  • Signs: Dalrymple (lid retraction), von Graefe (lid lag on downgaze), Kocher, Stellwag.
  • Sight-threatening: compressive optic neuropathy, exposure keratopathy, globe subluxation.

Grading

  • Activity: clinical activity score (CAS) 7 points (3 or more = active).
  • Severity: EUGOGO (mild, moderate-to-severe, sight-threatening), NOSPECS (historical), VISA.

Diagnostic modalities

  • Thyroid function (TSH, T3, T4), TSH receptor antibodies, CT (muscle belly enlargement with tendon sparing, IR > MR > SR), MRI (activity), fields and colour vision.

Indications for intervention

  • Active moderate-to-severe: IV methylprednisolone (EUGOGO weekly protocol, 4.5 g cumulative), mycophenolate, teprotumumab (IGF-1R antibody), tocilizumab, orbital radiotherapy.
  • Sight-threatening (DON): IV steroids, urgent orbital decompression.
  • Mild: selenium, lubricants, smoking cessation.

Surgical management (in order, inactive phase)

  • Orbital decompression (medial and lateral walls, fat), strabismus surgery (IR recession with adjustable sutures), lid surgery (Müller muscle and levator recession, lower lid retractor recession, spacer grafts), then blepharoplasty.

OL 45. Describe the thyroid function tests and the clinical picture and management of hyperthyroidism with special regard to ocular involvement; lid retraction; orbital decompression. · outline

Thyroid function tests

  • TSH (first-line; low in hyperthyroidism), free T4 and free T3, TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb or TSI: Graves disease, correlate with TED activity), anti-TPO antibodies, radioactive iodine uptake scan.

Hyperthyroidism (Graves disease)

  • Systemic: weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations, AF, goitre, pretibial myxoedema, acropachy.
  • Ocular (thyroid eye disease): lid retraction (Dalrymple sign), lid lag (von Graefe), proptosis, conjunctival injection and chemosis, restrictive myopathy, exposure keratopathy, compressive optic neuropathy.

Lid retraction

  • Causes: Müller muscle overaction (sympathetic), levator fibrosis and adhesion, compensatory for IR restriction.
  • Management: treat thyroid status, lubricants, botulinum or hyaluronic acid injections, levator and Müller muscle recession (graded), lower lid retractor recession with spacer grafts.

Management of hyperthyroidism and the eye

  • Antithyroid drugs (carbimazole, methimazole), radioactive iodine (may worsen TED: steroid prophylaxis in active disease and smokers), thyroidectomy; keep euthyroid, stop smoking, selenium in mild TED.

Orbital decompression

  • Indications: compressive optic neuropathy (urgent if not responding to steroids), severe proptosis with exposure, cosmetic (inactive phase).
  • Techniques: fat decompression, bony (medial wall and floor: endoscopic or external; lateral wall: deep lateral); complications: diplopia (new-onset), infraorbital numbness, CSF leak, sinusitis, vision loss.

OL 46. Clinical Activity Score, EUGOGO guidelines and teprotumumab. · outline

Clinical Activity Score (CAS)

  • Seven items at first visit (one point each): spontaneous retrobulbar pain, pain on eye movement, eyelid redness, conjunctival redness, eyelid swelling, chemosis, caruncle swelling; at follow-up three more (increase in proptosis 2 mm, decrease in motility 8°, decrease in vision); CAS 3 or more of 7 = active disease.

EUGOGO guidelines (2021)

  • Severity: mild (minor impact), moderate-to-severe (lid retraction 2 mm or more, moderate or severe soft tissue involvement, proptosis 3 mm or more above normal, diplopia), sight-threatening (DON, corneal breakdown).
  • Mild: local measures, selenium for 6 months, smoking cessation.
  • Moderate-to-severe active: IV methylprednisolone 4.5 g over 12 weeks with mycophenolate first line; second line: higher-dose steroids, orbital radiotherapy with steroids, teprotumumab, tocilizumab, rituximab, cyclosporine.
  • Sight-threatening: IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g for 3 days (repeat once), then urgent decompression if no response within 2 weeks.
  • Rehabilitative surgery in inactive disease: decompression → strabismus → lid surgery.

Teprotumumab

  • Monoclonal antibody against IGF-1 receptor; 8 IV infusions every 3 weeks; reduces proptosis (about 3 mm) and diplopia in active TED (OPTIC, OPTIC-X) and in chronic TED; side effects: hearing loss (can be permanent), hyperglycaemia, muscle spasms, IBD flare; contraindicated in pregnancy; costly; relapses in some.

Orbital Trauma

OL 47. How would you approach and manage a suspected blow-out fracture (pure vs impure)? Orbital fractures in relation to structural anatomy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Blow-out fracture

  • Pure: orbital floor or medial wall fracture with an intact rim (hydraulic and buckling mechanisms); impure: involves the rim (zygoma, maxilla).

Clinical features

  • Periorbital bruising, diplopia on upgaze (entrapment of the IR), enophthalmos, infraorbital anaesthesia, surgical emphysema, oculocardiac reflex (bradycardia, nausea in white-eyed blowout in children: urgent surgery).

Investigations

  • CT (coronal) for floor fractures (teardrop sign), forced duction test, Hess chart.

Management

  • Conservative: antibiotics, decongestants, no nose blowing, steroids for oedema; review at 1–2 weeks.
  • Surgery within 2 weeks if: muscle entrapment with persistent diplopia, enophthalmos over 2 mm, fracture over 50% of the floor; immediate for trapdoor fractures in children with oculocardiac reflex.
  • Repair with implants (porous polyethylene, titanium mesh) via transconjunctival or subciliary approach.

Relation to structural anatomy

  • Floor is weakest medial to the infraorbital groove; medial wall (lamina papyracea) is thinnest but supported by ethmoid air cells; lateral wall and roof fractures from high-energy trauma.

Revise from: Orbital Fracture: Emergency vs Observation

OL 48. Critically analyse recent advances in orbital and canalicular interventions, with special reference to trauma. · outline

Recent advances in orbital interventions

  • Endoscopic orbital surgery (medial apex tumours, decompression, optic canal decompression), image-guided navigation, 3D-printed patient-specific implants for fracture repair, intraoperative CT, mirror-image virtual planning, resorbable implants in children, transcaruncular and transconjunctival approaches (scarless).
  • Medical: teprotumumab (TED), rituximab (IOI, IgG4), sirolimus (vascular malformations), propranolol (infantile haemangioma), sclerotherapy with bleomycin for lymphatic malformations.

Canalicular interventions

  • Mini-monoka stents (monocanalicular) for canalicular lacerations; pigtail probes (risk of damage); bicanalicular stents; endoscopic DCR with mucosal flaps; balloon dacryoplasty; dacryoendoscopy; 3D printed lacrimal stents.

Trauma

  • Canalicular lacerations repaired within 48 hours with stents (high success); timing of orbital fracture repair (immediate for trapdoor with oculocardiac reflex, within 2 weeks for large fractures); management of retrobulbar haemorrhage (lateral canthotomy and cantholysis within 2 hours); traumatic optic neuropathy (observation favoured over steroids).
