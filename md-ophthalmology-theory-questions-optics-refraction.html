MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Optics, Refraction and Low Vision (39 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 39 optics, refraction and low vision theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Physiological Optics & Aberrations

OP 1. Schematic and reduced eye (Gullstrand, Donders) — cardinal points, with diagrams; the aphakic schematic eye. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Gullstrand’s schematic eye

  • Six cardinal points: two principal points (about 1.35 and 1.60 mm behind the cornea), two nodal points (about 7.08 and 7.33 mm), anterior focal point (−15.7 mm) and posterior focal point (24.4 mm).
  • Total power about +58.6 D (cornea about +43 D, lens about +19 D in situ); refractive indices: cornea 1.376, aqueous 1.336, lens 1.386–1.406, vitreous 1.336.

Reduced eye (Listing, Donders)

  • Single refracting surface with one principal point (1.35 mm) and one nodal point (7.08 mm behind the cornea), power about +58–60 D, axial length about 22.6–24 mm.
  • Use: calculating retinal image size (about 0.015 mm per minute of arc) and the visual angle.

Aphakic schematic eye

  • Lens removed: power about +43 D, about +10 to +12 D hypermetropia; magnification about 25–30% with spectacles (aniseikonia), less with contact lenses (7–9%) and minimal with IOLs.

Revise from: Visual Optics for Optometrists: Schematic Eye, Purkinje Images, Pupil, Aberrations and Depth of Focus

OP 2. Pinhole and stenopaic slit; angle kappa; Purkinje-Sanson images; Stiles-Crawford effect and Purkinje shift. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pinhole

  • Small aperture (about 1.2 mm) allows only central rays, increasing depth of focus; improvement means refractive error; no improvement suggests media or retinal disease (pinhole may worsen vision in macular disease).

Stenopaic slit

  • Slit (about 1 mm) used to find the axis of astigmatism, to assess optical iridectomy site and in irregular astigmatism.

Angle kappa

  • Angle between the visual axis and the pupillary axis; positive kappa (nasal displacement of corneal reflex) gives pseudo-exotropia; relevant for multifocal IOL centration and refractive surgery.

Purkinje–Sanson images

  • Four reflections: I anterior cornea (upright, brightest), II posterior cornea, III anterior lens (largest, upright, moves with accommodation), IV posterior lens (inverted); uses: accommodation studies, eye trackers, Hirschberg test, IOL tilt.

Stiles–Crawford effect

  • Light entering the pupil centre is more effective in stimulating cones than peripheral rays (waveguide nature of cones); reduces aberrations of large pupils.

Purkinje shift

  • Change in peak spectral sensitivity from 555 nm (photopic) to 507 nm (scotopic) in dim light: red appears darker, blue-green brighter.

Revise from: Visual Optics for Optometrists: Schematic Eye, Purkinje Images, Pupil, Aberrations and Depth of Focus, Monocular Diplopia and Ghost Images: Causes, the Pinhole Test and How to Find the Source

OP 3. Sturm's conoid — definition, interval and clinical applications. DNB / UNIV · outline

Sturm’s conoid

  • The shape of the bundle of rays refracted by an astigmatic (toric) lens: two focal lines at right angles separated by an interval, with the circle of least confusion between them.

Interval of Sturm

  • Distance between the two focal lines; proportional to the amount of astigmatism.

Clinical applications

  • Classification of astigmatism (simple, compound, mixed by position of focal lines relative to the retina).
  • Spherical equivalent places the circle of least confusion on the retina (best compromise vision).
  • Jackson cross-cylinder, fan and block tests, astigmatic dial.
  • Fogging technique: both lines placed in front of the retina so the patient’s accommodation does not interfere.
OP 4. Optical aberrations of the eye — spherical, chromatic and higher-order; spectacle-induced aberrations and their minimisation. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Monochromatic aberrations

  • Spherical: peripheral rays focus more strongly; reduced by the pupil, the aspheric cornea and lens grading; increases after myopic LASIK.
  • Coma, oblique astigmatism, field curvature, distortion (barrel, pincushion).

Chromatic aberration

  • Blue focuses in front of red (about 2 D difference); basis of the duochrome test.

Higher-order aberrations

  • Zernike polynomials: coma, trefoil, spherical aberration; measured by wavefront aberrometry (Hartmann–Shack); increased in keratoconus, after refractive surgery; cause haloes and glare.

Spectacle-induced aberrations and minimisation

  • Oblique astigmatism and curvature of field in strong lenses: best-form (meniscus) lenses, aspheric and lenticular designs.
  • Prismatic effects and chromatic dispersion: high-Abbe materials, correct centration and vertex distance, smaller frames.

Revise from: Clinical Optics Made Easy: Vergence, Prisms and Magnification

OP 5. Uniocular diplopia — causes and evaluation. · outline

Uniocular diplopia

  • Double vision persisting when the other eye is covered; persists through a pinhole being absent (optical) or present (others).

Causes

  • Cornea: astigmatism (high or irregular), keratoconus, scars, dry eye, refractive surgery (decentred ablation).
  • Lens: cataract (cortical spokes, early nuclear: polyopia), subluxation (edge of lens in pupil), decentred or tilted IOL, posterior capsular wrinkles, iridotomy (light passing through).
  • Iris: polycoria, iridodialysis, large peripheral iridectomy.
  • Retina: macular disease (ERM, CNV, foveal fluid: rarely).
  • Functional and cerebral (palinopsia, cerebral polyopia: bilateral).

Evaluation

  • Pinhole test, refraction and retinoscopy (scissors reflex), topography, slit lamp with retroillumination, OCT macula; treat the cause.

Revise from: Monocular Diplopia and Ghost Images: Causes, the Pinhole Test and How to Find the Source

OP 6. Prisms — prismatic effect (Prentice rule), Fresnel prisms, and their clinical and therapeutic uses. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Prismatic effect: Prentice rule

  • Prism dioptres = decentration (cm) × lens power (D). One prism dioptre deviates light 1 cm at 1 m.
  • Base direction: decentration of a plus lens gives base in the direction of decentration; a minus lens gives base opposite.

Fresnel prisms

  • Thin PVC sheets of small parallel prisms on the back of spectacles; light, temporary, up to about 30 Δ; reduce acuity and contrast.

Clinical uses

  • Diagnostic: measuring deviations (prism cover test), 4 Δ base-out test, malingering, fusional reserves.
  • Therapeutic: diplopia in decompensating phorias and paralytic squint, convergence insufficiency exercises, nystagmus (null point shift, induced convergence), field expansion in hemianopia (Peli prisms).

Revise from: Prisms in Practice: Prescribing, Splitting, Decentration and Fresnel Prisms, Clinical Optics Made Easy: Vergence, Prisms and Magnification

OP 7. Wavefront analysis and higher-order aberrations — principles and clinical applications. · outline

Wavefront analysis

  • Measures the shape of the light wave leaving (or entering) the eye compared with an ideal plane wave; Hartmann–Shack sensor (lenslet array) or Tscherning and ray tracing.

Zernike polynomials

  • Lower-order aberrations: defocus (sphere) and astigmatism.
  • Higher-order aberrations: coma (decentred cornea, keratoconus), trefoil, spherical aberration (large pupils, night vision, after myopic LASIK), secondary astigmatism; RMS value expresses total error.

Clinical applications

  • Wavefront-guided and wavefront-optimised LASIK and PRK; aspheric IOL selection (negative spherical aberration IOLs balance positive corneal spherical aberration); assessment of keratoconus, night vision complaints, dry eye; contact lens design; adaptive optics imaging of photoreceptors.

Refraction & Refractive Errors

OP 8. Discuss streak retinoscopy and the subjective verification of refraction. Principles and pitfalls of autorefraction. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Streak retinoscopy

  • Principle: observe the reflex from the patient’s pupil; neutralise with lenses (with movement: add plus; against movement: add minus); subtract the working distance (for example 1.5 D at 67 cm).
  • Find the two principal meridians for astigmatism (break, width, skew); cycloplegia in children.

Subjective verification

  • Fogging, best vision sphere, duochrome, Jackson cross-cylinder for axis and power, binocular balancing (prism dissociation or alternate occlusion), near addition.

Autorefraction

  • Principles: Scheiner principle, optometer principle, image quality analysis; infrared light.
  • Pitfalls: instrument (proximal) myopia, accommodation in young patients, media opacities, small pupils, irregular corneas, poor fixation; always verify subjectively.

Revise from: RCOphth Refraction Certificate: Station-by-Station Guide to Retinoscopy, Subjective Refraction, Binocular Balance and Lens Neutralisation

OP 9. Jackson cross cylinder — uses in refraction. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Jackson cross cylinder

  • A combination of a plus and a minus cylinder of equal power at right angles (for example ±0.25 D); handle at 45° to the axes.

Uses

  • Refining cylinder axis: place handle along the cylinder axis, flip, rotate the axis towards the better position (towards the plus axis for plus cylinders).
  • Refining cylinder power: align the cross-cylinder axes with the correcting cylinder, flip, add or reduce cylinder; maintain spherical equivalent.
  • Checking for astigmatism when none is found; near add assessment (fused cross-cylinder).

Revise from: Jackson Cross-Cylinder: How to Refine Axis and Power, Subjective Refraction Step by Step: Fogging, Best Sphere, Cross-Cylinder, Binocular Balance and Near Add

OP 10. Duochrome test. DNB / UNIV · outline

Duochrome test

  • Based on chromatic aberration: red light (longer wavelength) focuses behind green; the patient compares letters on red and green backgrounds.

Interpretation

  • Letters clearer on red: under-corrected myope or over-plussed hyperope (add minus).
  • Clearer on green: over-corrected myope (add plus).
  • Equal: correct.

Uses and limitations

  • Refining spherical end-point in subjective refraction; works in colour-deficient patients (relies on wavelength, not colour naming); less reliable in older patients (yellow lens), with pupil size changes, and when vision is worse than 6/12.

Revise from: Duochrome Test: Red-Green Rule (RAM GAP) Explained

OP 11. Compound astigmatism. · outline

Compound astigmatism

  • Both focal lines lie on the same side of the retina: compound myopic astigmatism (both in front) or compound hypermetropic astigmatism (both behind).

Correction

  • Spherocylindrical lens, e.g. −2.00 DS/−1.00 DC × 180 (compound myopic) or +2.00 DS/+1.00 DC × 90 (compound hypermetropic).

Notes

  • Transposition: change sign of cylinder, add cylinder to sphere, rotate axis by 90°.
  • Compare with simple (one line on the retina) and mixed (one line in front and one behind) astigmatism.
OP 12. Types of astigmatism and the basic principles applied in its management and correction. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Regular (principal meridians at 90°): with-the-rule, against-the-rule, oblique.
  • By focal points: simple, compound, mixed myopic or hypermetropic.
  • Irregular: keratoconus, scars, post-surgical.
  • Corneal vs lenticular vs total.

Principles of correction

  • Full correction in children (amblyopia prevention); in adults, partial correction or rotating the axis towards 90° or 180° to reduce distortion; maintain spherical equivalent.
  • Spectacles (toric, minus cylinder), soft toric or RGP contact lenses (irregular astigmatism), toric IOLs, limbal relaxing incisions, LASIK, PRK and SMILE, corneal cross-linking and ring segments in keratoconus.

Revise from: Refractive Errors for MBBS Students: Myopia, Hypermetropia, Astigmatism, Presbyopia and Their Correction

OP 13. Hypermetropia — classification (manifest, latent), uncovering latent hypermetropia, and spectacle prescription. DNB / UNIV · outline

Classification of hypermetropia

  • Axial, curvature, index, positional (posterior IOL), absence of lens (aphakia).
  • Total hypermetropia (under cycloplegia) = latent (corrected by ciliary tone) + manifest; manifest = facultative (overcome by accommodation) + absolute (cannot be overcome).

Uncovering latent hypermetropia

  • Cycloplegic refraction (cyclopentolate 1% or atropine in young children), fogging techniques, repeated refraction over time with gradual acceptance of plus lenses.

Spectacle prescription

  • Children: full cycloplegic correction if esotropia; otherwise moderate under-correction (e.g. reduce by 1 D symmetrically) with amblyopia and symptoms considered; treat anisometropia.
  • Adults: maximum plus accepted for clear distance vision (manifest); increase gradually; near add when presbyopic.
  • Complications if untreated: accommodative esotropia, amblyopia, asthenopia, angle closure in adults (small eyes).

Revise from: Spectacle Prescription Generator: Printable Glasses Prescription With Near Power, PD and Lens Advice

OP 14. Anisometropia — definition, significance and management. · outline

Definition

  • Difference in refractive error between the two eyes (clinically significant if 1 D or more in sphere or astigmatism).

Significance

  • Amblyopia (hyperopic anisometropia worse: the more hyperopic eye is never in focus), loss of binocularity and stereopsis, aniseikonia (image size difference: about 1% per dioptre with spectacles), prismatic effects when looking away from optical centres (vertical imbalance at near), asthenopia.

Management

  • Children: full spectacle correction plus amblyopia therapy; contact lenses for large differences; refractive surgery in selected cases.
  • Adults: spectacles if tolerated (up to about 3–4 D), contact lenses (reduce aniseikonia in axial anisometropia less, Knapp rule), refractive surgery or lens surgery; slab-off prism for vertical imbalance at near.

Revise from: Anisometropia and Aniseikonia: Prescribing High and Unequal Powers

OP 15. Aniseikonia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Aniseikonia

  • Difference in the perceived size or shape of images between the two eyes; up to about 5% is tolerated.

Types

  • Optical (static: refractive, induced by spectacles; dynamic: prismatic) and retinal (macular disease: ERM causes macropsia; macular oedema: micropsia).

Causes

  • Anisometropia, aphakia corrected by spectacles (about 25–30% magnification), unilateral pseudophakia mismatch, refractive surgery in one eye, retinal disease.

Symptoms and assessment

  • Asthenopia, diplopia, distortion of space, headache; measured with the space eikonometer or new aniseikonia test.

Management

  • Contact lenses or IOL, iseikonic (size) lenses (changing base curve, thickness, vertex distance), refractive surgery; treat the retinal cause.

Revise from: Anisometropia and Aniseikonia: Prescribing High and Unequal Powers

OP 16. Aphakia — problems of aphakic vision (jack-in-the-box phenomenon) and modalities of correction. DNB / UNIV · outline

Problems of aphakic vision with spectacles

  • Magnification (about 25–33%), difficulty judging distances, pincushion distortion, ring scotoma (prismatic effect at lens edge causes a ring of missed field) and jack-in-the-box phenomenon (objects appear and disappear as gaze moves), restricted field, spherical aberration, heavy thick lenses, loss of accommodation, unilateral aphakia: diplopia (aniseikonia).

Modalities of correction

  • Spectacles (high plus lenticular or aspheric lenses), contact lenses (magnification about 7–10%), intraocular lenses (best: secondary IOL in the bag, sulcus, iris-claw, scleral-fixated or glued IOLs), epikeratophakia and refractive keratoplasty (historical).
OP 17. Selection of cycloplegics and mydriatics in refraction. · outline

Choosing a cycloplegic

  • Atropine 1% (ointment, twice daily for 3 days or drops): strongest; children under 5 years with esotropia or suspected latent hypermetropia; allowance (subtract about 1 D) for ciliary tone.
  • Cyclopentolate 1% (two doses 5–10 minutes apart; refract at 30–45 minutes): routine in children over 1 year (0.5% in infants); allowance smaller.
  • Homatropine 2%: older children (rarely used now).
  • Tropicamide 1%: adults and older children (weak cycloplegia, good mydriasis).

Mydriatics for fundus examination

  • Tropicamide 1% with phenylephrine 2.5% (10% avoided in infants, cardiac patients); in preterm babies: dilute combinations (cyclopentolate 0.2% with phenylephrine 1%).

Precautions

  • Check the angle (shallow AC), allergy, systemic side effects (punctal occlusion), dark irides need more drops, warn about blurring and photophobia.

Revise from: Diagnostic Drugs for Optometrists: Mydriatics, Cycloplegics, Anaesthetics and Stains

OP 18. Visual acuity — definition, ETDRS chart and limitations of Snellen; age-appropriate vision tests from birth to school age. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Visual acuity

  • The finest spatial detail the eye resolves; minimum angle of resolution (1 minute of arc for 6/6).

ETDRS chart vs Snellen

  • ETDRS: logMAR progression, five letters per line, equal spacing and legibility; scored letter by letter; reliable for research.
  • Snellen limitations: unequal number of letters per line, irregular steps, crowding differences, poor at low vision levels.

Age-appropriate tests

  • Birth to 6 months: fixation and following, optokinetic nystagmus, preferential looking (Teller cards), VEP.
  • 6 months to 2 years: preferential looking (Cardiff cards), picking up small objects.
  • 2–3 years: picture tests (Kay, Lea symbols, Allen cards).
  • 3–5 years: matching tests (Sheridan-Gardiner, HOTV, Lea), tumbling E, Landolt C.
  • School age: Snellen or logMAR letter charts.

Revise from: Visual Acuity Conversion Chart: Snellen, logMAR, Decimal and Near

OP 19. Evaluation of visual function in opaque media; macular function tests. DNB / UNIV · outline

Evaluation of visual function in opaque media

  • Light perception and projection (PL, PR in four quadrants), colour perception, pupillary reactions (RAPD), two-point discrimination, Maddox rod test (continuous line suggests macular function), entoptic phenomena (Purkinje vascular tree, blue field entoptoscopy), laser interferometry and potential acuity meter, B-scan ultrasound, electrophysiology (flash ERG, VEP), photostress test.

Macular function tests

  • Visual acuity, Amsler grid, photostress recovery time (macular disease: prolonged; optic nerve: normal), colour vision, contrast sensitivity, Maddox rod, two-light discrimination, multifocal ERG, OCT, microperimetry.

Uses

  • Pre-operative prognosis before cataract surgery or keratoplasty in dense media; counselling.
OP 20. Discuss the changing concepts in the management of myopia (including pathological high myopia), with a clinical appraisal of the merits and demerits of the methods available. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Changing concepts

  • Myopia is epidemic (about half of the world by 2050); now treated to slow progression, not only corrected.

Methods to slow progression

  • Low-dose atropine (0.01–0.05%; LAMP study: 0.05% most effective).
  • Optical: defocus-incorporated spectacles (DIMS), highly aspheric lenslets, orthokeratology, multifocal soft contact lenses (MiSight).
  • Outdoor time (at least 2 hours daily), red light therapy (emerging, safety concerns).
  • Merits and demerits: atropine (photophobia and near blur at higher doses, rebound), ortho-k (microbial keratitis risk), spectacles (adherence).

Pathological myopia

  • META-PM classification; myopic CNV (anti-VEGF), myopic traction maculopathy (vitrectomy), staphyloma, retinal detachment risk.
  • Refractive surgery options: LASIK, SMILE, phakic IOLs (ICL) for high myopia, clear lens extraction (RD risk).

Revise from: Pathological Myopia and Myopic Maculopathy: META-PM and ATN Grading

OP 21. Spectacle lenses — bifocal designs, progressive-power and toric lenses; centration, decentration and back-vertex distance. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Bifocals

  • Fused (round-top, flat-top/D-segment), executive (one-piece); image jump and prismatic effect at the segment line; flat-top reduces image jump.

Progressive addition lenses

  • Gradually increasing plus power down the corridor; distance, intermediate and near zones; peripheral unwanted astigmatism (Minkwitz theorem); adaptation required.

Toric lenses

  • Correct astigmatism; minus cylinder form on the back surface (less distortion).

Centration and decentration

  • Optical centres aligned with the pupils (interpupillary distance); deliberate decentration creates prism (Prentice rule); vertical imbalance in anisometropia (slab-off).

Back-vertex distance

  • Effective power changes with distance from the eye for lenses over ±4 D: Fe = F / (1 − dF); plus lenses moved forward act stronger.

Revise from: Spectacle Lens Designs: Aspheric, Bifocals and Progressive Lenses (Fitting and Troubleshooting)

OP 22. Computer vision syndrome; asthenopia and eye strain. DNB / UNIV · outline

Computer vision syndrome (digital eye strain)

  • Eye and vision symptoms related to prolonged screen use.

Causes

  • Reduced and incomplete blinking (dry eye), uncorrected refractive error and presbyopia, accommodative and convergence stress, glare and poor ergonomics, blue light (not proven harmful).

Symptoms

  • Asthenopia (tired eyes, headache, brow ache), dryness, burning, blurring, neck and shoulder pain.

Management

  • 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), correct refraction (computer glasses for intermediate distance), lubricants, blink exercises, screen slightly below eye level at 50–70 cm, reduce glare, humidity, treat binocular vision problems (convergence insufficiency).

Asthenopia

  • Symptoms of eye strain from refractive error, binocular vision anomalies, dry eye, poor lighting, systemic factors (stress, sleep).
OP 23. Space myopia, night myopia and pseudomyopia. DNB / UNIV · outline

Space (empty field) myopia

  • Myopic shift (about 1 D) when looking at a featureless field (sky, fog) as accommodation rests at its tonic position; pilots and astronauts.

Night myopia

  • Myopic shift in dim light (0.5–1.5 D): spherical aberration from large pupils, Purkinje shift towards shorter wavelengths, tonic accommodation; correct with a small minus lens for night driving.

Pseudomyopia

  • Apparent myopia from excessive accommodation (ciliary spasm): near work, stress, uncorrected hyperopia, convergence excess, drugs (miotics, sulphonamides), uveitis; refraction under cycloplegia shows less myopia or hyperopia; treat with cycloplegia, correct hyperopia, vision therapy.

Revise from: Night Myopia, Pseudomyopia and Other Myopic Shifts: When "More Minus" Is Not True Myopia

OP 24. Myopia control in children — low-dose atropine, myopia-control spectacle lenses, orthokeratology and outdoor time: evidence and practice. · outline

Low-dose atropine

  • 0.01–0.05% at night; LAMP study: 0.05% most effective (slows progression by about 50%) with acceptable side effects; ATOM 1 and 2; rebound on stopping is smaller with low doses.

Myopia-control spectacle lenses

  • Defocus incorporated multiple segments (DIMS: about 50–60% slowing), highly aspherical lenslets (HAL), diffusion optics technology (DOT).

Contact lenses

  • Orthokeratology (overnight rigid lenses reshape the cornea; slows axial growth by about 40–50%; infection risk), dual-focus soft lenses (MiSight: about 50–59% slowing in 3 years).

Outdoor time

  • At least 2 hours daily delays onset of myopia (light exposure, dopamine); 20-20-20 rule for near work.

Practice

  • Start treatment in progressing myopes, monitor axial length every 6 months, combine methods (atropine with ortho-k), counsel on adherence and cost; red-light therapy is under evaluation with safety concerns.

Revise from: Myopia Control in 2026: Atropine, Lenslet Spectacles, Ortho-K and Red Light

OP 38. Prescribing spectacles in practice — children, anisometropes and older patients; partial prescriptions and adaptation to cylinders. NEW · outline

General principles

  • Prescribe for the patient’s visual needs and symptoms, not just the numbers; compare with the old prescription; avoid large changes at once; give a trial frame for big changes.

Children

  • Cycloplegic refraction; full correction of hyperopia in esotropia; correct significant myopia, astigmatism (1.5 D or more) and anisometropia (1 D or more) because of amblyopia risk.
  • Guidelines (AAO PPP): treat hyperopia over +4.5 D at 1 year; age-based thresholds; small hyperopia in a straight eye may be left uncorrected.
  • Polycarbonate or Trivex lenses for safety; good frame fit (sports bands); regular review.

Anisometropes

  • Children: full correction (they adapt), contact lenses for large differences.
  • Adults: limit difference to about 2–3 D in spectacles if new; aniseikonia and prismatic effects (vertical imbalance at near: slab-off prism); contact lenses or refractive surgery.

Older patients

  • Small changes, keep the same lens design and base curves, avoid sudden changes in cylinder axis, careful near additions (lower adds for intermediate needs), single-vision distance glasses for walking (falls risk with bifocals and progressives).

Partial prescriptions and cylinder adaptation

  • New or larger cylinders cause distortion (tilted floors, walls): reduce cylinder and adjust sphere to keep the spherical equivalent, or move the axis toward 90° or 180°; minus cylinder form, aspheric designs, short vertex distance.
  • Myopic under-correction is not recommended for myopia control; hyperopes may be under-corrected when asymptomatic.

Revise from: When to Prescribe Glasses in Children: AAO Thresholds by Age, Spectacle Non-Tolerance: A Step-by-Step Troubleshooting Guide for Optometrists

OP 39. Spectacle lens materials and coatings — polycarbonate, high-index, photochromic, anti-reflective and blue-light filtering lenses: evidence and advice. NEW · outline

Lens materials

  • Crown glass (n 1.523; scratch resistant, heavy, shatter risk), CR-39 plastic (n 1.498; light, good optics), polycarbonate (n 1.586; impact resistant, UV blocking; lower Abbe value causes chromatic aberration), Trivex (n 1.53; impact resistant, better optics), high-index plastics (1.60–1.74; thinner for high powers; lower Abbe value).
  • Abbe value: higher means less chromatic dispersion (CR-39 58, polycarbonate 30).

Coatings and treatments

  • Anti-reflective (multi-layer interference coatings: less glare and ghost images, better cosmesis), scratch resistant hard coat, hydrophobic and oleophobic layers, UV filters, mirror coatings.
  • Photochromic lenses: darken with UV; slow in cars (windscreen blocks UV), temperature-dependent.
  • Tints and polarised lenses: reduce glare from water and roads; not for night driving.
  • Blue-light filtering lenses: Cochrane review (2023) found no good evidence that they reduce eye strain, improve sleep or protect the macula.

Advice

  • Children and monocular patients: polycarbonate or Trivex for safety; high prescriptions: high-index, small frames, aspheric designs; outdoor workers: UV protection and polarised sunglasses; avoid over-selling coatings without evidence.

Revise from: Spectacle Lens Materials and Coatings: CR-39, Polycarbonate, Trivex, High Index, Abbe Value and Blue-Cut

Accommodation & Presbyopia

OP 25. Discuss the mechanism (and theories) of accommodation, lens changes during accommodation, range and amplitude, and methods of assessment of its anomalies. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Mechanism

  • Helmholtz theory (accepted): ciliary muscle contraction relaxes zonular tension, the elastic capsule lets the lens become more convex.
  • Schachar theory (equatorial zonular tension increases): controversial. Coleman’s hydraulic theory: vitreous support.

Lens changes

  • Anterior surface curvature increases most, lens thickens, AC shallows, pupil constricts (near triad), lens moves slightly forward.

Range and amplitude

  • Amplitude: difference between far and near point power (about 14 D at 10 years, 1 D at 60 years; Hofstetter formulas).

Assessment

  • Push-up method (RAF rule), minus lens method, dynamic retinoscopy (MEM), accommodative facility (±2 D flippers).

Anomalies

  • Insufficiency, infacility, excess or spasm, paralysis (third nerve, drugs, Adie pupil), presbyopia.

Revise from: Accommodation Testing: Amplitude, MEM and Nott Retinoscopy, Facility, NRA and PRA, Accommodative Anomalies and Vision Therapy: Insufficiency, Excess, Infacility and Spasm

OP 26. Presbyopia — assessment and management, including recent advances without spectacles. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Assessment

  • Age, near point, amplitude of accommodation, working distance and tasks; near add (half the amplitude in reserve), binocular balance.

Management with spectacles

  • Reading glasses, bifocals, progressive lenses, office (occupational) lenses.

Without spectacles

  • Contact lenses: monovision, multifocal.
  • Pharmacological: pilocarpine 1.25% (pinhole effect; risk of RD).
  • Corneal: presbyLASIK, monovision LASIK, corneal inlays (largely withdrawn).
  • Lenticular: refractive lens exchange with multifocal, trifocal or EDOF IOLs.
  • Scleral and accommodating IOL approaches remain experimental.
OP 27. Spasm of accommodation; anomalies of convergence (insufficiency and excess). DNB / UNIV · outline

Spasm of accommodation

  • Excessive, sustained accommodation: pseudomyopia, blurred distance vision, headache, miosis, convergence (spasm of the near reflex: may be functional).
  • Causes: prolonged near work, uncorrected hyperopia, drugs (miotics), trauma, functional.
  • Treatment: cycloplegic drops, correct refraction, reassurance, reading glasses with cycloplegia.

Convergence insufficiency

  • Exophoria greater at near, receded near point of convergence (over 10 cm), reduced positive fusional vergence; asthenopia and diplopia with reading.
  • Treatment: office-based vergence therapy (CITT: most effective), pencil push-ups, base-in prisms for near, correct refractive error.

Convergence excess

  • Esophoria or esotropia greater at near; high AC/A ratio; treatment: plus add for near (bifocals), base-out prisms, surgery for large deviations.

Revise from: Accommodative Anomalies and Vision Therapy: Insufficiency, Excess, Infacility and Spasm

OP 28. Pharmacological and surgical correction of presbyopia — presbyopia-correcting drops, corneal inlays, monovision and EDOF/multifocal IOLs. · outline

Pharmacological correction of presbyopia

  • Pilocarpine 1.25% (Vuity) and others: pupil constriction increases depth of focus (pinhole effect); side effects: headache, brow ache, poor dim-light vision, rare RD; lens-softening drops failed.

Corneal procedures

  • Corneal inlays (KAMRA small aperture, Raindrop: many withdrawn), presbyLASIK, monovision LASIK, conductive keratoplasty (historical).

Monovision

  • Dominant eye for distance, non-dominant for near (about −1.25 to −1.50 D): contact lenses, LASIK or IOLs; trial with contact lenses first; reduced stereopsis.

IOL options

  • Multifocal (diffractive, refractive: trifocals), extended depth of focus (EDOF), accommodating IOLs, light-adjustable IOL, blended vision; trade-offs: haloes, glare, reduced contrast; patient selection (healthy macula, realistic expectations).

Lens-based

  • Refractive lens exchange (risk of RD in myopes).

Revise from: Presbyopia Eye Drops: Pilocarpine and Aceclidine Explained

Colour Vision & Contrast Sensitivity

OP 29. Discuss the theories and recent concepts of the psychophysiology of colour vision, types of colour vision deficiency, and compare the tests available, including colour visual field examination. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Theories

  • Young–Helmholtz trichromatic theory (three cone types: S, M, L) at the receptor level.
  • Hering’s opponent process theory (red–green, blue–yellow, black–white) at the ganglion and LGN level; zone theory combines them.

Recent concepts

  • Cone photopigment genes (L and M on the X chromosome), parvocellular (red–green) and koniocellular (blue–yellow) pathways; cortical colour processing (V4).

Types of deficiency

  • Congenital: anomalous trichromats (protanomaly, deuteranomaly), dichromats (protanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia), monochromats (rod, blue-cone); X-linked red–green in about 8% of males.
  • Acquired: red–green in optic nerve disease, blue–yellow in retinal and lens disease (Köllner’s rule).

Tests

  • Ishihara (screening red–green), Hardy–Rand–Rittler (includes tritan, quantifies), Farnsworth D-15 and 100-hue (classification and severity), Nagel anomaloscope (gold standard), City University test.
  • Colour visual fields: red targets in optic nerve and chiasmal disease (desaturation).

Revise from: Colour Vision Testing: Ishihara Scoring, Other Tests and Job Rules

OP 30. Role of contrast sensitivity in ophthalmology — principles, methods of testing and clinical applications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principle

  • Contrast sensitivity is the ability to detect differences in luminance; the contrast sensitivity function (CSF) across spatial frequencies is more informative than acuity alone.

Methods

  • Pelli–Robson chart (letters of decreasing contrast), sine-wave gratings (Vistech, FACT), Mars letter chart, computerised tests.

Clinical applications

  • Reduced in cataract, glaucoma, optic neuritis, diabetic retinopathy, AMD and after refractive surgery; explains complaints with “6/6” vision; quality of vision after IOLs (multifocal), driving and low-vision assessment.

Revise from: Contrast Sensitivity Testing: Pelli-Robson, Mars and Grating Charts, Normal Values and Uses

Contact Lenses

OP 31. Optics of contact lenses; selection, indications and contraindications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Optics

  • Tear lens: the tear film behind an RGP lens corrects corneal astigmatism; vertex distance effect (power change for over ±4 D); smaller retinal image changes than spectacles (less aniseikonia, wider field).

Selection

  • Keratometry and topography, refraction, tear assessment, lid tension and occupation; trial fitting (central, apical clearance, movement).

Indications

  • Optical: high myopia, anisometropia, aphakia, irregular astigmatism (keratoconus).
  • Therapeutic: bandage, drug delivery. Cosmetic. Sports and occupational.

Contraindications

  • Dry eye, active infection or inflammation, blepharitis, poor hygiene, uncontrolled diabetes, dusty environment, inability to handle lenses.
OP 32. Types of contact lenses — RGP, soft, extended-wear, disposable, therapeutic/bandage and cosmetic lenses; safe contact-lens practice. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • RGP: oxygen permeable, sharp optics, keratoconus and astigmatism; scleral lenses for irregular corneas and ocular surface disease.
  • Soft hydrogel and silicone hydrogel (higher oxygen transmission); toric and multifocal designs.
  • Extended wear: higher microbial keratitis risk.
  • Disposable: daily (safest), fortnightly, monthly.
  • Therapeutic (bandage): epithelial defects, after PRK, bullous keratopathy, small perforations.
  • Cosmetic and prosthetic tinted lenses (iris printed); orthokeratology (reverse geometry).

Safe contact lens practice

  • Hand washing, no tap water or saliva, no sleeping in lenses unless prescribed, replace on schedule, fresh solution, case care, remove and seek help for a red eye (Acanthamoeba and Pseudomonas keratitis).

Revise from: Contact Lens Calculator: Soft, Toric and RGP Power, Base Curve and Tear Lens, Contact Lens Quick Reference and Fitting Guide: Vertex, LARS, RGP Patterns, Irregular Cornea and Scleral Lenses

OP 33. Contact lenses in keratoconus and irregular astigmatism; orthokeratology. DNB / UNIV · outline

Contact lenses in keratoconus and irregular astigmatism

  • Rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses: a tear lens neutralises irregular astigmatism; fitting patterns (three-point touch, apical clearance).
  • Special designs: Rose K, hybrid lenses (rigid centre, soft skirt), piggyback (RGP over soft lens), scleral and mini-scleral lenses (vault over the cornea: advanced keratoconus, post-keratoplasty, ocular surface disease).
  • Other indications: post-keratoplasty astigmatism, corneal scars, post-refractive surgery irregularity.

Orthokeratology

  • Reverse-geometry rigid lenses worn overnight to flatten the central cornea temporarily: corrects low to moderate myopia (up to about −4 to −6 D) during the day and slows myopia progression in children.
  • Risks: microbial keratitis (Pseudomonas, Acanthamoeba), need for strict hygiene.

Revise from: Contact Lens Quick Reference and Fitting Guide: Vertex, LARS, RGP Patterns, Irregular Cornea and Scleral Lenses

OP 34. Complications of contact-lens wear. DNB / UNIV · outline

Complications of contact lens wear

  • Hypoxic: corneal oedema, epithelial microcysts, neovascularisation, endothelial polymegathism, corneal warpage.
  • Mechanical: abrasions, superior epithelial arcuate lesions (SEALs), lens decentration.
  • Toxic and allergic: solution toxicity, giant papillary conjunctivitis, contact lens-induced papillary conjunctivitis.
  • Inflammatory: sterile infiltrates (CLARE, contact lens peripheral ulcer).
  • Infectious: microbial keratitis (Pseudomonas, Acanthamoeba, fungi): sight-threatening; risk factors: overnight wear, poor hygiene, tap water, swimming.
  • Dry eye, superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis, limbal stem cell deficiency.

Prevention

  • Proper fitting, daily disposable lenses, avoid sleeping and swimming in lenses, rub and rinse cleaning, replace cases, regular review.

Low Vision & Rehabilitation

OP 35. What are low vision aids? Discuss their optical principles, indications and clinical applicability. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Devices that improve visual function in low vision (best-corrected acuity below 6/18 or field under 10° from fixation, but still potentially usable vision).

Optical principles

  • Relative distance magnification (moving closer), relative size magnification (large print), angular magnification (telescopes), electronic magnification (CCTV), field expansion (prisms, reverse telescopes).

Types

  • Near: high-plus spectacles, hand-held and stand magnifiers, illuminated magnifiers.
  • Distance: Galilean and Keplerian telescopes (hand-held, spectacle-mounted, bioptic).
  • Non-optical: lighting, contrast enhancement, typoscope, large print, apps, screen readers, tactile aids.

Clinical applicability

  • AMD, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, optic atrophy, albinism; training and motivation determine success; limitations include small field and working distance with higher magnification.

Revise from: Low Vision Aids Calculator: Kestenbaum Rule and Magnification, Low Vision Assessment Step by Step: History, Charts, Contrast, Magnification and Rehabilitation

OP 36. WHO definitions of low vision; evaluation of a person with low vision and goals of visual rehabilitation. DNB / UNIV · outline

WHO definitions

  • Low vision (WHO, 1992 for services): best-corrected acuity in the better eye less than 6/18 to light perception, or visual field less than 10° from fixation, but who uses or could use vision for planning or doing a task.
  • ICD-11: moderate (worse than 6/18 to 6/60), severe (worse than 6/60 to 3/60) visual impairment, and blindness (worse than 3/60).

Evaluation of a person with low vision

  • History: diagnosis, goals (reading, mobility, work, school), current aids, lighting, psychosocial status.
  • Examination: distance and near acuity (logMAR, ETDRS), contrast sensitivity, fields (central scotoma, preferred retinal locus), colour, glare, refraction (careful, with large steps).
  • Trial of aids: magnifiers (hand, stand), spectacle magnifiers, telescopes, electronic devices.

Goals of rehabilitation

  • Maximise use of residual vision, achieve the patient’s goals (reading, mobility), independence in daily living, education and employment, psychological support, eccentric viewing training.

Revise from: Low Vision Assessment Step by Step: History, Charts, Contrast, Magnification and Rehabilitation

OP 37. Electronic and smartphone-based low vision devices and assistive technology. · outline

Electronic low vision devices

  • Video magnifiers (CCTV: desktop and portable), head-mounted electronic magnifiers (eSight, IrisVision), optical character recognition devices (OrCam), screen magnification and screen readers (JAWS, NVDA), audiobooks and talking devices.

Smartphone and tablet aids

  • Built-in accessibility (zoom, magnifier, VoiceOver and TalkBack, high-contrast modes, dictation), apps for text reading (Seeing AI, Envision, Google Lookout), object and currency recognition, navigation apps, remote assistance (Be My Eyes).

Advantages

  • High variable magnification, contrast enhancement, portability, affordable (often free apps); need training, battery, cost of dedicated devices.

Other assistive technology

  • Smart canes, GPS devices, Braille displays, accessible digital books (DAISY), artificial vision (retinal and cortical prostheses).
