Paediatric Optometry: Vision Testing by Age, Refraction and an Amblyopia Plan for Optometrists

For: Optometrists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Infant acuity test using gratings: Preferential looking (Teller or Keeler cards)
  • Acuity test for 3–5 years: Crowded logMAR (Keeler, Kay, Lea, HOTV)
  • First step in amblyopia: Full-time glasses (refractive adaptation)
  • PEDIG patching for moderate amblyopia: 2 hours a day
  • Examine the child first and talk later: attention spans are short, so do the cover test and acuity before anything uncomfortable.

RPC Rounds for Optometrists · B.Optom study guide

Children are not small adults: they cannot always tell you what is wrong, their vision is still developing, and an uncorrected problem in early childhood can cause lifelong amblyopia. A paediatric examination must be quick, playful and complete, with the right test for each age.

History

Birth history (prematurity, ROP screening), developmental milestones, family history (squint, amblyopia, high refractive error, retinoblastoma, childhood glaucoma), parental concerns (eye turn, head tilt, holding things close, squinting, poor school performance), and screen and outdoor time.

Visual acuity tests by age

AgeTest
Birth to 6 monthsFixing and following a face or light; preferential looking (Teller or Keeler cards); objection to occlusion of one eye
6 months to 2 yearsPreferential looking cards; Cardiff cards (also up to 3 years); fixation preference in strabismus
2 to 3 yearsPicture tests (Kay pictures, Lea symbols) with matching
3 to 5 yearsCrowded logMAR tests (Keeler crowded logMAR, Kay crowded, Lea symbols, HOTV), matching card
Over 5 yearsLetter logMAR charts (crowded where possible)

Crowded tests are essential: single optotypes overestimate acuity in amblyopia. Expect lower "normal" acuity in young children (for example about 0.2 logMAR at 3 years, reaching adult levels by about 5–6 years).

Binocular vision and other tests

  • Cover test at near and distance with an interesting target; Hirschberg and Bruckner in infants; ocular motility.
  • Stereopsis: Lang (no glasses), Frisby, TNO or Titmus.
  • Red reflex (Bruckner) to detect cataract, retinoblastoma and anisometropia; pupil reactions.
  • Colour vision in school-age children (Ishihara, or pictorial plates for young children).

Refraction

Cycloplegic retinoscopy is the standard for a first examination and for any squint or amblyopia (cyclopentolate 1%; 0.5% under 1 year). Prescribe according to age-based guidelines (see paediatric glasses prescribing and cycloplegic refraction).

Amblyopia: an optometrist's plan

  1. Confirm reduced best-corrected acuity in one eye (two or more lines difference) with no structural cause; dilate and examine the fundus.
  2. Glasses first: full-time wear of the full correction (refractive adaptation over about 16 weeks improves many children).
  3. If vision plateaus with a residual difference: patching (2 hours a day for moderate, 6 hours for severe amblyopia, per PEDIG) or atropine penalisation of the better eye.
  4. Review every 6–8 weeks; check the better eye for reverse amblyopia; taper treatment slowly to reduce recurrence.
  5. Dichoptic digital treatments are an option where available. Refer squint, dense or non-responding amblyopia, and any structural cause to a paediatric ophthalmologist.

Myopia in children

Encourage at least about 2 hours outdoors daily, limit continuous near work (20-20-20 rule), and discuss myopia control (low-dose atropine, DIMS or HAL spectacle lenses, myopia control contact lenses, ortho-k) for progressing myopes (spectacle designs compared in myopia-control lenses). See myopia control.

Red flags: refer the same day or urgently

  • White pupil (leukocoria) or absent red reflex: retinoblastoma or cataract.
  • New squint, especially with headache or other neurological signs; sudden onset squint in a previously normal child.
  • Large cloudy corneas with watering and photophobia (congenital glaucoma).
  • Nystagmus in an infant, poor visual behaviour, or a head tilt with abnormal eye movements.
  • Any suspicion of non-accidental injury.

Practical pearls

  • Examine the child first and talk later: attention spans are short, so do the cover test and acuity before anything uncomfortable.
  • Make every test a game: "put your finger on the matching picture" works better than "read the chart".
  • Give parents a written plan for glasses wear and patching; adherence decides the outcome.

Where students slip

  • Using single optotypes and missing amblyopia.
  • Prescribing from a dry autorefraction in a young child.
  • Missing an absent red reflex because the child was "too difficult to examine".
