Autoclaves, Sterilisation Indicators and OT Fogging: Class N, S and B Autoclaves, Bowie-Dick and Biological Indicators, Fumigation

For: Practising ophthalmologists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • For eye surgery, use a Class B (pre-vacuum) autoclave: handpieces, cannulas and wrapped sets need active air removal.
  • Check every load with the printout and chemical indicators outside and inside each pack; run a biological indicator at least weekly and with every implant load.
  • Run a Bowie-Dick or Helix test every morning before the first load.
  • Chemical indicator types 1 to 6 measure different things; tape (type 1) only shows the pack was processed.
  • Cleaning comes first; fogging is an add-on. Replace formaldehyde fumigation with safer agents such as hydrogen peroxide, and record everything.
Print tool: Printable registers (autoclave cycle log and OT fogging log).

For a doctor in private practice, the theatre is your responsibility from the floor up: the autoclave, the indicators, the cleaning, the air and the records. This guide explains the classes of autoclave, the types of sterilisation indicators and how often to use each, and what fogging and fumigation can and cannot do. It is written for eye surgeons and other doctors setting up or running a small theatre, and for their OT staff.

The short answer

  1. For eye surgery, a Class B (pre-vacuum) benchtop autoclave is the safe choice, because phaco handpieces, cannulas and wrapped or pouched sets need active air removal.
  2. Monitor every cycle three ways: the physical printout, chemical indicators (outside and inside every pack) and biological indicators at least weekly.
  3. Run a Bowie-Dick or Helix test every day before the first load in a pre-vacuum autoclave.
  4. Thorough cleaning is what makes rooms safe; fogging is only an add-on. Formaldehyde fumigation is toxic and is being replaced by safer methods.
  5. Write everything down: cycle number, load, indicators, operator. If it is not recorded, it did not happen.

How steam sterilisation works

Steam kills microbes by transferring heat as it condenses on every surface. It only works if the steam touches every surface, so air must be removed from the chamber, the packs and the lumens of hollow instruments. Air pockets are the commonest reason sterilisation fails.

CycleTemperatureTypical holding timeApproximate pressure
Standard (wrapped or unwrapped)121 °C15 to 20 minutes (longer for gravity cycles and large packs)about 1.1 bar (15 psi) above atmospheric
Fast (pre-vacuum)134 °C3 to 4 minutesabout 2.1 bar (30 psi) above atmospheric
Extended (prion-risk instruments, where policy requires)134 °C18 minutesabout 2.1 bar

Holding time is the time at full temperature, not the whole cycle. The whole cycle also includes air removal, heating, exhaust and drying. Always use the cycles your autoclave maker has validated.

Classes of benchtop autoclave

The European standard EN 13060 sorts small steam sterilisers into three classes by what they can sterilise. Indian, Middle Eastern and many Asian suppliers use the same labels.

ClassHow air is removedWhat it can steriliseProsCons
Class N ("naked")Gravity: steam pushes air outSolid, unwrapped instruments onlyCheapest; simple; short cyclesCannot sterilise hollow, porous, wrapped or pouched items; instruments are not sterile once the door opens
Class S ("specified")Single vacuum or other method; varies by modelWhatever the maker has tested and states, often some wrapped and some hollow itemsCheaper than Class B; some models cover simple pouched loadsYou must read the maker's list of validated loads; not all S models handle lumens such as phaco tips
Class B ("big small")Fractionated (repeated) pre-vacuumAll loads: wrapped, pouched, porous (drapes, gauze) and hollow (cannulas, handpieces)Most reliable; dries loads; Bowie-Dick and Helix test cycles built inHighest cost; longer cycles; needs good-quality water and regular servicing

Other options you will meet:

  • Vertical or horizontal "drum" autoclaves (common in India): gravity steam, often heated electrically with a pressure gauge only. They are fine for linen and solid metal instruments in drums if run long enough, but cannot be relied on for lumens or handpieces, and have no printout or air-removal test.
  • Large steam sterilisers (EN 285), used in hospital sterile services (CSSD): pre-vacuum, validated, with full records.
  • Immediate-use steam sterilisation ("flash"): an unwrapped item run on a short cycle for urgent use. Keep it for emergencies, such as a dropped instrument, never as routine.
  • Low-temperature methods for heat-sensitive items: hydrogen peroxide gas plasma, ethylene oxide (needs aeration and is toxic) and low-temperature steam formaldehyde. Check the instrument maker's instructions before using any of them.

Sterilisation indicators

No single test proves a load is sterile. Use three kinds of monitoring together:

KindWhat it showsProsCons
Physical (mechanical)Gauges, display and printout of time, temperature and pressureEvery cycle; immediateShows chamber conditions only, not what happened inside a pack or lumen
ChemicalInk or chemical changes colour when exposed to conditionsCheap; immediate; can be used in and on every packSome types show only exposure, not sterility
Biological (BI)Spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus are killed or notThe only direct test of killingResults take time (from under an hour with rapid-readout BIs to 24 to 48 hours); costs more; needs an incubator

Chemical indicator types (ISO 11140-1). The type number tells you what the indicator measures; a higher number is not simply "better".

TypeExampleWhat it tells youPros and cons
Type 1: process indicatorAutoclave tape, indicator printed on pouchesThe pack went through a cycleSeparates processed from unprocessed packs; says nothing about sterility
Type 2: specific testBowie-Dick test pack or sheet; Helix PCD with stripAir removal and steam penetration in pre-vacuum autoclavesEssential daily test for Class B; not a load indicator
Type 3: single variablePellet that melts at a set temperatureOne condition reached (usually temperature)Rarely used now; ignores time and steam
Type 4: multi-variableStrip that reacts to time and temperatureTwo or more conditions reachedBetter than types 1 and 3; still less exact than 5 and 6
Type 5: integrating indicatorMoving-front stripAll critical conditions, matched to a biological indicator's performanceGood internal pack indicator; can be used to release non-implant loads where policy allows
Type 6: emulating indicatorStrip for one specific cycle (for example 134 °C, 3.5 minutes)All conditions of that specific cycleVery precise; must match the cycle exactly or results mislead

Bowie-Dick and Helix tests

  • Bowie-Dick test: run daily, in an empty chamber, after a warm-up cycle and before the first load, in any pre-vacuum autoclave. A uniform colour change means air was removed; a pale centre means air leak, poor vacuum or poor steam.
  • Helix test (a hollow-load process challenge device): a long narrow tube with an indicator strip in a capsule at its end. It mimics a cannula or handpiece lumen. Use it daily in Class B machines that sterilise hollow instruments, and in every load if your policy requires it.
  • Vacuum leak test: weekly, or as the maker advises.
  • If any test fails, do not use the autoclave for patient loads until it is fixed and retested.

How to read indicators: pass and fail examples

These drawings show what a good and a bad result usually look like. Real products differ in colour and design, so keep the maker's colour chart next to the autoclave and train every staff member who releases loads.

If any indicator fails: do not use the pack; reprocess it; check how the load was packed and placed; run a Bowie-Dick or Helix test; and if the failure repeats, stop using the autoclave, call the engineer and recall items from earlier loads back to the last good biological indicator.

Special care for eye instruments

  • TASS (toxic anterior segment syndrome) is linked to residues: detergent, enzymatic cleaner, endotoxin, oxidised metal and denatured viscoelastic. Flush cannulas and handpiece lumens with sterile or distilled water straight after use, rinse very thoroughly after cleaning, and never use tap water for the final rinse.
  • Follow the instrument maker's reprocessing instructions, especially for phaco handpieces and tips; some ban ultrasonic cleaning or certain detergents.
  • Use single-use items where available (cannulas, blades, vitrectomy cutters) and never reprocess items marked single use.
  • Dry instruments fully before packing; wet packs after a cycle are not sterile.
  • Keep a separate tray and labels for each set so traceability is easy.
  • For more on sutures and instruments, see ophthalmic sutures, needles and sterilisation; for outbreak steps, see the endophthalmitis guide.

Fogging and fumigation

Fumigation fills a sealed room with a gas or vapour; fogging sprays a fine mist of liquid disinfectant. Both are meant to reach surfaces and air that cleaning misses. Neither replaces cleaning: dust and organic material shield microbes, so the room must be cleaned first.

MethodHow it worksProsCons
Formaldehyde (formalin) fumigationFormalin heated or mixed with potassium permanganate releases formaldehyde gas; neutralised with ammonia afterCheap; long tradition; wide activityFormaldehyde is a known human carcinogen and strong irritant; long closure (often overnight); residues; staff exposure. Indian public-health cleanliness (Kayakalp) guidelines advise replacing it with safer agents
Hydrogen peroxide with silver (for example 11% H2O2 with silver nitrate)Fogging machine sprays a fine mist; dose per room volumeBreaks down to water and oxygen; shorter closure; widely used in Indian hospitalsNeeds correct dose for the room volume and a closed room; can damage some electronics and metals; weaker on dirty surfaces
Hydrogen peroxide vapour or dry mist systemsMachine generates vapour or very fine particles of H2O2Strong evidence for killing spores on surfaces; good penetrationExpensive equipment; room must be sealed; trained operator
Ultraviolet-C (UV-C) devicesMobile lamp irradiates surfacesNo chemicals; quick; no residueOnly works in line of sight; shadows; eye and skin hazard, so the room must be empty
Routine spraying of other disinfectantsHand or machine spray of quaternary ammonium or alcohol productsEasyLittle evidence; uneven coverage; inhalation exposure

What the evidence says. Clean surfaces, good air handling and good surgical practice prevent theatre infections. Fogging may help after construction work, after an outbreak or known contamination, or when reopening a room left closed for a long time. In theatres with a working HVAC system with HEPA filters and good cleaning, many infection-control bodies do not consider routine fogging necessary. If your accreditation body or hospital policy requires it, follow the policy and record it.

If you fog:

  1. Clean all surfaces with detergent and then disinfectant first.
  2. Measure the room: length × width × height, in cubic metres or cubic feet.
  3. Dilute and dose exactly as the product label says for that volume.
  4. Cover or remove sensitive electronics if the maker advises; switch off HVAC if the product requires it.
  5. Seal the room, put a "Do not enter" sign on the door, and keep everyone out for the stated contact time.
  6. Ventilate as directed before staff re-enter; wipe residue from work surfaces if required.
  7. Record date, product, dilution, dose, contact time and operator.

Never mix formalin with other chemicals except as a validated method, never enter a fumigated room without the stated wait and ventilation, and never fog with people, patients or food in the room. Staff handling formalin need gloves, goggles and a suitable respirator.

Day-to-day theatre cleaning

WhenWhat
Start of dayDamp-dust horizontal surfaces, lights and equipment; check temperature, humidity and pressure readings
Between casesClean and disinfect surfaces that touched the patient or were splashed (table, armrests, microscope handles, foot pedals, trolleys); remove waste and used linen
End of day (terminal clean)Clean all surfaces and the floor wall-to-wall with detergent and disinfectant; clean scrub sinks; restock
WeeklyWalls, ceilings within reach, shelves, cupboards, air grilles; check and clean the autoclave chamber and water tank as the maker advises
Blood or body-fluid spillsAbsorb, then disinfect with freshly made hypochlorite at your policy strength (often 1% available chlorine) or another approved product
  • Use freshly prepared disinfectant solutions; label the dilution and date.
  • Use separate mops and cloths for the theatre, colour-coded by area.
  • Microscope optics and phaco machines: clean only as their makers advise; alcohol and hypochlorite damage some coatings and plastics.
  • Air: theatres with HVAC should have positive pressure and HEPA filtration; ASHRAE 170, used in many countries, sets at least 20 total air changes an hour, of which at least 4 are fresh air, for operating rooms. Keep doors closed during surgery.
  • Routine air and surface cultures (settle plates, swabs) are not recommended by most guidelines except after construction, during outbreak investigation or where your accreditation body requires them.

Common mistakes

  • Using a Class N or drum autoclave for phaco handpieces and cannulas.
  • Overloading the chamber or stacking pouches flat, which traps air; stand pouches on edge, paper to plastic.
  • Opening the door before drying is finished; wet packs are not sterile.
  • Skipping the daily Bowie-Dick or Helix test "because the machine was fine yesterday".
  • Using tap water in the autoclave reservoir.
  • Relying on autoclave tape alone.
  • Fogging a dusty room instead of cleaning it.
  • No log, so no traceability when a problem appears.

Questions doctors ask

Is a pressure-cooker type autoclave acceptable for cataract surgery? Only for solid, unwrapped metal instruments with long enough cycles. For handpieces, cannulas and wrapped sets you need validated air removal: Class B, or Class S validated for those loads.

How long are sterilised packs good for? Most policies now use event-related shelf life: a pack stays sterile until something compromises it (tear, wetness, dropped, seal broken), provided it is stored clean, dry and off the floor. Some still use fixed dates; follow your policy and label every pack with the date and cycle number.

Do I need an incubator for biological indicators? Yes, a small BI incubator (or a rapid-readout reader) at the temperature the BI maker specifies, usually 55 to 60 °C for steam BIs.

Should I fog after every list? No. Clean between cases and do a terminal clean at the end of the day. Fog only when your policy requires it or for the special situations above.
