Corneal Drawing: Colour Coding Chart

Last updated: November 2026

A corneal drawing records what you see at the slit lamp so that anyone can follow a keratitis, a scar or a graft from visit to visit. Draw two views: a frontal view of the cornea as a circle with clock hours, and a slit view (optical section) showing how deep the lesion goes. A shared colour code makes the drawing readable at a glance. This page is the corneal companion to our fundus drawing colour chart.

How to draw it

  1. Frontal view first. Draw the limbus as a circle and mark the clock hours, with temporal (T) and nasal (N) sides labelled for the eye you are drawing (OD or OS).
  2. Stain before you draw. Instil fluorescein and examine with the cobalt blue filter, so epithelial defects, punctate staining and a Seidel leak go in green at the same sitting.
  3. Draw the lesion to scale and write its size in millimetres (length × width), measured with the slit-beam length.
  4. Add the slit view at the level of the lesion: epithelium on the outside curve, endothelium on the inside, with the stroma divided into anterior, middle and posterior thirds (A / M / P).
  5. Record the anterior chamber: KPs, cells and the height of any hypopyon or hyphaema in millimetres.
Record every time: size (mm × mm) · depth (A / M / P, or % of corneal thickness) · clock-hour location · distance from the limbus · hypopyon height (mm). Repeat the same measurements at each visit to judge whether a keratitis is responding.

Colour code

ColourWhat it shows
BlackCorneal outline and limbus; scars graded by density: nebula (stippled), macula (grey), leucoma (solid); band keratopathy; guttata; Vogt's striae; foreign body; wound with sutures; corneal nerves; contact lens or tissue adhesive (dashed outline); arcus; IOL
BlueOedema: stromal oedema as diffuse shading, epithelial oedema as small circles; Descemet's folds as wavy lines; epithelial bulla (an omega shape on the slit view)
BrownPigment: iron line and melanosis; rust ring around a foreign body; old (pigmented) KPs; Krukenberg spindle; pupil and synechiae; Fleischer ring
RedBlood vessels and blood: superficial vessels bright red, wavy, crossing the limbus; deep vessels dark red, straight, ending at the limbus; ghost vessels as dotted lines; rose bengal staining as dots; haemorrhage; hyphaema; pterygium
YellowInflammatory cells: infiltrate (dense core, feathery edge); satellite lesions; ring infiltrate; hypopyon (mark the height in mm); fresh KPs; mutton-fat KPs
GreenFluorescein staining: epithelial defect or ulcer (filled area); punctate epithelial keratitis (dots); dendrite with terminal bulbs; filaments; Seidel-positive leak

Telling look-alikes apart on the drawing

PairHow the drawing shows the difference
Superficial vs deep vesselsSuperficial: bright red, wavy, branching, continuous with conjunctival vessels across the limbus. Deep: dark red, straight, parallel, stopping at the limbus.
Infiltrate vs scarInfiltrate (yellow) has a dense core and a fuzzy, feathery edge and means active disease. A scar (black) has a sharp edge and graded density.
Epithelial defect vs infiltrateDraw the green staining area and the yellow infiltrate separately and measure both: in healing, the defect closes before the infiltrate clears.
Fresh vs old KPsFresh KPs are yellow (active); old, pigmented KPs are brown. Mutton-fat KPs are large and greasy, typical of granulomatous uveitis.
Dendrite vs pseudodendriteA herpetic dendrite has terminal bulbs and stains well (green). Pseudodendrites (healing abrasion, Acanthamoeba, zoster) lack true terminal bulbs.

KP colour varies between textbooks: some draw all KPs yellow, others all brown. The scar shading (nebula, macula, leucoma), mutton-fat KPs and the rust ring symbols are common teaching conventions rather than a published standard. Follow your department's code and keep it consistent.
