Landmark Trials in Paediatric Ophthalmology: CRYO-ROP, ETROP, BEAT-ROP, PEDIG, IATS, LAMP and CHAMP

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • CRYO-ROP: Unfavourable structural outcome fell from about 48% to 26% at 1 year, and the benefit persisted at 15 years.
  • STOP-ROP: Progression to threshold was not significantly reduced (41% vs 48%), and supplemental oxygen worsened lung disease.
  • ETROP: Early treatment reduced unfavourable visual (about 20% to 15%) and structural outcomes (about 16% to 9%).
  • BEAT-ROP: Recurrence was lower with bevacizumab in zone I disease (about 6% vs 42%), but not in zone II.
  • RAINBOW: Treatment success was about 80% with 0.2 mg vs 66% with laser; the difference narrowly missed statistical significance.
  • PEDIG ATS: patching: Two hours daily was as effective as six for moderate amblyopia; six hours was as effective as full-time for severe amblyopia.

Paediatric trials changed three areas: when and how to treat retinopathy of prematurity, how much patching or atropine amblyopia needs, and whether atropine drops can slow childhood myopia. Many were run by the Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group (PEDIG).

How each trial is set out: design and size, the question it asked, what it found, why it matters, the trap examiners like to set, and where it stands today. Numbers are rounded; check the original paper before quoting a figure in a thesis.

Retinopathy of prematurity

CRYO-ROP (1988–2001)

Multicenter Trial of Cryotherapy for Retinopathy of Prematurity

DesignCryotherapy in one eye vs observation in the other · 291 infants with threshold ROP · followed to 15 years
QuestionDoes cryotherapy help threshold ROP?
FindingUnfavourable structural outcome fell from about 48% to 26% at 1 year, and the benefit persisted at 15 years.
Why it mattersTreating threshold ROP became standard.
Viva trapThreshold ROP: stage 3 in zone I or II, at least 5 contiguous or 8 total clock hours, with plus disease.

STOP-ROP (2000)

Supplemental Therapeutic Oxygen for Prethreshold ROP

DesignOxygen saturation target 96–99% vs 89–94% · 649 infants with prethreshold ROP
QuestionDoes extra oxygen stop prethreshold ROP from progressing?
FindingProgression to threshold was not significantly reduced (41% vs 48%), and supplemental oxygen worsened lung disease.
Why it mattersOxygen is not a treatment for established ROP.
Viva trapThis is not the "oxygen causes ROP" trial; it says nothing about oxygen targets from birth.

ETROP (2003)

Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity

DesignEarly treatment of high-risk prethreshold ROP vs conventional treatment at threshold · 401 infants
QuestionShould high-risk ROP be treated before it reaches threshold?
FindingEarly treatment reduced unfavourable visual (about 20% to 15%) and structural outcomes (about 16% to 9%).
Why it mattersCreated the type 1 / type 2 classification: treat type 1 within 72 hours; observe type 2.
Viva trapType 1: zone I any stage with plus; zone I stage 3 without plus; zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus.

BEAT-ROP (2011)

Bevacizumab Eliminates the Angiogenic Threat of ROP

DesignIntravitreal bevacizumab vs laser · 150 infants with zone I or posterior zone II stage 3+ ROP
QuestionIs intravitreal bevacizumab better than laser?
FindingRecurrence was lower with bevacizumab in zone I disease (about 6% vs 42%), but not in zone II.
Why it mattersAnti-VEGF became an option, especially for zone I disease.
Viva trapLate reactivation after anti-VEGF means longer follow-up; the trial was not powered for systemic safety.

RAINBOW (2019)

Ranibizumab vs laser for ROP

DesignRanibizumab 0.1 or 0.2 mg vs laser · 225 infants · 24 weeks
QuestionIs a single ranibizumab injection as good as laser?
FindingTreatment success was about 80% with 0.2 mg vs 66% with laser; the difference narrowly missed statistical significance.
Why it mattersRanibizumab approved for ROP in many countries.
Viva trapFewer unfavourable structural outcomes with ranibizumab, but the primary end point was not met.

Amblyopia

PEDIG ATS: patching (2003)

Amblyopia Treatment Studies: patching dose

DesignTwo hours vs six hours of daily patching (moderate amblyopia); six hours vs full-time (severe) · children aged 3 to under 7
QuestionHow many hours of patching are needed?
FindingTwo hours daily was as effective as six for moderate amblyopia; six hours was as effective as full-time for severe amblyopia.
Why it mattersModerate daily patching became standard, improving adherence.
Viva trapModerate amblyopia is 6/12 to 6/24 (20/40 to 20/80); severe is 6/30 to 6/120.

PEDIG ATS: atropine (2002–2004)

Amblyopia Treatment Studies: atropine

DesignAtropine 1% vs patching; then weekend vs daily atropine · children aged 3 to under 7 with moderate amblyopia
QuestionIs atropine penalisation as good as patching?
FindingAtropine was about as effective as patching, and weekend atropine was as effective as daily.
Why it mattersAtropine is a first-line alternative to patching.
Viva trapAtropine works best when the fellow eye is hyperopic, so blurring it actually forces use of the amblyopic eye.

PEDIG: refractive adaptation (2006)

Optical treatment of amblyopia

DesignGlasses alone · 84 children aged 3 to under 7 with untreated anisometropic or strabismic amblyopia
QuestionHow much can glasses alone achieve?
FindingGlasses alone improved acuity by about 3 lines on average, and about a quarter of children resolved without patching.
Why it mattersWear glasses for several weeks before starting patching.
Viva trapImprovement continued for up to 18 weeks or more.

Infant cataract

IATS (2010–2014)

Infant Aphakia Treatment Study

DesignPrimary IOL vs contact lens aphakia after unilateral cataract surgery · 114 infants aged 1–6 months · followed to 10 years
QuestionPrimary IOL or contact lens in infants?
FindingVisual outcomes were similar, but the IOL group had more adverse events and more reoperations.
Why it mattersContact lens aphakia remains reasonable in infants under 7 months.
Viva trapThe higher reoperation rate with IOLs was mainly visual axis opacification.

Binocular vision

CITT (2008)

Convergence Insufficiency Treatment Trial

DesignOffice-based vergence and accommodative therapy with home reinforcement vs home-based pencil push-ups vs home computer therapy vs placebo therapy · 221 children aged 9–17 · 12 weeks
QuestionWhich treatment works for symptomatic convergence insufficiency?
FindingOffice-based therapy with home reinforcement was the most effective, with about 73% successful or improved.
Why it mattersOffice-based therapy became the evidence-based first choice.
Viva trapPencil push-ups alone were no better than placebo therapy.

Myopia control

ATOM2 (2012)

Atropine for the Treatment of Myopia 2

DesignAtropine 0.5%, 0.1% or 0.01% · 400 children · Singapore · 5 years
QuestionDoes a lower atropine dose work with fewer side effects?
Finding0.01% gave the smallest rebound after stopping and the fewest side effects, with myopia control close to higher doses over 5 years.
Why it mattersLow-dose atropine entered clinical use.
Viva trapATOM2 had no placebo arm; axial length control with 0.01% was weak.

LAMP (2019)

Low-concentration Atropine for Myopia Progression

DesignAtropine 0.05%, 0.025% or 0.01% vs placebo · 438 children aged 4–12 · Hong Kong
QuestionWhich low concentration works best?
FindingA clear dose response: 0.05% was the most effective, for both refraction and axial length, and was well tolerated.
Why it mattersShifted practice towards 0.05% in many centres.
Viva trap0.01% had little effect on axial length in LAMP.

CHAMP (2023)

Childhood Atropine for Myopia Progression

DesignAtropine 0.01% or 0.02% vs placebo · 576 children aged 3–17 · USA and Europe · 3 years
QuestionDoes low-dose atropine slow myopia in non-Asian children?
Finding0.01% slowed refractive progression and axial elongation versus placebo; 0.02% slowed axial elongation but not refraction.
Why it mattersSupported low-dose atropine outside East Asia.
Viva trapThe results with 0.02% were less consistent than with 0.01%.
