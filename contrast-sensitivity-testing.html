Contrast Sensitivity Testing: Pelli-Robson, Mars and Grating Charts, Normal Values and Uses

For: PG residents · Optometrists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Contrast sensitivity (CS) measures how faint a target can be and still be seen; acuity measures how small.
  • CS can be reduced while Snellen acuity is still normal, for example in cataract, optic neuritis, glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy.
  • The Pelli-Robson chart is the clinical standard: letter triplets that fade in steps of 0.15 log units, read at 1 m.
  • Healthy adults score about 1.8 log CS on Pelli-Robson, with lower values in young children and older adults.
  • Use the same chart, lighting and distance every time, and test each eye with the full correction.

Two patients with 6/6 vision can see very differently in fog, dusk or glare. Contrast sensitivity testing picks up this loss, explains many "my vision is bad but the chart is fine" complaints, and helps in decisions such as the timing of cataract surgery and fitness to drive.

The basics

  • Contrast is the difference in luminance between a target and its background. Contrast sensitivity is the reciprocal of the lowest contrast a person can detect, usually given in log units.
  • Spatial frequency is how fine the pattern is (cycles per degree). The contrast sensitivity function is the curve of sensitivity across spatial frequencies; it peaks at mid frequencies and falls at high frequencies, where acuity sets the limit.
  • Letter charts such as Pelli-Robson test one size of letter at falling contrast; grating tests measure several spatial frequencies.

How to do a Pelli-Robson test

  1. Light the chart evenly at the recommended luminance, with no glare or window reflections.
  2. Seat the patient at 1 m with the full distance correction, plus a reading addition suited to 1 m if presbyopic. Test each eye, then both.
  3. Ask the patient to read the letters from the top, guessing when unsure, and give plenty of time near threshold.
  4. Score the last triplet in which at least two of the three letters are correct, or score letter by letter (0.05 log units per letter), which is more precise. Use the same method every time.

Normal values

  • Healthy adults score about 1.8 log CS binocularly on Pelli-Robson; a Malaysian study of 200 healthy people found about 1.82 between 7 and 30 years.
  • Children aged 4–6 score lower (about 1.69 in the same study), and scores fall gradually with age because of media and retinal changes.
  • Rough guide for adults under 60: about 1.65 or more is normal; below about 1.5 is reduced; below 1.0 is a marked loss. Interpret against age.

Clinical uses

ConditionWhat contrast testing adds
CataractDocuments loss of function (with or without glare testing) when acuity is still good
Optic neuritis and multiple sclerosisContrast loss often persists after acuity recovers; low-contrast charts track it

Diabetic retinopathy and AMDExplains reading and mobility difficulty; helps plan low vision aids
After refractive surgery or with multifocal IOLsMeasures loss of contrast and night vision quality

More on functional assessment is in the low vision assessment guide and colour vision testing guide.

Where students slip

  • Testing at the wrong distance or in a dim room.
  • Stopping too early: patients need encouragement to guess near threshold.
  • Changing the scoring method between visits.
  • Using a faded or dirty chart, which lowers every score.
