Riddles in Ophthalmology

Last updated: October 2026

Here are some fun riddles related to ophthalmology. Try to guess the answer yourself before revealing it — click "Reveal answer" to check.

Riddle 1

Yesterday It was visible barely,

Today It become barley,

First It blushed,

Then it got flushed.

Which disease am I?
Reveal answer

Hordeolum (stye). "Hordeolum" is Latin for "little barley grain" — the riddle plays on "barely" becoming "barley." The blush-then-flush progression describes the classic evolution from mild tenderness to visible redness and swelling of the eyelid.


Riddle 2

This eye open and close, along with your mouth;

Gun is there in my name, leaves no doubt.

Which syndrome am I?
Reveal answer

Marcus Gunn jaw-winking syndrome. A congenital synkinesis where the ptotic eyelid elevates involuntarily when the jaw moves (chewing, sucking, or opening the mouth) — due to an aberrant connection between the trigeminal nerve (jaw movement) and the branch supplying the levator palpebrae superioris.


Riddle 3

I am known as grape,

As I am always under a drape.

Which system am I?
Reveal answer

The uveal tract (uvea). "Uvea" comes from the Latin uva, meaning grape — a reference to the deeply pigmented, grape-like appearance of the choroid when stripped from the sclera. It sits beneath the sclera, the eye's outer "drape."


Riddle 4

I make you squint at things far,

Yet up close, there is no bar.

Greek for “to squint” is how I got my name,

Bring it closer — that's my game.

Which refractive error am I?
Reveal answer

Myopia (near-sightedness). The word comes from the Greek myein (to close/squint) + ops (eye) — describing the characteristic squint distance-vision sufferers adopt. It's also called "brachymetropia," since a myope sees best by bringing objects closer.


Riddle 5

I have no true membrane, yet I bear a mirror's name,

Peel me back in surgery, retinal detachment is my claim.

What am I?
Reveal answer

The internal limiting membrane (ILM). Despite its name, it's not a true basement membrane in the classic sense but the footplate layer of the Müller cells. Peeling it during vitrectomy is central to modern macular hole and macular hole retinal detachment surgery.


Riddle 6

Named for a girl, or so it's told,

Look in my mirror, a doll to behold.

I open wide in darkened rooms,

And shrink away when brightness looms.

What am I?
Reveal answer

The pupil. "Pupil" comes from the Latin pupilla, literally "little doll" — the Romans named it after the tiny reflection of yourself you see when looking into someone else's eye. The Greeks made the same observation independently: their word kore meant both "girl" and "pupil of the eye."


Riddle 7

I accommodate, but I will not react,

An old disease is why, in fact.

They call me by a naughty nickname too,

Because, like her, I "accommodate, don't react" — true?

What pupil am I?
Reveal answer

The Argyll Robertson pupil. Classically caused by neurosyphilis, this pupil constricts normally with accommodation (near vision) but fails to react to light — light-near dissociation. It earned the informal nickname "prostitute's pupil" from exactly the mnemonic in this riddle. Named after Scottish ophthalmologist Douglas Argyll Robertson, who described it in 1869.


Riddle 8

A pilot's shattered windshield, sharp as it flew,

Lodged in an eye, yet caused no rue.

From wartime wreckage, an idea took flight —

Now millions see clearly, restored to sight.

What invention am I?
Reveal answer

The intraocular lens (IOL). Sir Harold Ridley noticed that WWII fighter pilots with acrylic (Perspex) windshield fragments embedded in their eyes tolerated the material remarkably well, with minimal inflammation. This observation led him to implant the first IOL on 29 November 1949 at St Thomas' Hospital, London — against fierce skepticism from the ophthalmic establishment of the time.


Riddle 9

Fifteen degrees out, I stand aside,

Never quite noticed, yet never denied.

Where nerve fibers gather and turn to leave,

I show nothing — and you'd never believe.

What am I?
Reveal answer

The blind spot (optic disc / physiologic scotoma). The blind spot lies about 15° temporal to fixation, slightly below the horizontal. At the point where the optic nerve exits the eye, there are no photoreceptors at all — so this spot genuinely cannot detect light. You don't normally notice it because the brain fills in the gap using information from the other eye and surrounding context, and each eye's blind spot falls in a different part of the visual field.


Riddle 10

In a town gone pale and milky white,

One small spot stays cherry-bright.

Not because I'm healthy, no —

There's nothing on top to dim my glow.

Which sign am I?
Reveal answer

The cherry-red spot (central retinal artery occlusion). When the retina becomes ischaemic, its inner layers swell and turn opaque white. The fovea has no inner retinal layers, so the red choroid keeps showing through and looks bright by contrast. The same sign appears in storage disorders such as Tay-Sachs disease.


Riddle 11

A golden-brown halo, I circle your view,

Copper's the metal that paints me through.

I sit in a membrane named after Descemet,

And the liver is where my story is set.

Which ring am I?
Reveal answer

The Kayser-Fleischer ring (Wilson disease). Copper is deposited in Descemet's membrane at the peripheral cornea, usually seen first superiorly and inferiorly. It is a key sign of Wilson disease, a disorder of copper metabolism that affects the liver and brain. Gonioscopy can reveal faint early rings.


Riddle 12

Copper again, but this time the lens:

Petals of colour, a flower it sends.

I turn to the sun, or so says my name —

A foreign body may share the blame.

Which cataract am I?
Reveal answer

The sunflower cataract (chalcosis or Wilson disease). Copper deposited under the anterior lens capsule forms a central disc with radiating petals. It is seen with retained intraocular copper foreign bodies (chalcosis) and in Wilson disease.


Riddle 13

Glittering colours in the lens of the eye,

Decorated like a festive tree on high.

Muscles that grip and refuse to let go —

A dystrophy's clue you should certainly know.

Which cataract am I?
Reveal answer

The Christmas tree cataract (myotonic dystrophy). Fine multicoloured polychromatic crystals in the lens cortex are classic for myotonic dystrophy, a condition in which muscles relax slowly after contraction (myotonia). Bilateral ptosis and frontal balding are other clues.


Riddle 14

A rash on the tip of the nose, so small,

Yet it warns of the eye's involvement after all.

The nasociliary nerve gives the clue away —

A sign named after a surgeon of old, they say.

Which sign am I?
Reveal answer

Hutchinson's sign (herpes zoster ophthalmicus). Vesicles on the tip, side or root of the nose mean the nasociliary branch of the ophthalmic nerve is involved. This branch also supplies the eye, so ocular involvement is much more likely. The sign is named after Sir Jonathan Hutchinson.


Riddle 15

A vertical spindle of pigment I show,

On the back of the cornea, row upon row.

From the back of the iris my particles came,

A German pathologist gave me my name.

Which sign am I?
Reveal answer

The Krukenberg spindle (pigment dispersion syndrome). Pigment rubbed off the posterior iris is carried by aqueous convection currents and settles on the central corneal endothelium as a vertical spindle. It is a hallmark of pigment dispersion syndrome.


Riddle 16

A blow to the eye, and the iris leaves

A ring of pigment that nobody grieves.

Stamped on the lens like a rubber seal,

The size of a pupil, that's my reveal.

Which ring am I?
Reveal answer

Vossius ring (blunt trauma). Blunt trauma presses the pupillary margin of the iris against the anterior lens capsule, imprinting a ring of iris pigment. Its diameter matches the pupil size at the moment of impact.


Riddle 17

Rows of letters, largest on top,

Read down the lines until you stop.

A Dutch eye doctor drew me with care,

In eighteen sixty-two I was there.

What am I?
Reveal answer

The Snellen chart. Herman Snellen, a Dutch ophthalmologist, introduced his letter chart in 1862. Its specially designed letters (optotypes) are built on a 5 × 5 grid, so the full letter subtends 5 minutes of arc and each limb 1 minute of arc at the test distance.


Riddle 18

Dots of colour hiding a number inside,

Some see it clearly, for others it hides.

From Japan I came in nineteen-seventeen,

Red-green confusion is the trouble I've seen.

What am I?
Reveal answer

The Ishihara plates. Shinobu Ishihara published his pseudoisochromatic plates in 1917. They screen mainly for congenital red-green colour vision defects and are less suitable for blue-yellow (tritan) defects.


Riddle 19

I'm a line on the lid, but you'd never see,

Unless you flip it and look at me.

A blinding disease left a scar so neat —

A horizontal mark where the tarsal folds meet.

Which sign am I?
Reveal answer

Arlt's line (trachoma). In cicatricial trachoma, a horizontal band of scarring forms on the upper tarsal conjunctiva, seen only on lid eversion. Its limbal counterpart is Herbert's pits — scarred limbal follicles.


Riddle 20

A flower that opens in the morning light,

My disc is funnel-shaped, wide and white.

Vessels radiate from my rim like spokes,

A congenital anomaly, not a stroke.

Which disc am I?
Reveal answer

Morning glory disc anomaly. An enlarged, funnel-shaped excavation of the optic disc with a central glial tuft, a surrounding pigmented ring, and straightened radial vessels. It can be associated with serous retinal detachment and with basal encephalocele.


Riddle 21

Iron paints a ring around a cone,

At the base of the bulge, I'm brown and alone.

Shine the cobalt blue light to find my trace,

Ectasia's footprint, I mark the place.

Which ring am I?
Reveal answer

The Fleischer ring (keratoconus). Iron is deposited in the basal epithelium around the base of the cone in keratoconus. It is easier to see with the cobalt blue filter of the slit lamp. Do not confuse it with the Kayser-Fleischer ring (copper, Descemet's membrane).


Riddle 22

Four little reflections I make in your eye,

Two from the cornea, from the lens two more nearby.

One of us flips, upside down we're seen,

A Czech physiologist named our sheen.

What are we?
Reveal answer

Purkinje images (Purkinje-Sanson images). Images I and II reflect from the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, III from the anterior lens surface, and IV from the posterior lens surface. Image IV is inverted because the posterior lens surface acts as a concave mirror. They are named after Jan Evangelista Purkinje.


Riddle 23

Close your eyes tight and I roll up high,

A protective reflex you can't deny.

If the lids can't shut, I still protect,

A palsy's patient I help to detect.

Which phenomenon am I?
Reveal answer

Bell's phenomenon. On forced eyelid closure, the eyes normally rotate upwards and outwards. In facial nerve palsy with incomplete lid closure (lagophthalmos), a good Bell's phenomenon helps protect the cornea; a poor one increases the risk of exposure keratopathy.


Riddle 24

A circular canal, hidden from sight,

In the angle I drain the aqueous right.

A German anatomist gave me his name,

Block me, and glaucoma's the game.

What am I?
Reveal answer

Schlemm's canal. A circumferential channel just outside the trabecular meshwork that collects aqueous humour and passes it to the collector channels and aqueous veins. It is named after Friedrich Schlemm, a German anatomist. Increased outflow resistance in this pathway is central to raised IOP in open-angle glaucoma, and many MIGS devices target it.
