MS and DNB Ophthalmology Theory: 40 Most-Asked Short Notes with Model Answer Outlines

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • 40 topics that come up again and again in MS and DNB ophthalmology theory papers, grouped by subspecialty.
  • Each outline gives the points an examiner looks for: definition, classification, features, investigations, treatment and recent evidence.
  • Turn each outline into a full answer with a diagram and a table where you can; practise against the clock.
  • Topics were chosen from recurring themes in theory papers; the outlines are original and not copied from any question paper.

Short notes carry most of the marks in MS and DNB ophthalmology theory papers. Here are 40 of the most-asked topics with model answer outlines you can expand into full answers. Tap a topic to open its outline.

How to write a short note that scores

  1. Start with a definition in one or two lines.
  2. Use headings: classification, aetiology, features, investigations, management, complications.
  3. Draw a labelled diagram wherever possible (angle structures, retinal layers, surgical steps).
  4. Add a table for comparisons (for example DSAEK versus DMEK).
  5. End with recent advances or a landmark trial; it shows depth.
  6. Keep time: about 6–8 minutes for a 5-mark note, 12–15 for a 10-mark question.

Jump to: Cornea and external eye, Lens and cataract, Glaucoma, Retina, Uvea, Neuro-ophthalmology, Paediatrics and strabismus, Oculoplasty and orbit, Community ophthalmology

Cornea and external eye

  1. Keratoconus
    • Definition: progressive, non-inflammatory thinning and conical protrusion of the cornea, usually inferocentral, starting in the teens.
    • Signs: scissoring reflex, Munson and Rizzuti signs, Vogt striae, Fleischer ring, acute hydrops.
    • Diagnosis: topography and tomography (inferior steepening, thinning, posterior elevation); grading by Amsler-Krumeich or ABCD.
    • Treatment: glasses, then rigid or scleral lenses; corneal cross-linking to halt progression; intrastromal ring segments; DALK or penetrating keratoplasty for scarring or intolerance.
    • Hydrops: hypertonic saline, cycloplegic, pressure control; intracameral air or gas in selected cases.
  2. Fungal keratitis
    • Risk: injury with plant matter, agriculture, steroid use, diabetes; Fusarium and Aspergillus (filamentous), Candida (yeast).
    • Signs: dry, raised, grey-white infiltrate with feathery margins, satellite lesions, endothelial plaque, thick hypopyon.
    • Diagnosis: scraping with KOH and calcofluor white, Sabouraud agar; confocal microscopy.
    • Treatment: natamycin 5% for filamentous (MUTT I), voriconazole for yeast or deep disease; no steroids; therapeutic keratoplasty if progressing.
  3. Acanthamoeba keratitis
    • Risk: contact lens wear with tap water exposure or poor hygiene; swimming in lenses.
    • Signs: pain out of proportion to signs, epitheliopathy, radial keratoneuritis, ring infiltrate.
    • Diagnosis: scraping with calcofluor white, culture on non-nutrient agar with E. coli overlay, PCR, confocal microscopy.
    • Treatment: biguanide (PHMB or chlorhexidine) with or without diamidine (propamidine) for months; manage pain; avoid early keratoplasty.
  4. Corneal cross-linking
    • Principle: riboflavin plus UVA (370 nm) creates covalent bonds between collagen fibrils, stiffening the cornea.
    • Indication: progressive keratoconus or ectasia after refractive surgery.
    • Protocols: Dresden epithelium-off (3 mW/cm² for 30 min), accelerated, epithelium-on.
    • Minimum thickness about 400 µm after epithelial removal to protect the endothelium; hypotonic riboflavin for thinner corneas.
    • Complications: infection, haze, sterile infiltrates, endothelial damage.
  5. Endothelial keratoplasty: DSAEK and DMEK
    • Replaces only diseased endothelium (Fuchs dystrophy, pseudophakic bullous keratopathy).
    • DSAEK: donor posterior stroma plus Descemet membrane and endothelium; easier, slower visual recovery.
    • DMEK: Descemet membrane and endothelium only; faster and better vision, lower rejection, harder to handle.
    • Complications: graft detachment (rebubbling), primary failure, rejection, pupillary block from air.
  6. Dry eye disease
    • Definition (TFOS DEWS II): a multifactorial disease of the tear film with loss of homeostasis, instability, hyperosmolarity, inflammation and neurosensory abnormality.
    • Types: aqueous-deficient and evaporative (meibomian gland dysfunction, the commonest).
    • Tests: OSDI, tear break-up time, Schirmer, staining with fluorescein and lissamine green, meibography, osmolarity.
    • Treatment in steps: education and lid hygiene, preservative-free tears, anti-inflammatory drops (ciclosporin, short steroid), punctal plugs, autologous serum, scleral lenses.

Lens and cataract

  1. Manual small-incision cataract surgery versus phacoemulsification
    • MSICS: scleral tunnel, manual nucleus delivery; low cost, suits dense cataracts, sutureless; more astigmatism.
    • Phaco: 2–2.8 mm clear corneal incision, ultrasound nucleus removal; faster recovery, less astigmatism; costly equipment.
    • Outcomes: similar uncorrected vision at a few months in trials from India and Nepal.
    • Choice depends on cataract density, resources, surgeon skill and patient expectations.
  2. Posterior capsule rupture
    • Signs: sudden deepening of the chamber, nucleus dropping back, vitreous in the wound, pupil snap.
    • Management: stop phaco, keep the chamber formed with dispersive viscoelastic, remove remaining nucleus safely, anterior vitrectomy (bimanual, with triamcinolone staining).
    • IOL: sulcus IOL with power reduced by about 0.5–1 D, optic capture if possible; AC or scleral-fixated IOL if no support.
    • Refer dropped nucleus for vitrectomy; watch for CME, retinal detachment and raised pressure.
  3. Post-operative endophthalmitis
    • Acute (within 6 weeks): pain, reduced vision, hypopyon, vitritis; usually coagulase-negative staphylococci.
    • Chronic: Propionibacterium (Cutibacterium) acnes, white capsular plaque.
    • Management: vitreous tap and intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg plus ceftazidime 2.25 mg; vitrectomy if vision is perception of light (EVS).
    • Prevention: povidone-iodine 5%, intracameral antibiotic (ESCRS study), treat lid and lacrimal disease.
  4. IOL power calculation
    • Needs keratometry and axial length; optical biometry is preferred, ultrasound for dense cataract.
    • Generations: SRK (regression), SRK/T, Holladay 1, Hoffer Q (third), Haigis, Barrett Universal II, Kane, Hill-RBF (newer).
    • Short eyes: Hoffer Q, Haigis or newer formulas; long eyes: SRK/T or newer; Wang-Koch axial length adjustment.
    • After refractive surgery: Barrett True-K, Haigis-L, ASCRS calculator, total keratometry.
  5. Paediatric cataract
    • Causes: idiopathic, hereditary, intrauterine infection (rubella), metabolic (galactosaemia), trauma, syndromes.
    • Timing: dense unilateral by 6 weeks, bilateral by 8–10 weeks to prevent deprivation amblyopia.
    • Surgery: lensectomy with primary posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy; IOL usually after age 1–2 (IATS).
    • After: aphakic contact lens or glasses, amblyopia treatment, lifelong glaucoma checks.

Glaucoma

  1. Optic disc evaluation in glaucoma
    • Disc size first (small discs have small cups).
    • Vertical cup-disc ratio, rim shape (ISNT rule), focal notching, disc haemorrhage.
    • Vessels: bayoneting, baring of circumlinear vessels; peripapillary atrophy (beta zone).
    • Nerve fibre layer defects in red-free light; OCT RNFL and ganglion cell analysis.
  2. Classification of primary angle closure
    • ISGEO: primary angle closure suspect (iridotrabecular contact in 180° or more), primary angle closure (raised pressure or PAS, normal disc), primary angle closure glaucoma (with optic neuropathy).
    • Mechanisms: pupillary block, plateau iris, lens-related, posterior pushing.
    • Treatment: laser iridotomy; lens extraction (EAGLE) in PAC and PACG; ZAP showed iridotomy gives small benefit in suspects.
  3. Target intraocular pressure
    • The pressure range at which progression is expected to be slow enough to keep the patient seeing for life.
    • Set from baseline untreated pressure, stage of damage, age, life expectancy and risk factors.
    • Typical: 20–30% reduction in early disease; lower in advanced disease (EMGT, AGIS).
    • Reassess with fields and OCT; adjust if progression continues.
  4. Trabeculectomy and antimetabolites
    • Guarded fistula under a partial-thickness scleral flap to the subconjunctival space.
    • Mitomycin C (0.2–0.4 mg/ml) or 5-fluorouracil to prevent scarring.
    • Early complications: hypotony, shallow chamber, bleb leak, choroidal effusion, aqueous misdirection.
    • Late: thin bleb, blebitis and endophthalmitis, cataract, bleb failure; needling with antimetabolite.
  5. Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery
    • Ab interno, small incision, high safety, modest pressure lowering; often with cataract surgery.
    • Trabecular bypass (iStent, Hydrus), canal procedures (goniotomy with Kahook blade, canaloplasty, GATT), suprachoroidal devices, subconjunctival stents (XEN, PreserFlo).
    • Best for mild to moderate open-angle glaucoma to reduce drops.
  6. Primary congenital glaucoma
    • Triad: watering, light sensitivity, blepharospasm; buphthalmos, corneal enlargement and haze, Haab striae.
    • Examination under anaesthesia: corneal diameter, pressure, axial length, disc.
    • Surgery is first-line: goniotomy or trabeculotomy, or combined trabeculotomy–trabeculectomy (common in India).
    • Lifelong follow-up for pressure, amblyopia and refractive error.
  7. Neovascular glaucoma
    • Causes: ischaemic CRVO, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, ocular ischaemic syndrome.
    • Stages: rubeosis, open-angle, closed-angle (synechial).
    • Treatment: anti-VEGF injection, panretinal photocoagulation, pressure-lowering drugs, tube surgery or cyclophotocoagulation.

Retina

  1. Anti-VEGF agents
    • Drugs: ranibizumab, aflibercept (2 mg and 8 mg), bevacizumab (off-label), brolucizumab, faricimab (also blocks angiopoietin-2).
    • Uses: wet AMD, diabetic macular oedema, macular oedema from vein occlusion, myopic CNV, ROP.
    • Regimens: monthly loading then treat-and-extend or as needed.
    • Risks: endophthalmitis, raised pressure, retinal tear; intraocular inflammation and vasculitis with brolucizumab.
  2. Classification of diabetic retinopathy
    • Non-proliferative: mild, moderate, severe (4-2-1 rule), very severe.
    • Proliferative: new vessels at the disc or elsewhere; high-risk features (DRS).
    • Diabetic macular oedema: centre-involving or not, by OCT.
    • Screening and follow-up intervals by grade.
  3. Management of diabetic macular oedema
    • Control sugar, blood pressure and lipids.
    • Centre-involving with reduced vision: anti-VEGF first line (DRCR Protocol T: aflibercept better when vision 6/18 or worse).
    • Good vision: observation is reasonable (Protocol V).
    • Steroid implants for pseudophakic or poor responders; focal laser for non-centre-involving.
  4. Retinopathy of prematurity
    • Screening: birth weight under 2000 g or gestational age under 34 weeks in India, or sicker babies.
    • ICROP3: zones I–III, stages 1–5, plus disease, A-ROP, posterior zone II.
    • Treatment: type 1 ROP (ETROP) with laser or anti-VEGF.
    • Follow-up for recurrence after anti-VEGF, myopia, squint and amblyopia.
  5. Central retinal vein occlusion
    • Risk: age, hypertension, diabetes, glaucoma, hyperviscosity.
    • Ischaemic versus non-ischaemic: vision, RAPD, fields, angiography (capillary non-perfusion).
    • Treatment: anti-VEGF for macular oedema; PRP for neovascularisation; monitor for neovascular glaucoma.
  6. Optical coherence tomography
    • Principle: low-coherence interferometry, like ultrasound with light.
    • Normal layers from vitreous to choroid; foveal pit.
    • Uses: macular oedema, AMD, macular hole and epiretinal membrane, glaucoma RNFL, OCT angiography.
  7. Macular hole
    • Gass stages 1–4; International Vitreomacular Traction Study classification by size and traction.
    • Diagnosis: OCT; Watzke-Allen sign.
    • Treatment: vitrectomy, internal limiting membrane peel, gas and posturing; inverted flap for large holes; ocriplasmin historically for small holes with traction.

Uvea

  1. Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease
    • Autoimmune disease against melanocytes; more common in pigmented races.
    • Phases: prodromal (headache, meningism), acute uveitic (bilateral exudative detachments), convalescent (sunset glow fundus, vitiligo, poliosis), chronic recurrent.
    • Investigations: OCT, angiography (pinpoint leaks), ICGA, B-scan, CSF pleocytosis.
    • Treatment: high-dose steroids early with steroid-sparing immunosuppression.
  2. Ocular toxoplasmosis
    • Commonest cause of posterior uveitis in many regions.
    • Signs: focal retinochoroiditis next to an old scar, dense vitritis ("headlight in the fog").
    • Treatment if sight-threatening: classic triple therapy, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or intravitreal clindamycin; steroids after antimicrobials.
  3. Tubercular uveitis
    • Presentations: serpiginous-like choroiditis, choroidal tubercles, granulomatous anterior uveitis, retinal vasculitis.
    • Diagnosis: Mantoux, IGRA, chest imaging, ocular PCR; COTS consensus guidance.
    • Treatment: anti-tubercular therapy with steroids under cover.

Neuro-ophthalmology

  1. Papilloedema and idiopathic intracranial hypertension
    • Papilloedema means disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure; Frisén grading.
    • IIH: obese women of childbearing age; headache, transient visual obscurations, sixth nerve palsy.
    • Investigations: MRI and MR venography, then lumbar puncture opening pressure.
    • Treatment: weight loss, acetazolamide (IIHTT); optic nerve sheath fenestration or shunting for vision loss.
  2. Optic neuritis
    • Young women; painful vision loss, RAPD, colour desaturation, central scotoma.
    • ONTT: IV methylprednisolone speeds recovery but not final vision; oral steroids alone increase recurrence.
    • MRI for multiple sclerosis risk; test for MOG and AQP4 antibodies in atypical cases.
  3. Third nerve palsy
    • Signs: ptosis, eye down and out, with or without dilated pupil.
    • Pupil-involving palsy suggests compression (posterior communicating artery aneurysm): urgent imaging.
    • Pupil-sparing complete palsy in a vasculopath often ischaemic, but imaging is now widely recommended.
  4. Visual pathway lesions and field defects
    • Optic nerve: central scotoma; chiasm: bitemporal hemianopia.
    • Optic tract: incongruous homonymous hemianopia; temporal lobe: superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky").
    • Parietal lobe: inferior quadrantanopia; occipital: congruous homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.

Paediatrics and strabismus

  1. Amblyopia
    • Types: strabismic, anisometropic, deprivation, isoametropic.
    • Treatment: correct refractive error first (glasses alone can improve vision); then patching or atropine (PEDIG: 2 hours patching as good as 6 for moderate amblyopia).
    • Binocular digital treatments; older children can still respond.
  2. Retinoblastoma
    • Commonest primary intraocular malignancy of childhood; RB1 gene.
    • Presentation: leukocoria, squint, red eye.
    • Classification: International Classification of Retinoblastoma (A–E).
    • Treatment: intravenous or intra-arterial chemotherapy, focal therapy, intravitreal chemotherapy, enucleation; genetic counselling.

Oculoplasty and orbit

  1. Thyroid eye disease
    • Autoimmune; smoking is the main modifiable risk.
    • Signs: lid retraction, proptosis, restrictive myopathy, compressive optic neuropathy.
    • Activity (CAS) and severity (EUGOGO) guide treatment.
    • Treatment: IV methylprednisolone, mycophenolate, teprotumumab, radiotherapy; decompression for optic neuropathy.
  2. Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction
    • Membrane at the valve of Hasner; watering and discharge from early infancy.
    • Most resolve by the first birthday: lacrimal sac massage.
    • Probing at about 12–18 months if persistent; intubation or balloon dacryoplasty if probing fails.
  3. Basal cell carcinoma of the eyelid
    • Commonest malignant eyelid tumour; lower lid and medial canthus.
    • Types: nodular, morphoeic (infiltrative).
    • Treatment: excision with frozen section or Mohs surgery, then reconstruction; vismodegib for advanced disease.

Community ophthalmology

  1. National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment
    • Aims to reduce the prevalence of blindness in India; the programme set a target of 0.25% by 2025.
    • Activities: free cataract surgery, school eye screening and free glasses, diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma screening, eye banking, training.
    • Leading causes of blindness in India: cataract, then refractive error, corneal blindness and glaucoma.
  2. Eye banking
    • Donation within 6–8 hours of death; contraindications include unknown cause of death, rabies, septicaemia, HIV and hepatitis.
    • Storage: McCarey–Kaufman (up to 4 days), Optisol-GS (up to 14 days), organ culture (up to 4 weeks).
    • Evaluation: slit lamp and specular microscopy (endothelial cell count over 2000 cells/mm² for optical grafts).
  3. Low vision aids
    • Low vision: best corrected vision below 6/18 to perception of light, or field under 10° from fixation, in the better eye after treatment.
    • Optical aids: magnifiers, telescopes, prisms; non-optical: lighting, contrast, large print.
    • Electronic aids and rehabilitation services.

Where candidates lose marks

  • Writing everything known about a topic instead of answering the question asked.
  • No diagram or table in a 10-mark question.
  • Old classifications only (for example ROP without ICROP3, DR without OCT-based DME).
  • Running out of time on the first question.
