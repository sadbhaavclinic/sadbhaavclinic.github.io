Eye Embryology Made Easy: Four Rules, Timeline and Anomalies

Last updated: October 2026

Eye embryology looks like a list of facts to cram, but most of it follows four simple rules. Learn the rules and the handful of exceptions, then hang the timeline and the birth defects on them.

The four rules

1. Surface ectoderm: "what starts on the outside"The skin of the face folds in to make the lens, the corneal and conjunctival epithelium, the lid skin, lashes and glands (meibomian, Zeis, Moll) and the lacrimal gland and drainage system.
2. Neuroectoderm: "what grows out of the brain"The forebrain bulges out as the optic vesicle, so it forms everything neural and its two-layered lining: retina and RPE, optic nerve axons and glia, the epithelium of the iris and ciliary body, and, the famous exception, the iris sphincter and dilator muscles.
3. Neural crest: "the connective tissue of the front of the eye"Cells migrating from the edges of the neural tube build the scaffolding: corneal stroma and endothelium, trabecular meshwork, iris stroma, ciliary muscle, most of the sclera, choroidal stroma, melanocytes, orbital bones and the eyelid tarsus.
4. Mesoderm: "skeletal muscle and blood vessels"Extraocular muscles, orbicularis, the endothelium of all blood vessels (including the hyaloid artery and choriocapillaris), the temporal part of the sclera and the dura of the optic nerve.

The exceptions worth memorising

  • Iris sphincter and dilator: the only muscles in the body made from neuroectoderm. Everything else muscular is mesoderm (striated) or neural crest (ciliary muscle).
  • RPE is neuroectoderm (outer layer of the optic cup), not mesoderm, even though it is pigmented like the choroid.
  • Melanocytes of the uvea are neural crest, while the iris and RPE pigment epithelium are neuroectoderm: this explains why albinism and neural crest disorders affect pigment differently.
  • Sclera is mostly neural crest, but its temporal part is mesoderm.
  • Optic nerve meninges: pia and arachnoid from neural crest, dura from mesoderm.

Memory hook: "Lids, Lens, Lacrimal and the Lining epithelium" are the four Ls of surface ectoderm. For neural crest, think "everything you see through and around in the anterior segment": cornea stroma and endothelium, angle, iris stroma, ciliary muscle.

How the eye forms: the story in six steps

  1. Optic sulcus (day 22, week 3): grooves appear in the forebrain neural folds.
  2. Optic vesicle (week 4): the grooves balloon out towards the surface ectoderm, connected to the brain by the optic stalk (future optic nerve).
  3. Lens placode and vesicle (weeks 4–5): the vesicle induces the surface ectoderm to thicken into the lens placode, which sinks in and pinches off as the lens vesicle (about day 33).
  4. Optic cup (weeks 4–5): the vesicle folds in on itself, making a two-layered cup: inner layer → neural retina, outer layer → RPE. The fold leaves a gap underneath, the optic (choroidal) fissure, through which the hyaloid artery enters.
  5. Fissure closure (weeks 5–7): the fissure zips up from the middle, outwards in both directions. Failure leaves a coloboma, always inferonasal.
  6. Neural crest waves (weeks 6–8): three waves migrate between lens and surface ectoderm: first the corneal endothelium and trabecular meshwork, second the corneal stroma, third the iris stroma.

Timings are approximate and vary slightly between textbooks; learn the order and the rough week rather than exact days.

The lens and vitreous, simply

  • Lens: the posterior cells of the lens vesicle elongate first (primary fibres → embryonic nucleus). After that, the equatorial epithelium adds secondary fibres for life, so the oldest fibres are always in the centre. The fibres meet at sutures: an upright Y in front and an inverted Y behind.
  • Vitreous in three generations: primary (vascular, the hyaloid system), secondary (the definitive avascular gel, mainly from the retina) and tertiary (the zonules, from the ciliary epithelium).
