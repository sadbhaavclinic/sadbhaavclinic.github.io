Certifying Eyesight for Driving: What to Check, How to Record It, and Standards in India, the UK, EU, USA, Canada and Australia

For: PG residents · Practising ophthalmologists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Check acuity with both eyes and each eye, with and without glasses; the field of vision; colour vision; diplopia; and glare or night vision problems.
  • In India, Form 1A is signed by a registered medical practitioner; the rules ask about reading a number plate at 25 m, colour vision and night blindness, with no numeric acuity written in.
  • Since 2020, mild and moderate colour vision deficiency is allowed in India; grade the severity rather than writing "colour blind".
  • Abroad, most private-driving standards are about 6/12 with both eyes and a horizontal field of about 110 to 120 degrees; commercial standards are stricter.
  • Only certify what you have examined, record the conditions (glasses, daylight only) and tell patients who fall short not to drive.
Driving and job vision pages: vision standards for licences and jobs (for patients) · how to examine and certify (for doctors) · certificate generator.

Driving certificates are often signed in a minute at the end of a busy OPD. Yet the certificate is a medico-legal document, and the eye doctor is often the only person who can tell whether a patient with glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, diplopia or advanced cataract is safe on the road. This page gives a practical routine, how to write the certificate, and the standards your patients will meet if they drive abroad.

The examination routine

Seven checks for a driving certificate

  1. History: eye diseases (glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, retinitis pigmentosa, cataract, previous stroke or optic neuropathy), double vision, night driving difficulty, glare, recent eye surgery, and medicines that blur vision. Ask what vehicle they drive and whether it is for work.
  2. Distance acuity: each eye and both eyes together, unaided and with current glasses, in good light. Refract if the acuity is borderline.
  3. Number plate equivalent: the Indian forms ask about reading a number plate at 25 metres. Use a Snellen or logMAR result to support your answer and record it.
  4. Visual field: confrontation for every applicant; a formal binocular field (for example Esterman) when there is any disease that affects the field, or when the patient has one eye.
  5. Colour vision: Ishihara plates; if they fail, grade the severity (mild, moderate, severe) with a further test or a recognised grading of the plates.
  6. Diplopia and ocular alignment: cover test and motility; ask about double vision in primary gaze and when looking to the sides.
  7. Glare and dim light: history of night blindness, glare from headlights; consider contrast sensitivity or a glare test in cataract and corneal disease.

Writing the certificate

  • Record each finding, not just "fit". For example: "VA 6/9 RE, 6/6 LE, 6/6 both eyes with glasses; full fields to confrontation; Ishihara 17/17; no diplopia."
  • State conditions clearly: "fit to drive with corrective lenses", "daylight driving only" where the local rules allow conditional licences, or "review in one year" for progressive disease.
  • Write your full name, qualification, registration number, date and clinic stamp. Never sign a blank or partly completed form, and never certify a patient you have not examined.
  • Keep a copy or a record in the case notes.
  • If the patient does not meet the standard, explain it, write it down, and advise them not to drive until reassessed. In India there is no legal duty for the doctor to inform the transport office, but in some countries (for example the UK) the driver must notify the licensing authority and the doctor may need to if the patient continues to drive unsafely.

India in brief

Under the Central Motor Vehicles Rules, 1989, applicants complete Form 1 (self-declaration) and, for transport vehicles and for applicants aged 40 and over in most states, Form 1A (medical certificate by a registered medical practitioner with an MBBS or higher qualification). The vision questions ask whether the applicant can read a vehicle number plate at 25 metres in good daylight (with glasses if worn), whether they can distinguish colours, and whether they have night blindness. Since GSR 401(E) of June 2020, mild and moderate colour vision deficiency is permitted. The details, including railway and defence standards, are in vision standards for driving and jobs in India.

Standards in other countries

Patients who move abroad, or who drive on an International Driving Permit, will meet the local rules. These are the main standards for private cars (Group 1) and commercial vehicles such as lorries and buses (Group 2). Always check the official source, as rules change.

Pearls

  • Always record both-eyes acuity: it is the one most driving standards use.
  • Acuity alone is not enough in glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa; test the field.
  • Grade colour vision deficiency; Indian rules now accept mild and moderate deficiency.
  • Write down any advice not to drive, and give it to the patient in writing.
  • Patients going abroad can use your record to support their application; give them a typed summary.

Common mistakes

  • Signing a form without testing the field in a patient with advanced glaucoma.
  • Writing "colour blind, unfit" for a mild deficiency.
  • Certifying the patient's acuity from an old prescription instead of examining them.
  • Forgetting that the patient may need glasses to meet the standard, and not writing that condition.
