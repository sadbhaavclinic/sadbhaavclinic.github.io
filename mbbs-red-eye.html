Red Eye for MBBS Students: Conjunctivitis, Keratitis, Iritis, Acute Glaucoma and When to Refer

For: MBBS students

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Most red eyes are conjunctivitis, but the dangerous ones are keratitis, iridocyclitis and acute angle-closure glaucoma.
  • Red flags: reduced vision, pain (not just grittiness), photophobia, ciliary congestion, corneal opacity, abnormal pupil and raised pressure.
  • Conjunctival congestion is maximal in the fornices; ciliary congestion is a violet ring around the cornea and points to deeper disease.
  • Pupil: normal in conjunctivitis, small in iridocyclitis, mid-dilated and fixed in acute angle closure.
  • Never give topical steroids for an undiagnosed red eye.

Red eye is the commonest eye complaint in general practice, and one of the commonest exam questions. The skill is not naming every cause but separating the harmless red eye from the one that threatens sight, using a few simple observations.

MBBS syllabus (NMC CBME): OP3.1, OP3.2, OP3.3, OP4.1, OP5.1, OP5.2, OP6.1, OP6.7 · see the full syllabus map

Never prescribe steroid eye drops for an undiagnosed red eye. They make herpes simplex and fungal keratitis much worse and can cause glaucoma and cataract.

When to refer urgently

  • Reduced vision, severe pain, photophobia
  • Corneal opacity or staining, hypopyon
  • Abnormal or non-reacting pupil
  • Raised pressure (hard eye)
  • Chemical injury or penetrating trauma
  • Red eye in a contact lens wearer (risk of Acanthamoeba and Pseudomonas)
  • Neonate with sticky red eyes (ophthalmia neonatorum)

Exam pearls

  • Normal vision, normal pupil and clear cornea: almost always conjunctivitis.
  • Ciliary congestion means the problem is deeper than the conjunctiva.
  • Mid-dilated fixed pupil with a hard eye: acute angle closure.
  • Contact lens wearer with a red eye: treat as keratitis until proved otherwise.

Common mistakes

  • Using steroid-antibiotic combinations for every red eye.
  • Dilating an eye with acute angle closure.
  • Missing a dendritic ulcer because fluorescein was not used.
  • Treating scleritis as conjunctivitis.
