Direct Ophthalmoscopy for Medical Students: Technique, How to Present and a Practice Gallery of Fundus Drawings

For: PG residents · Optometrists

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • The GMC expects every UK graduate to perform direct ophthalmoscopy: red reflex, anterior segment and fundus.
  • Dim the room, dilate the pupil when allowed, use your right eye for the patient's right eye, and get close.
  • Find a vessel, follow it to the disc, then look at the disc, the four vessel arcades and finally the macula.
  • Describe the disc in order: colour, cup, contour (margins), then vessels and background.
  • Practise on the drawings below until you can name each picture in a few seconds.

Ophthalmoscopy is the eye skill students fear most. This page gives a step-by-step technique and a practice gallery of original fundus drawings, from normal to the classic exam pictures.

Technique step by step

  1. Introduce yourself, explain, gain consent; ask about driving if you will dilate.
  2. Dim the lights; ask the patient to look at a distant target.
  3. Set the ophthalmoscope to 0 and use the same eye as the patient's eye being examined (right for right).
  4. From about an arm's length, check the red reflex in each eye.
  5. Move in at about 15° temporal to the line of sight; turn the dial to bring the retina into focus.
  6. Find a vessel and follow it to the disc (vessel branches point back towards the disc).
  7. Describe the disc: colour, cup-to-disc ratio, contour of the margins.
  8. Follow the four arcades: arteries narrower and lighter than veins; look for nipping, haemorrhages, exudates.
  9. Ask the patient to look at the light to see the macula last (it is bright and uncomfortable).
  10. Thank the patient; warn about blur and glare after dilation.

How to present what you see

"The red reflex is present in both eyes. In the right fundus the optic disc is pink with a cup-to-disc ratio of about 0.3 and clear margins. The vessels are of normal calibre. There are scattered dot and blot haemorrhages and hard exudates around the macula, with no new vessels. This is consistent with background diabetic retinopathy with possible maculopathy."

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong eye, so your noses meet.
  • Standing too far away.
  • Looking for the macula first (the pupil constricts).
  • Describing the fundus without the disc first.
