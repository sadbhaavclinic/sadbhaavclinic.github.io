Ophthalmic Nursing Viva and OSCE: Skill Checklists and Questions

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Practical exams check that you can do common tasks safely, step by step, while talking to the patient.
  • 9 skill checklists below, written as marking points.
  • 25 viva questions with short model answers.
  • Practise aloud with a colleague acting as the patient.

Skill stations (OSCE checklists)

Each list is a marking checklist: introduce yourself, identify the patient, explain, gain consent and wash hands at the start of every station, and document at the end.

Instilling eye drops and teaching the patient

  1. Check prescription: patient, eye, drug, strength, time; check expiry and opening date.
  2. Wash hands; position patient with head tilted back.
  3. Pull lower lid down, ask patient to look up, instil one drop into the pocket without touching the eye.
  4. Close eye gently, punctal occlusion 1–2 minutes; wipe excess with a separate tissue.
  5. Wait 5 minutes before a second drug; ointment last.
  6. Teach-back: patient demonstrates; give written chart.
  7. Document drug, eye and time.

Measuring distance visual acuity

  1. Chart at correct distance and lighting; patient wearing distance glasses.
  2. Occlude left eye with an occluder; test right eye first.
  3. Read from the top; record the smallest line with most letters correct (e.g. 6/9 −1).
  4. Repeat for the left eye; then with pinhole if worse than 6/6.
  5. If top letter not seen: reduce distance (3/60, 1/60), then CF, HM, PL with projection.
  6. Document each eye, with or without correction, pinhole result.

Irrigating a chemical injury

  1. Recognise emergency; start immediately; call for help.
  2. Topical anaesthetic; protect clothing; patient lying with head turned to the injured side.
  3. Use normal saline or Ringer’s lactate through an infusion set; hold lids apart (retractor or speculum if needed).
  4. Ask the patient to look in all directions; evert lids and sweep fornices with moist cotton bud.
  5. Continue at least 15–30 minutes; check pH 5 minutes after stopping; continue until 7.0–7.5.
  6. Measure vision; inform doctor; document chemical, duration, volume and pH.

Applying an eye pad and shield

  1. Confirm the order and that the eye is not infected or a chemical injury awaiting irrigation.
  2. Ask the patient to close both eyes.
  3. Place a folded or single pad over the closed lid; tape diagonally from forehead to cheek without pressure on the globe.
  4. For a shield, place on the bony orbital rim so it does not touch the eye; tape securely.
  5. Check comfort and that the lid stays closed; explain how long to keep it on.

Pre-operative check before cataract surgery

  1. Two identifiers and wristband; consent signed, correct eye and procedure.
  2. Eye marked; allergies recorded; anticoagulants and INR as per protocol.
  3. Blood pressure, pulse, blood sugar for diabetics; fasting status per protocol.
  4. Biometry and IOL power and model checked against the plan.
  5. Pupil dilated as ordered; lids clean; no active infection.
  6. Explain the experience of surgery; empty bladder; hand over using the checklist.

Assisting with intravitreal injection

  1. Confirm identity, eye, drug, dose, consent; clean room; masks.
  2. Topical anaesthetic; povidone-iodine 5% to eye surface and 10% to lids and lashes; allow contact time.
  3. Sterile gloves for the injector; speculum; drug prepared aseptically and checked.
  4. After injection: check perfusion (counting fingers or hand movements) and IOP if required.
  5. Give written warning signs (pain, falling vision, redness, discharge) and emergency number; document batch number.

Discharge teaching after eye surgery

  1. Involve the carer; use the patient’s language; check vision for reading the chart.
  2. Explain each drop, frequency and duration; demonstrate and teach-back.
  3. Shield at night, hygiene, activity limits, no rubbing.
  4. Warning signs and how to get help day and night.
  5. Follow-up appointment; give leaflet; document teaching and understanding.

Hand hygiene and cleaning the tonometer

  1. Hand rub for 20–30 seconds or wash with soap and water for 40–60 seconds covering all surfaces.
  2. Clean the slit-lamp chin rest and forehead bar between patients.
  3. Disinfect or replace the tonometer prism as per the manufacturer’s instructions; rinse and dry if required.
  4. Record cleaning in the log where required.

Guiding a patient with severe vision loss

  1. Introduce yourself and ask how they prefer to be guided.
  2. Offer your arm; patient holds just above the elbow; walk half a step ahead.
  3. Describe steps, slopes, doors and narrow spaces; place their hand on the chair back before sitting.
  4. Tell them when you are leaving.

Viva questions

What does “6/18” mean?

At 6 metres the patient reads what a normal eye reads at 18 metres.

Why is visual acuity tested before any examination?

Lights, drops and touching the eye can temporarily reduce vision; acuity is also the baseline medico-legal record.

Name three causes of a sudden painless loss of vision.

Retinal artery occlusion, retinal vein occlusion, vitreous haemorrhage (also retinal detachment, ischaemic optic neuropathy).

Name three causes of a painful red eye with reduced vision.

Acute angle closure, keratitis, anterior uveitis (also endophthalmitis, scleritis).

What is the difference between a mydriatic and a cycloplegic?

A mydriatic dilates the pupil; a cycloplegic also paralyses accommodation (ciliary muscle). Tropicamide and cyclopentolate do both; phenylephrine only dilates.

What systemic side effects can timolol drops cause?

Slow pulse, heart block, bronchospasm, tiredness, masking of hypoglycaemia.

How do you reduce systemic absorption of eye drops?

Punctal occlusion and closing the eyes for 1–2 minutes; one drop only.

What are the signs of endophthalmitis?

Increasing pain, falling vision, redness, lid swelling, hypopyon, usually 2–7 days after surgery or injection.

What is TASS?

Toxic anterior segment syndrome: sterile inflammation within 12–24 hours of surgery from contaminants or wrong drug concentrations.

What precautions apply to a patient with intraocular gas?

No flying or high altitude, no nitrous oxide anaesthesia, posture as instructed, wear a gas alert band.

How is the operative eye identified before surgery?

Consent, notes and biometry checked; eye marked above the brow; confirmed at sign-in and time-out.

What is the oculocardiac reflex and how is it managed?

Bradycardia from traction or pressure on the eye; stop traction, and give atropine if needed.

Which colour bags and containers are used for biomedical waste in India?

Yellow (infectious, anatomical, soiled), red (contaminated recyclable plastics), white (sharps, puncture-proof), blue (glassware and metal implants).

What is the Bowie-Dick test?

A daily test of air removal and steam penetration in pre-vacuum autoclaves.

How should IV mannitol be given and monitored?

At the prescribed dose and rate (often over 30–60 minutes), checking heart and kidney status, urine output, BP, and for confusion or retention.

What is a relative afferent pupil defect?

On the swinging torch test, the pupil of the eye with optic nerve or extensive retinal damage dilates when the light moves to it.

What advice do you give a patient after pupil dilation?

Blur and glare for several hours; do not drive until clear; sunglasses help; return if pain and halos occur.

Why should anaesthetic drops never be given for home use?

Repeated use delays healing and can cause severe corneal damage.

What is the first aid for a suspected open globe?

Rigid shield without pressure, nil by mouth, antiemetic, analgesia, tetanus check, urgent surgeon; no drops or tonometry.

Which patients need yearly diabetic eye screening?

Everyone with diabetes (type 2 from diagnosis; type 1 within 5 years of diagnosis), more often in pregnancy or when retinopathy is present.

What is Charles Bonnet syndrome?

Visual hallucinations in people with vision loss, with insight that they are not real; reassurance is the main treatment.

What are the elements of informed consent?

Capacity, information (nature, benefits, risks, alternatives), understanding, voluntariness, documentation.

Which drugs must be recorded before cataract surgery?

Anticoagulants and antiplatelets, tamsulosin and other alpha-blockers, diabetic medicines, steroids, and allergies.

How is an intracameral antibiotic prepared safely?

Using the unit’s validated dilution protocol, aseptically, checked by a second person, labelled, and discarded after use.

What is the role of the circulating nurse?

Outside the sterile field: positioning, checklists, opening sterile items, IOL check, monitoring, equipment and documentation.
