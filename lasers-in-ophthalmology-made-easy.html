Lasers in Ophthalmology Made Easy: Time, Colour and Use

Last updated: October 2026

Ophthalmic lasers seem like a zoo of names and wavelengths. Two questions sort them all: how long and how hard does the laser hit (which decides what it does to tissue) and which colour it is (which decides what absorbs it).

Three rules

1. Time and intensity decide the effectThe same energy delivered slowly heats (photocoagulation); delivered in nanoseconds or femtoseconds it tears tissue with plasma (photodisruption). Ultraviolet breaks molecular bonds (photoablation); low-power light plus a drug triggers chemistry (photochemical).
2. The colour picks the targetMelanin (RPE, choroid, trabecular pigment) absorbs green to near-infrared. Haemoglobin absorbs green and yellow, not red. Xanthophyll at the macula absorbs blue, so blue light is avoided there. Water absorbs infrared; corneal protein absorbs far ultraviolet (193 nm).
3. The name is the lasing mediumArgon and krypton are gases, Nd:YAG is a crystal (1064 nm; halved to 532 nm by frequency doubling), diode lasers are semiconductors (810 nm), excimer is argon fluoride (193 nm). The femtosecond laser is a solid-state laser near 1053 nm.

Common procedures, simply

ProcedureLaserKey settings and points
Panretinal photocoagulation532 nm or 577 nm (or pattern lasers)Moderate grey-white burns one burn-width apart, sparing the macula and disc; destroys ischaemic retina so VEGF falls. Effective spot size depends on the contact lens magnification

Why the colour matters at the slit lamp

  • Green 532 nm: well absorbed by melanin and haemoglobin; the workhorse for retina and SLT.
  • Yellow 577 nm: near the haemoglobin peak and barely absorbed by xanthophyll, so it is gentler near the fovea.
  • Red and near-infrared (647, 810 nm): pass through blood and cloudy media and reach deeper into the choroid; burns are less visible and more painful.
  • Blue (488 nm): absorbed by xanthophyll and scattered by the ageing lens, so avoided at the macula.

Safety essentials

  • Most ophthalmic treatment lasers are Class 4: everyone in the room needs eyewear rated for that wavelength.
  • Know the aiming beam and focus carefully: the commonest serious error is a foveal burn.
  • After Nd:YAG procedures, check IOP at 1 hour, especially in glaucoma patients.
  • Write down the laser, wavelength, spot size, duration, power and number of spots: repeat treatments depend on it.

Where residents slip

  • Thinking the Nd:YAG “burns” the capsule: it disrupts with plasma, not heat.
  • Using a blue wavelength at the macula: xanthophyll absorbs it.
  • Forgetting that the contact lens changes the spot size on the retina: always know your lens's magnification.
  • Assuming SLT is the same as ALT: SLT uses nanosecond pulses that spare the meshwork structure and can be repeated.
  • Calling corneal cross-linking a laser treatment: it uses UVA light with riboflavin.
