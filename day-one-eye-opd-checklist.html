Day 1 in the Eye OPD: Pocket Checklist for Junior Residents

Last updated: November 2026

Your first day in the eye OPD: what to carry, the order to examine in, what to write, which patients must not go home, and the mistakes juniors make. Print it as a pocket card or keep it open on your phone.

On this page What to carry Exam sequence Note template Do not send home Never do Dilating safely Asking for help

Before clinic: what to carry

  • Pen torch, near vision chart and pinhole occluder
  • Direct ophthalmoscope; 90 D or 78 D lens (and 20 D if you will use the indirect)
  • Fluorescein strips, topical anaesthetic drops (for examination only)
  • Red-top object (bottle cap) for colour desaturation and fields
  • Ishihara book access, Amsler grid, millimetre ruler
  • A notebook or phone list of senior and on-call numbers, and the referral pathway for retina, glaucoma, cornea, paediatrics, oculoplasty and neuro clinics

The examination sequence (every patient)

  1. Vision first, each eye separately, with glasses and pinhole; near vision if over 40. Never skip it, even in an emergency (except chemical injury: irrigate first).
  2. Pupils before any drops: size, reaction, and swinging-torch test for RAPD.
  3. Ocular movements and alignment; cover test if a squint or diplopia is reported.
  4. Confrontation fields when the history suggests a neurological or glaucoma problem.
  5. Slit lamp from front to back: lids and lashes, conjunctiva, cornea (with fluorescein if red or painful), anterior chamber depth (van Herick) and cells, iris, lens.
  6. IOP (after fluorescein and anaesthetic); avoid applanation on an infected or perforated cornea.
  7. Fundus: disc, macula and vessels, dilated if indicated and safe.

Always write which eye (RE / LE, never just "the eye"), the time of IOP measurement, the drug strength, and the follow-up date. If you did not see the fundus, write "fundus not seen" and why.

Do not send home without a senior review

PresentationWhy it is urgent
Chemical injuryIrrigate immediately until the pH is neutral, before history or vision; then grade the limbal ischaemia.
Sudden painless loss of visionCentral retinal artery occlusion (a stroke equivalent), vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment, ischaemic optic neuropathy.
Elderly patient with headache, jaw claudication or scalp tenderness and visual symptomsGiant cell arteritis: same-day ESR and CRP and steroids to protect the other eye.
Painful red eye with a mid-dilated pupil, hazy cornea and high IOPAcute angle closure.
Flashes, new floaters, a curtain or shadowRetinal tear or detachment: needs a dilated peripheral fundus examination today.
Corneal ulcer or infiltrate, especially in a contact lens wearerMicrobial keratitis: scrape, start intensive treatment, review next day.
Pain, worse vision or hypopyon after eye surgery or an injectionEndophthalmitis: vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics the same day.
Penetrating injury, or high-velocity injury (hammering, grinding)Open globe or intraocular foreign body: shield the eye, no pressure, X-ray or CT (not MRI if metal suspected).
New III nerve palsy involving the pupil, or with headachePosterior communicating artery aneurysm until proven otherwise: same-day CT or MR angiography.
Bilateral disc swellingRaised intracranial pressure: urgent neuroimaging.
Swollen red lids with fever, proptosis or painful eye movementsOrbital cellulitis: admit for IV antibiotics and CT.
HyphaemaCheck IOP, ask about sickle cell disease and anticoagulants; arrange daily review.
Leukocoria or a new squint with poor vision in a childExclude retinoblastoma: urgent referral.

Never do

  • Never prescribe topical anaesthetic drops for home use: they cause non-healing ulcers and corneal melt.
  • Never start steroid drops for a red eye without a slit lamp and fluorescein examination: they can turn a herpetic dendrite or fungal ulcer into a perforation.
  • Never dilate a patient before checking the pupils and anterior chamber depth, or a patient under neurological observation without the neurosurgeon's agreement.
  • Never pad an eye with a suspected infection, or apply pressure to an eye that may be open.
  • Never label a red eye "conjunctivitis" if vision is reduced, the eye is painful or photophobic, or the cornea stains.
  • Never prescribe timolol without asking about asthma and heart block.
  • Never let a patient leave without knowing when to come back and which symptoms mean "come back today".

Dilating safely

  • Usual adult regimen: tropicamide 1% with phenylephrine 2.5%, one drop each, repeated after 10 minutes if needed; full dilation in 20–30 minutes.
  • Check the anterior chamber depth (van Herick) first. A shallow angle is not an absolute bar, but warn the patient about pain and blurred vision and check the IOP before they leave if you are worried.
  • Use phenylephrine with care in patients with uncontrolled hypertension or heart disease, and lower strengths in infants.
  • Tell patients not to drive until the blur wears off (usually 4–6 hours).

Asking for help and handing over

  • Use SBAR when you call a senior: Situation (who and what), Background (relevant history), Assessment (your findings, including vision and IOP), Recommendation (what you need: review, advice, theatre).
  • Write down what the senior advised, with their name and the time.
  • Hand over every patient still waiting for a result or review at the end of clinic.
