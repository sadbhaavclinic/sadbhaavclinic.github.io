Ophthalmic Nursing Module 2: Front-of-Eye Disorders: Lids, Tears, Conjunctiva, Cornea and Uvea

For: Allied eye-care staff

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • A red eye with reduced vision, severe pain, photophobia or a hazy cornea is not simple conjunctivitis: refer the same day.
  • Corneal ulcers are emergencies: scrape for culture before starting fortified drops, and give drops exactly on time, often hourly round the clock.
  • Contact lens wearers with a painful red eye must remove the lens and be seen urgently (risk of Pseudomonas or Acanthamoeba).
  • Steroid drops help uveitis but can worsen herpes and fungal infections and raise eye pressure: never start or stop them without the doctor.

Eyelid problems

ConditionWhat it isNursing points
BlepharitisLong-term inflammation of the lid margins, often with meibomian gland dysfunctionTeach warm compresses and lid hygiene daily; it is controlled, not cured.
Stye (hordeolum)Acute infection of a lash follicle or glandWarm compresses; do not squeeze.
ChalazionPainless lump from a blocked meibomian glandWarm compresses; incision and curettage if persistent. A recurrent lump in an older adult may need biopsy (sebaceous carcinoma).
Entropion / ectropionLid turned in (lashes rub the cornea) / turned out (watering, exposure)Lubricants and taping as advised until surgery.
PtosisDrooping upper lidSudden ptosis with double vision or a large pupil is urgent (third nerve palsy). Variable ptosis worse in the evening suggests myasthenia.
TrichiasisLashes growing towards the eyeEpilation gives short relief; recurrence common. Trachoma is a historical cause in India.

Tear drainage and dry eye

  • Watering eye: either too many tears (irritation) or poor drainage (blocked duct, lid problems). Syringing tests whether the drainage path is open.
  • Dacryocystitis: infection of the lacrimal sac: a tender swelling at the inner corner, sometimes with pus. Acute cases need systemic antibiotics; the cure is usually DCR surgery later.
  • Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction: watering and sticky eye in babies; most clear by the first birthday with massage. See the patient guide.
  • Dry eye disease: grittiness, burning, fluctuating vision, worse with screens and air-conditioning. Treatment: lubricants (preservative-free if used more than about four times a day), lid hygiene, blinking breaks, and sometimes anti-inflammatory drops. Severe dry eye with dry mouth suggests Sjögren syndrome.

Conjunctivitis and other red eyes

TypeCluesCare
Viral (adenovirus)Watery discharge, often both eyes, swollen node in front of the ear, recent coldHighly contagious for about 2 weeks: hand washing, separate towels, clean tonometers and chin rests. Lubricants and cold compresses.
BacterialSticky yellow discharge, lids stuck in the morningAntibiotic drops; hygiene.
AllergicItching, both eyes, history of allergy; in children, vernal keratoconjunctivitisCold compresses, anti-allergy drops; steroids only under supervision.
Neonatal (ophthalmia neonatorum)Discharge in the first month of lifeUrgent: gonococcal infection can perforate the cornea within days. Swab and treat promptly.

Other red eyes: subconjunctival haemorrhage (bright red patch, painless, vision normal; check blood pressure and anticoagulants), episcleritis (mild, sectoral redness), scleritis (deep boring pain that wakes the patient, often with rheumatoid arthritis: refer urgently).

Red-eye danger signs (refer the same day)

  • Reduced vision
  • Moderate or severe pain, or pain on eye movement
  • Light sensitivity
  • Hazy cornea or white spot on the cornea
  • Irregular or non-reacting pupil
  • Recent eye surgery or injection
  • Contact lens wearer
  • Newborn baby

Cornea

Corneal abrasion

A scratch of the surface layer, which stains green with fluorescein under blue light. It is very painful and watery. Treatment is antibiotic ointment or drops and pain relief; most heal in 1–3 days. Never give anaesthetic drops to take home. See corneal abrasion.

Microbial keratitis (corneal ulcer)

  • Causes: bacteria (contact lenses, injury), fungi (injury with plant matter, common in agricultural India), Acanthamoeba (contact lenses with tap water), herpes simplex (dendritic ulcer).
  • Signs: white infiltrate, epithelial defect, sometimes hypopyon (pus layer in the anterior chamber).
  • Nursing: help with corneal scraping for smear and culture before treatment; prepare and label fortified antibiotic drops (stored in the fridge, short shelf life); keep an hourly drop chart, including at night in the first 48 hours if prescribed; space different drops by at least 5 minutes; watch for perforation (sudden gush of fluid, flat chamber).
  • Herpes simplex keratitis: treated with antiviral (for example ganciclovir gel or oral acyclovir). Steroids alone can make it much worse.

Other corneal conditions

  • Keratoconus: progressive thinning and coning, usually in teenagers and young adults; eye rubbing and allergy worsen it. Corneal cross-linking stops progression. Acute hydrops (sudden clouding) may occur.
  • Fuchs endothelial dystrophy: failing endothelial pump; vision worse in the morning. Treated with endothelial keratoplasty when advanced.
  • Exposure keratopathy: eye not closing (facial palsy, unconscious ICU patients, proptosis). In ICU, keep the lids closed and the surface moist: this is a nursing responsibility.
  • Vitamin A deficiency (xerophthalmia): night blindness, Bitot spots, corneal melting in malnourished children. Give vitamin A as per national schedule; urgent if the cornea is affected.

Uveitis

  • Anterior uveitis (iritis): painful red eye, photophobia, small pupil, cells and flare in the anterior chamber. Treatment: steroid drops (often frequent, then tapered) and dilating drops (to relieve spasm and prevent the iris sticking to the lens).
  • Associations: HLA-B27 conditions (ankylosing spondylitis), sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis (often silent: children need regular screening), herpes.
  • Posterior uveitis causes floaters and blurred vision and often needs systemic treatment.
  • Nursing: teach the steroid taper exactly (a written chart helps; see the taper schedule maker), explain that steroid drops can raise IOP so pressure checks are needed, and shake suspension drops (prednisolone acetate) well.

Infection control in the eye clinic

Adenovirus survives on surfaces and spreads easily in eye departments. Clean the tonometer prism, slit-lamp chin rest and forehead bar between patients, use single-use items where possible, wash hands before and after every patient, and separate patients with red eyes in the waiting area during outbreaks. See the infection control checklist.

Practice questions (short answer and viva)

How do you tell conjunctivitis from a sight-threatening red eye?

Conjunctivitis keeps normal vision, normal pupil and clear cornea, with discharge and little pain. Reduced vision, significant pain, photophobia, hazy cornea or abnormal pupil need same-day review.

What must happen before fortified antibiotic drops are started for a corneal ulcer?

Corneal scraping for smear and culture, as ordered, so the organism can be identified.

Why are dilating drops given in anterior uveitis?

To relieve ciliary spasm (pain) and stop the iris sticking to the lens (posterior synechiae).

A newborn has heavy eye discharge on day 3. What do you do?

Treat as urgent ophthalmia neonatorum: inform the doctor, take swabs, start treatment as ordered; gonococcal infection can perforate the cornea.

How do you protect the eyes of an unconscious ICU patient?

Keep lids closed (taping or moisture chamber as per protocol), lubricate regularly and check the cornea for exposure.

Self-test: 8 MCQs

Answer all, then press “Check my score”. Option order is mixed.

1. A painless bright red patch on the white of the eye with normal vision is most likely:

Show answer

Answer: Subconjunctival haemorrhage. Check blood pressure and anticoagulants.

2. Fungal keratitis is commonly associated with injury from:

Show answer

Answer: Vegetable matter. Common in agricultural settings.

3. A dendritic ulcer is typical of:

Show answer

Answer: Herpes simplex. Steroids alone worsen it.

4. The most contagious common conjunctivitis in eye clinics is:

Show answer

Answer: Adenoviral. Clean equipment between patients.

5. A child with juvenile idiopathic arthritis needs regular eye screening because uveitis is:

Show answer

Answer: Often silent. White, quiet eye with damaging inflammation.

6. Prednisolone acetate drops must be:

Show answer

Answer: Shaken well before use. It is a suspension.

7. Deep boring eye pain that wakes the patient at night suggests:

Show answer

Answer: Scleritis. Often linked to systemic disease.

8. Night blindness with Bitot spots in a malnourished child suggests deficiency of:

Show answer

Answer: Vitamin A. Xerophthalmia.
