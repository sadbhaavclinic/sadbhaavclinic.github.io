Station 1: Senile cataract Lens

Examiner: A 68-year-old with gradual painless blurring of vision for two years. On the slit lamp you see a grey-white lens opacity with an iris shadow.

  1. What type and stage of cataract is this?
    An immature senile cataract: the presence of an iris shadow means clear cortex remains between the pupil margin and the opacity. Name the morphology: nuclear, cortical or posterior subcapsular.
  2. How do you grade it?
    LOCS III (Lens Opacities Classification System III) for nuclear opalescence and colour, cortical and posterior subcapsular cataract; or a simple clinical nuclear grade 1–4/5.
  3. What pre-operative work-up do you do?
    Visual acuity with pinhole, IOP, pupil reaction and RAPD, slit lamp (pseudoexfoliation, corneal guttata, phacodonesis), dilated fundus (or B-scan if no view), biometry (axial length and keratometry), and systemic review (diabetes, BP, anticoagulants, tamsulosin).
  4. Which IOL formula would you choose?
    Modern formulas (Barrett Universal II, Kane) for all eyes. If limited to older formulas: Hoffer Q for short eyes (< 22 mm), SRK/T for long eyes (> 26 mm), Holladay 1 or SRK/T for average eyes.
  5. Why does a nuclear cataract cause second sight?
    The sclerosing nucleus raises the refractive index of the lens, causing a myopic shift, so a presbyopic patient may read again without glasses.
  6. What is the commonest intraoperative complication and how do you manage it?
    Posterior capsule rupture. Stop, keep the chamber formed with viscoelastic, avoid dropping the nucleus, perform anterior vitrectomy if vitreous presents, and place the IOL in the sulcus (optic capture if possible) or defer.
  7. A day-3 post-op patient has pain, worse vision and hypopyon. What do you do?
    Suspect acute endophthalmitis: same-day vitreous tap and intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg and ceftazidime 2.25 mg; consider vitrectomy if vision is light perception only (EVS).

Station 2: Congenital cataract Lens

Examiner: A 6-week-old baby with a white pupil in the right eye noticed in photographs. Red reflex is absent on the right.

  1. What is your first priority?
    Urgent assessment because of the risk of dense stimulus deprivation amblyopia; also exclude retinoblastoma with a dilated fundus examination or B-scan.
  2. When should a dense unilateral cataract be operated?
    Ideally by 6 weeks of age; dense bilateral cataracts by 8 to 10 weeks, operating the second eye within a week or two.
  3. Which investigations do you do?
    Family history and examination of parents; TORCH serology, urine for reducing substances (galactosaemia), calcium and glucose, and a paediatric review for syndromes in bilateral cases.
  4. What surgery do you perform?
    Lens aspiration with primary posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy, because the posterior capsule opacifies rapidly in children.
  5. What did the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study show?
    In unilateral cataract operated at 1 to 6 months, an IOL gave no better vision than contact lens aphakia correction at age 5 and caused more reoperations, so many surgeons leave infants under 6 months aphakic.
  6. What is the main long-term complication?
    Glaucoma (aphakic or pseudophakic) in up to a quarter of eyes, especially with surgery in the first month, so lifelong IOP monitoring is needed.
  7. Why is amblyopia treatment essential after surgery?
    Surgery only clears the media; vision depends on immediate optical correction and patching of the better eye for years.

Station 3: Cataract with pseudoexfoliation Lens

Examiner: A 72-year-old listed for cataract surgery. Dilated examination shows grey flaky material on the anterior capsule in a target pattern and the pupil dilates only to 5 mm.

  1. What is pseudoexfoliation syndrome?
    An age-related systemic disorder of extracellular matrix in which fibrillar material is deposited in the anterior segment and elsewhere; linked to LOXL1 variants.
  2. Why is cataract surgery higher risk?
    Poor pupil dilation, weak zonules (phacodonesis, zonular dialysis), shallow chamber, and a higher risk of posterior capsule rupture and dropped nucleus.
  3. How do you manage a small pupil?
    Intracameral mydriatics, viscomydriasis, iris hooks or a Malyugin ring.
  4. How do you support weak zonules?
    Gentle capsulorhexis of adequate size, hydrodissection, chop techniques, capsular tension ring, or capsular tension segments for larger dialyses.
  5. What late complication should you warn about?
    Late in-the-bag IOL dislocation years later, and pseudoexfoliation glaucoma, which needs regular IOP checks.
  6. Which signs on gonioscopy are typical?
    Heavy patchy trabecular pigmentation and a Sampaolesi line anterior to Schwalbe line.
  7. Why is the capsulorhexis size important here?
    A small rhexis causes capsule contraction (phimosis) which further stresses weak zonules; aim for about 5 to 5.5 mm.

Station 4: Ectopia lentis Lens

Examiner: A tall 14-year-old with long fingers and high myopia. The lens is displaced superotemporally and the zonules are visible in the pupil.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1 mutation), in which the lens typically subluxates superotemporally.
  2. Which other causes of ectopia lentis do you know?
    Homocystinuria (inferonasal, with thromboembolic risk), Weill-Marchesani (microspherophakia), sulfite oxidase deficiency, trauma, pseudoexfoliation and high myopia.
  3. Why does homocystinuria matter for anaesthesia?
    There is a high risk of thromboembolism under general anaesthesia; hydrate well and check homocysteine before surgery.
  4. What systemic referral is essential in Marfan syndrome?
    Cardiology for aortic root dilatation and mitral valve prolapse; the Ghent criteria combine lens dislocation, aortic dilatation and family history.
  5. How do you manage a subluxated lens?
    Best correction through the phakic or aphakic part of the pupil first; lensectomy with scleral-fixated, iris-fixated or anterior chamber IOL when vision cannot be corrected or amblyopia threatens.
  6. What causes pupil block glaucoma here?
    Anterior dislocation of the lens, especially a spherophakic lens, which can be relieved by dilating, laying supine or laser iridotomy.
  7. What retinal risk should you check?
    Retinal detachment, which is more common in Marfan syndrome and after lens surgery.

Station 5: Posterior capsule opacification Lens

Examiner: A 60-year-old had uncomplicated cataract surgery two years ago and now reports gradual glare and blurring. Retroillumination shows Elschnig pearls.

  1. What is posterior capsule opacification?
    Proliferation and migration of residual lens epithelial cells onto the posterior capsule, forming pearls or fibrosis.
  2. Which IOL features reduce PCO?
    A sharp square posterior optic edge, a capsulorhexis that overlaps the optic edge, and thorough cortical clean-up; hydrophobic acrylic lenses have lower rates.
  3. How do you perform Nd:YAG capsulotomy?
    A cruciate or circular opening of about 3 to 4 mm with the lowest effective energy, focused slightly behind the capsule, avoiding the IOL.
  4. What are the complications of YAG capsulotomy?
    IOP spike, IOL pitting, cystoid macular oedema, retinal detachment (especially in high myopes) and IOL dislocation.
  5. What else can cause poor vision after cataract surgery?
    CME, refractive surprise, dry eye, epiretinal membrane, IOL decentration, endophthalmitis and pre-existing macular disease.
  6. Why is PCO more common in children?
    Lens epithelial cells are very active, so primary posterior capsulotomy with anterior vitrectomy is routine in young children.
  7. What is capsular block syndrome?
    Viscoelastic or fluid trapped behind the IOL in a sealed bag, causing a myopic shift and milky fluid; treated by YAG at the bag edge or capsulotomy.

Station 6: Cystoid macular oedema after cataract surgery Lens

Examiner: A diabetic patient sees well on day one after phaco but returns at six weeks with blurring. OCT shows cystic spaces in the macula.

  1. What is this condition called?
    Pseudophakic cystoid macular oedema (Irvine-Gass syndrome), peaking around 4 to 6 weeks after surgery.
  2. What is the mechanism?
    Surgical inflammation releases prostaglandins, which break the blood-retinal barrier at the perifoveal capillaries.
  3. Which patients are at higher risk?
    Diabetics, uveitis, epiretinal membrane, previous RVO, complicated surgery with vitreous loss or iris trauma, and prostaglandin analogue use.
  4. What does FFA show?
    Petaloid hyperfluorescence at the macula from leaking perifoveal capillaries, often with optic disc leakage.
  5. How do you treat it?
    Topical NSAID (for example nepafenac or ketorolac) plus topical steroid; then periocular or intravitreal steroid for resistant cases.
  6. Can it be prevented?
    Yes, prophylactic topical NSAID in high-risk patients reduces CME (ESCRS PREMED study supported NSAID plus steroid; sub-Tenon triamcinolone helped diabetics).
  7. How do you tell it from diabetic macular oedema?
    Pseudophakic CME has petaloid leakage and disc leakage and appears after surgery; DME has microaneurysms and exudates and may predate surgery.

Station 7: Refractive surprise after cataract surgery Lens

Examiner: A patient aiming for emmetropia is -2.50 D one month after uneventful phaco in a 23.5 mm eye.

  1. What are the common causes?
    Errors in axial length or keratometry, wrong IOL power selected or inserted, wrong formula or constant, and previous refractive surgery not accounted for.
  2. What do you check first?
    Confirm the refraction, then review biometry records, the IOL label and power used, and repeat biometry and topography.
  3. Which formulas perform best?
    Barrett Universal II and Kane across axial lengths; Hoffer Q older choice for short eyes; formula choice matters most at the extremes.
  4. How much does a 1 mm axial length error cause?
    About 2.5 to 3 dioptres of refractive error in an average eye.
  5. How do you manage it?
    Spectacles or contact lenses, IOL exchange (best early), piggyback sulcus IOL, or corneal laser refractive surgery once stable.
  6. How do you handle an eye with previous LASIK?
    Use history-free methods such as Barrett True-K or Haigis-L, or the ASCRS calculator, because standard keratometry overestimates corneal power after myopic LASIK.
  7. How do you prevent wrong-lens events?
    WHO-style surgical checklist, confirm patient, eye, IOL model and power aloud before insertion.

Station 8: Toric IOLs and astigmatism Lens

Examiner: A cataract patient has 2.5 D of regular with-the-rule corneal astigmatism and wants good distance vision without glasses.

  1. What options do you have to correct astigmatism at cataract surgery?
    Toric IOL, limbal relaxing incisions, on-axis incision, and later laser vision correction.
  2. Why must posterior corneal astigmatism be considered?
    The posterior cornea usually adds against-the-rule astigmatism, so anterior keratometry alone overcorrects WTR and undercorrects ATR astigmatism. Barrett toric accounts for it.
  3. How do you mark the axis?
    Preoperative reference marks at 0 and 180 degrees with the patient sitting (to avoid cyclotorsion), or image-guided systems.
  4. How much effect does rotation have?
    Each degree of misalignment loses about 3.3% of the cylinder correction; 30 degrees off gives no benefit.
  5. When does a toric IOL rotate and how do you fix it?
    Usually in the first days to weeks, more in long eyes with large bags; reposition after 1 to 3 weeks once the capsule has started to contract.
  6. Which patients are poor candidates?
    Irregular astigmatism (keratoconus, scars), zonular weakness and unstable keratometry.
  7. What astigmatism do you aim to leave?
    A small amount of with-the-rule residual is better tolerated than against-the-rule in older patients, because ATR increases with age.

Station 9: Posterior capsule rupture and dropped nucleus Lens

Examiner: During phaco of a dense cataract, the anterior chamber suddenly deepens and the nucleus fragment tilts backwards.

  1. What has happened and what are the warning signs?
    Posterior capsule rupture: sudden deepening of the chamber, pupil dilation, a fragment that will not come to the tip or moves backwards, and a visible capsular edge or vitreous.
  2. What is your immediate action?
    Do not withdraw the phaco probe suddenly: stop ultrasound, keep the foot in position 1, inject dispersive viscoelastic through the side port behind the fragment, then withdraw.
  3. How do you manage vitreous in the anterior chamber?
    Anterior vitrectomy (bimanual, through separate incisions, high cut rate, low flow), using triamcinolone to stain the vitreous, until the chamber is free of vitreous.
  4. Where do you place the IOL?
    In the bag if the rent is small and stable; in the sulcus (three-piece IOL, power reduced by about 0.5–1.0 D) with optic capture if possible; otherwise an anterior chamber, iris-fixated or scleral-fixated lens.
  5. What do you do if the nucleus drops into the vitreous?
    Do not chase it. Clean up the anterior segment, close the wound, control inflammation and IOP, and refer for pars plana vitrectomy, ideally within 1–2 weeks.
  6. Which risk factors predict PCR?
    Dense or white cataract, pseudoexfoliation, small pupil, high myopia, previous vitrectomy, posterior polar cataract, poor cooperation and inexperience.
  7. What complications must you look for afterwards?
    Raised IOP, uveitis, cystoid macular oedema, retinal detachment and endophthalmitis.

Station 10: Lens-induced glaucomas Lens

Examiner: An elderly woman with a white mature cataract presents with a painful red eye, IOP 52 mmHg and a deep anterior chamber with floating white particles.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Phacolytic glaucoma: leaking high-molecular-weight lens proteins from an intact capsule, with macrophages, block an open angle.
  2. How does phacomorphic glaucoma differ?
    A swollen intumescent lens pushes the iris forward and closes the angle: shallow chamber and closed angle, unlike phacolytic glaucoma.
  3. What is phacoanaphylactic (lens-particle) uveitis?
    Lens particle glaucoma follows capsule rupture with free cortex in the chamber; phacoanaphylaxis is a granulomatous reaction to lens proteins after trauma or surgery.
  4. How do you manage phacolytic glaucoma?
    Lower IOP medically (aqueous suppressants, hyperosmotics), topical steroids, then early cataract extraction, which cures it.
  5. How do you manage phacomorphic glaucoma?
    Lower IOP medically, laser iridotomy may help temporarily, and early lens extraction.
  6. What precautions do you take during surgery on a white cataract?
    Stain the capsule with trypan blue, decompress an intumescent lens before the capsulorhexis to avoid the "Argentinian flag" sign, and consider MSICS for very hard nuclei.
  7. What is the visual prognosis?
    Usually good if the lens is removed early, but delay risks optic nerve damage; check for a relative afferent pupillary defect before surgery.

Station 11: Microbial keratitis Cornea

Examiner: A 40-year-old farmer with a painful red eye for 6 days after injury with vegetable matter. There is a dry, greyish infiltrate with feathery margins, satellite lesions and a hypopyon.

  1. What is the likely organism?
    Fungal keratitis (filamentous fungi such as Fusarium or Aspergillus): vegetative trauma, dry raised infiltrate, feathery margins, satellite lesions and endothelial plaque.
  2. How do you investigate?
    Corneal scraping: Gram stain, KOH mount and calcofluor white for fungi; culture on blood agar, chocolate agar and Sabouraud dextrose agar (non-nutrient agar with E. coli overlay if Acanthamoeba is suspected). Consider confocal microscopy.
  3. What is your treatment?
    Natamycin 5% hourly (MUTT I: better than voriconazole for filamentous fungi, especially Fusarium), with oral or intrastromal voriconazole for deep or non-responding ulcers. Avoid steroids.
  4. How would you treat a bacterial ulcer?
    Fortified cefazolin 5% plus tobramycin about 1.4% hourly, or a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone monotherapy for small peripheral ulcers.
  5. When may steroids be added in bacterial keratitis?
    SCUT: adjunctive steroids gave no overall benefit, but may help deep or central ulcers with poor vision once the organism is controlled; never in fungal or Acanthamoeba keratitis.
  6. What are the indications for therapeutic keratoplasty?
    Impending or actual perforation not manageable with glue, and progressive infection despite maximal medical treatment, particularly approaching the limbus.
  7. What are the features of Acanthamoeba keratitis?
    Contact lens wearer, pain out of proportion to signs, radial perineuritis, ring infiltrate, and slow response to standard treatment. Treat with biguanides (PHMB or chlorhexidine) with or without propamidine.

Station 12: Herpes simplex keratitis Cornea

Examiner: A 35-year-old with a red, watery, photophobic eye. Fluorescein shows a branching epithelial ulcer with terminal bulbs; corneal sensation is reduced.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    HSV epithelial keratitis (dendritic ulcer). Terminal bulbs, stain of the ulcer bed with fluorescein and the edges with rose bengal, and reduced corneal sensation are typical.
  2. How do you treat it?
    Topical antiviral: ganciclovir 0.15% gel five times a day or acyclovir 3% ointment five times a day until healed, or oral acyclovir 400 mg five times a day. Gentle debridement can help. No topical steroid for epithelial disease.
  3. How is herpetic keratitis classified?
    By layer: epithelial (dendritic, geographic), stromal (necrotising and immune/interstitial), endotheliitis (disciform, diffuse, linear) and neurotrophic keratopathy.
  4. How do you treat disciform keratitis?
    Topical steroid (for example prednisolone acetate 1% tapered slowly) under antiviral cover, usually oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily.
  5. What did HEDS show about prophylaxis?
    Oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily for a year halved recurrences of ocular HSV, and mattered most for people with previous stromal keratitis.
  6. Name some causes of pseudodendrites.
    Herpes zoster, healing epithelial defects, Acanthamoeba, contact lens wear, tyrosinaemia type II and topical drug toxicity.
  7. Why should you avoid steroids alone in a dendritic ulcer?
    Steroids enhance viral replication and can enlarge a dendrite into a geographic ulcer and promote stromal melting.

Station 13: Fungal keratitis Cornea

Examiner: A farmer with a two-week history of a painful eye after an injury with vegetable matter. There is a dry-looking grey infiltrate with feathery margins and satellite lesions.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis and which organisms?
    Filamentous fungal keratitis, most often Fusarium or Aspergillus in India. Candida (yeast) is more common in compromised eyes.
  2. Which investigations do you send?
    Corneal scraping for KOH mount and Gram stain, culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar and blood agar; confocal microscopy if available; corneal biopsy if scrapes are negative and the ulcer is deep.
  3. What did MUTT I show?
    Natamycin 5% gave better visual outcomes and fewer perforations than voriconazole 1% for filamentous fungal keratitis, especially Fusarium.
  4. What is your first-line treatment?
    Natamycin 5% hourly, tapered over weeks, plus cycloplegic; add oral or intrastromal/intracameral voriconazole for deep or progressing ulcers. Avoid steroids.
  5. When do you consider therapeutic keratoplasty?
    For impending or actual perforation, or a worsening ulcer despite maximal medical treatment; excise with a clear margin around the infiltrate.
  6. What is Pythium keratitis and why does it matter?
    An oomycete that mimics fungal keratitis but responds poorly to antifungals; antibacterial combinations (linezolid, azithromycin) and early surgery are used.
  7. Why does a hypopyon in fungal keratitis not always clear with antibiotics?
    Fungi can penetrate an intact Descemet membrane, so the hypopyon may contain organisms (unlike sterile bacterial hypopyon).

Station 14: Acanthamoeba keratitis Cornea

Examiner: A soft contact lens wearer who rinses lenses in tap water has severe pain out of proportion to signs. You see a ring infiltrate and radial perineural infiltrates.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Acanthamoeba keratitis. Pain out of proportion, radial keratoneuritis and a ring infiltrate in a contact lens wearer are classic.
  2. What is the main risk factor?
    Contact lens wear with exposure to water (swimming, showering, tap-water rinsing). Trauma with soil or water in non-wearers.
  3. How do you confirm it?
    Corneal scrape for culture on non-nutrient agar with an E. coli overlay, calcofluor white or Giemsa stain, PCR, and in vivo confocal microscopy showing double-walled cysts.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Biguanide (PHMB 0.02% or chlorhexidine 0.02%) with or without a diamidine (propamidine), hourly initially, for months. Polihexanide 0.08% monotherapy is now approved in Europe.
  5. Why is it hard to eradicate?
    Cysts are highly resistant and can lie dormant in the stroma, so treatment continues long after symptoms improve.
  6. Is early misdiagnosis common?
    Yes. It is often treated first as HSV keratitis because of the epithelial pseudodendrites and stromal infiltrate.
  7. How do you counsel lens wearers to prevent it?
    Never let water touch lenses or cases, do not sleep or swim in lenses, rub and rinse with fresh solution and replace the case regularly.

Station 15: Keratoconus Cornea

Examiner: A 17-year-old with allergic eye disease and rapidly changing astigmatism. Retinoscopy shows a scissoring reflex.

  1. What signs of keratoconus do you know?
    Scissoring reflex, oil-droplet red reflex, Munson sign, Rizzuti sign, Vogt striae, Fleischer ring, apical thinning and scarring, and acute hydrops.
  2. How do you diagnose it early?
    Corneal tomography (for example Pentacam): posterior elevation, pachymetry progression and the Belin-Ambrosio display are more sensitive than anterior curvature alone.
  3. How do you define progression?
    Change over time in at least two of: steepening of the anterior surface, steepening of the posterior surface, or thinning (ABCD progression display).
  4. What is corneal collagen cross-linking?
    Riboflavin with UV-A light creates covalent cross-links in stromal collagen to halt progression. The Dresden protocol needs a cornea of at least 400 microns after epithelium removal.
  5. How do you correct vision?
    Spectacles in mild cases, then rigid gas-permeable, hybrid or scleral lenses; intracorneal ring segments; deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty for advanced scarring.
  6. What is acute hydrops and how do you treat it?
    A break in Descemet membrane causing sudden stromal oedema. Treat with hypertonic saline, cycloplegia and sometimes intracameral gas (C3F8 or air) to speed resolution.
  7. Which conditions are associated?
    Atopy and eye rubbing, Down syndrome, Leber congenital amaurosis, connective tissue disorders and floppy eyelid syndrome.

Station 16: Fuchs endothelial dystrophy Cornea

Examiner: A 65-year-old woman reports blurred vision that is worst on waking and clears during the day. Specular reflection shows guttata.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy: central guttata with endothelial cell loss leading to stromal and later epithelial oedema.
  2. Why is vision worse in the morning?
    With the eyes closed there is no tear evaporation, so the cornea swells overnight and thins during the day.
  3. What is the genetics?
    Usually late-onset, female predominant; the commonest association is a CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion in TCF4.
  4. How do you assess severity?
    Slit lamp grading of guttata confluence, pachymetry, specular microscopy and Scheimpflug tomography signs of subclinical oedema.
  5. What is the surgical treatment of choice?
    Endothelial keratoplasty, preferably DMEK (better visual recovery and lower rejection) or DSAEK. Descemet stripping only (DSO) with ROCK inhibitor is an option for central guttata with a healthy periphery.
  6. How does it affect cataract surgery?
    Risk of post-operative corneal decompensation; protect the endothelium with dispersive viscoelastic and low phaco energy, and consider a triple procedure if the cornea is already thick.
  7. What medical treatment helps early disease?
    Hypertonic saline 5% drops and ointment, and a hairdryer at arm's length in the morning to evaporate tears.

Station 17: Corneal graft rejection Cornea

Examiner: A patient 8 months after penetrating keratoplasty notices redness and blurring. You see keratic precipitates in a line on the graft endothelium.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Endothelial graft rejection: a Khodadoust line of keratic precipitates advancing across the graft with stromal oedema behind it.
  2. What other types of rejection do you know?
    Epithelial rejection (a raised epithelial line), subepithelial infiltrates (Krachmer spots) and stromal rejection.
  3. How do you treat it?
    Intensive topical steroid (prednisolone acetate 1% hourly), with or without a periocular or systemic steroid pulse for severe or high-risk cases.
  4. Which factors increase rejection risk?
    Vascularised host cornea, previous graft failure, large or eccentric grafts, young age, active inflammation, loose sutures and anterior synechiae.
  5. How do lamellar grafts compare?
    DALK avoids endothelial rejection completely; DMEK has the lowest rejection rate of endothelial grafts.
  6. What is primary graft failure?
    A graft that is oedematous from the first day and never clears, caused by poor donor endothelium or surgical trauma, not rejection.
  7. What should every keratoplasty patient know?
    The RSVP warning signs: redness, sensitivity to light, vision loss and pain, and to attend the same day.

Station 18: Dry eye disease Cornea

Examiner: A 50-year-old office worker with burning, grittiness and fluctuating vision that worsens through the day at the computer.

  1. How is dry eye defined (TFOS DEWS)?
    A multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterised by loss of tear film homeostasis, with symptoms; tear instability, hyperosmolarity, inflammation and neurosensory abnormalities play roles.
  2. What are the two main subtypes?
    Evaporative (mostly meibomian gland dysfunction) and aqueous deficient (Sjögren and non-Sjögren); most patients have both.
  3. Which tests do you do?
    Symptom score (OSDI or DEQ-5), tear break-up time, fluorescein and lissamine green staining, Schirmer test, meibomian gland expression; osmolarity and meibography if available.
  4. How do you treat it step by step?
    Education, screen breaks and lid hygiene with warm compresses; preservative-free lubricants; then anti-inflammatories (cyclosporine, lifitegrast, short steroid course), punctal plugs; then serum drops, scleral lenses and procedures.
  5. What features suggest Sjögren syndrome?
    Dry mouth, arthralgia, parotid swelling, positive anti-Ro/La antibodies, low Schirmer values; there is a lymphoma risk, so refer to rheumatology.
  6. What treatments target meibomian gland dysfunction?
    Warm compresses, lid massage, oral doxycycline or azithromycin, intense pulsed light and thermal pulsation devices.
  7. Which drugs can cause dry eye?
    Antihistamines, antidepressants, isotretinoin, beta-blockers, diuretics, and preserved glaucoma drops.

Station 19: Vernal keratoconjunctivitis Cornea

Examiner: A 10-year-old boy with intense itching, ropy discharge and photophobia every spring. Lifting the upper lid shows cobblestone papillae.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (palpebral type), a chronic allergic disease of young boys in warm climates.
  2. What limbal signs may be present?
    Gelatinous limbal papillae with Horner-Trantas dots (collections of eosinophils).
  3. What is a shield ulcer?
    A sterile, oval epithelial defect in the upper cornea with plaque, caused by mechanical trauma from papillae and eosinophil toxins.
  4. How do you treat VKC?
    Mast cell stabiliser or dual-action drops (olopatadine), topical cyclosporine 0.1% or tacrolimus for persistent disease, short pulses of topical steroid for flares, and cold compresses.
  5. How do you treat a shield ulcer?
    Steroid and cyclosporine to control inflammation, debridement of the plaque, a bandage contact lens or amniotic membrane if it does not heal.
  6. What long-term complications must you watch for?
    Steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract, keratoconus from eye rubbing, limbal stem cell deficiency and amblyopia from shield ulcer scarring.
  7. How is atopic keratoconjunctivitis different?
    AKC affects adults with eczema, is perennial, involves the lower tarsus more and causes more scarring, keratoconus and herpetic infection.

Station 20: Pterygium Cornea

Examiner: A 45-year-old outdoor worker with a fleshy triangular growth from the nasal conjunctiva encroaching 3 mm onto the cornea.

  1. What is a pterygium and what causes it?
    A wing-shaped fibrovascular growth of conjunctiva onto the cornea, linked to UV exposure and limbal stem cell damage.
  2. What are the parts of a pterygium?
    Cap (the leading avascular edge), head (on the cornea), neck and body (over the sclera). Stocker line is iron deposition in front of the head.
  3. When do you operate?
    Progression towards the visual axis, induced astigmatism, cosmetic concern, restricted movement or diagnostic doubt.
  4. Which technique has the lowest recurrence?
    Excision with conjunctival limbal autograft, secured with fibrin glue or sutures. Bare sclera excision alone has recurrence rates up to 80%.
  5. What adjuncts reduce recurrence?
    Mitomycin C (intraoperative 0.02%), amniotic membrane, and anti-VEGF has been tried; avoid beta irradiation now because of scleral necrosis.
  6. How does pterygium affect cataract surgery planning?
    It flattens the cornea in its meridian and causes with-the-rule astigmatism, so remove it first and wait for keratometry to stabilise before biometry.
  7. Which lesions can mimic a pterygium?
    Pseudopterygium (after a burn or ulcer, can be probed underneath), OSSN, pinguecula and limbal dermoid.

Station 21: Stevens-Johnson syndrome Cornea

Examiner: A 25-year-old who developed skin blisters and mucosal ulcers after starting carbamazepine is referred with red, sticky eyes.

  1. What is SJS/TEN?
    A severe mucocutaneous drug reaction with epidermal necrosis: SJS under 10% body surface area, TEN over 30%, overlap in between.
  2. Which drugs are common triggers?
    Carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine, allopurinol, sulfonamides, NSAIDs (oxicams) and nevirapine. HLA-B*15:02 predicts carbamazepine SJS in South and East Asians.
  3. What do you do in the acute stage?
    Daily examination, aggressive lubrication, topical steroid and antibiotic, glass-rod sweeping of the fornices, and early amniotic membrane transplantation over the lid margins and ocular surface.
  4. Why is early amniotic membrane important?
    It reduces late lid margin keratinisation, symblepharon and corneal blindness when applied within the first week.
  5. What are the chronic complications?
    Lid margin keratinisation, trichiasis, symblepharon, dry eye, limbal stem cell deficiency, corneal vascularisation and keratinisation.
  6. How do you rehabilitate chronic disease?
    Mucous membrane grafts for lid margin keratinisation, scleral lenses (PROSE), limbal stem cell transplantation and, as a last resort, keratoprosthesis (Boston type 1 or 2).
  7. How do you differentiate it from mucous membrane pemphigoid?
    SJS is acute and drug triggered with a single episode; pemphigoid is chronic and progressive in older people with linear basement membrane deposits on biopsy.

Station 22: Refractive surgery work-up and ectasia Cornea

Examiner: A 22-year-old with −6.00 D in both eyes wants to get rid of his glasses. Central corneal thickness is 495 µm and he rubs his eyes a lot.

  1. What do you check before offering laser refractive surgery?
    Stable refraction for at least 1 year, age over 18, cycloplegic refraction, corneal tomography (Pentacam or similar), pachymetry, pupil size, dry eye assessment, and a full dilated examination including the retinal periphery.
  2. What are the main risk factors for post-LASIK ectasia?
    Abnormal topography or tomography (forme fruste keratoconus), thin cornea, low residual stromal bed, high percent tissue altered, young age, high myopia, and eye rubbing.
  3. What residual stromal bed and percent tissue altered are considered safe?
    Residual stromal bed of at least 250 µm (many aim for 300 µm) and percent tissue altered under 40%.
  4. How do you estimate ablation depth?
    Munnerlyn formula: depth in microns ≈ optical zone (mm) squared × dioptres ÷ 3; modern profiles remove a little more.
  5. What would you offer this patient?
    Not LASIK. With a thin cornea and eye rubbing, consider surface ablation only if tomography is completely normal, or more safely an implantable collamer lens (ICL), after stopping eye rubbing.
  6. How does SMILE differ from LASIK?
    A lenticule is cut by femtosecond laser and removed through a small incision, with no flap and less effect on corneal nerves; ectasia risk is similar if screening is poor.
  7. How do you recognise ectasia after surgery?
    Progressive myopia and irregular astigmatism with inferior steepening on topography, months to years later; treat with cross-linking and contact lenses.

Station 23: Ocular surface squamous neoplasia Cornea

Examiner: A 45-year-old man has a gelatinous, vascular, leukoplakic limbal lesion at 3 o'clock that has grown over 6 months.

  1. What is your diagnosis?
    Ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN): the spectrum from conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia to invasive squamous cell carcinoma.
  2. Which risk factors do you ask about?
    Ultraviolet exposure, HIV infection (OSSN in a young patient in Africa or India should prompt an HIV test), human papillomavirus, smoking, and immunosuppression.
  3. How can you diagnose it without a biopsy?
    Anterior segment (high-resolution) OCT shows a thickened, hyperreflective epithelium with an abrupt transition; impression cytology and toluidine blue or rose bengal staining also help.
  4. What is the surgical treatment?
    Excision with 4 mm clear margins using a "no-touch" technique, alcohol epitheliectomy of the corneal part, and double freeze-thaw cryotherapy to the conjunctival margins.
  5. What topical treatments are used?
    Interferon alfa-2b, 5-fluorouracil 1% or mitomycin C 0.02–0.04% drops, either alone (chemoreduction) or after surgery.
  6. How does it differ from a pterygium?
    Pterygium is a wing-shaped fibrovascular growth, usually nasal, with a flat head; OSSN is gelatinous or leukoplakic, can be anywhere at the limbus, and grows faster.
  7. What is the recurrence risk?
    About 10–30% after excision alone, lower with adjuvant cryotherapy or topical chemotherapy; recurrences are commonest in the first 2 years.

Station 24: Limbal stem cell deficiency Cornea

Examiner: Two years after a lime injury, a 30-year-old has a vascularised, opaque cornea with recurrent epithelial defects.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD): loss of the limbal epithelial stem cells, so conjunctival epithelium grows onto the cornea.
  2. What are the clinical signs?
    Loss of the palisades of Vogt, late fluorescein staining in a whorled pattern, superficial vascularisation, conjunctivalisation, persistent epithelial defects and scarring.
  3. How do you confirm it?
    Impression cytology showing goblet cells on the cornea; in vivo confocal microscopy and AS-OCT also help.
  4. What are the causes?
    Chemical and thermal burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, mucous membrane pemphigoid, aniridia, contact lens overuse, multiple surgeries and long-term topical medication.
  5. How do you treat unilateral LSCD?
    Limbal stem cell transplantation from the healthy eye: conjunctival-limbal autograft (CLAU) or simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET), developed in India and widely used.
  6. What about bilateral LSCD?
    Living-related or cadaveric limbal allograft with systemic immunosuppression, cultivated oral mucosal epithelial transplantation, or a keratoprosthesis in end-stage disease.
  7. Why does a penetrating keratoplasty alone fail?
    A donor cornea without healthy limbal stem cells is quickly conjunctivalised and the graft fails; restore the limbus first.

Station 25: Neurotrophic keratopathy Cornea

Examiner: A patient with previous herpes zoster ophthalmicus has a painless, oval central epithelial defect with smooth rolled edges.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Neurotrophic keratopathy: corneal epithelial breakdown from loss of corneal sensation (trigeminal nerve damage).
  2. What are the causes?
    Herpes simplex and zoster, diabetes, fifth nerve damage after surgery for acoustic neuroma or trigeminal neuralgia, chemical injury, topical anaesthetic abuse, and corneal surgery.
  3. How do you test corneal sensation?
    Cotton wisp compared with the other eye, or a Cochet-Bonnet aesthesiometer.
  4. How is it staged (Mackie)?
    Stage 1: punctate keratopathy; stage 2: persistent epithelial defect with smooth rolled edges; stage 3: stromal ulcer, melting or perforation.
  5. How do you treat it?
    Stop toxic drops, preservative-free lubricants, treat infection, bandage contact lens or autologous serum, tarsorrhaphy or botulinum-induced ptosis, amniotic membrane, and cenegermin (recombinant nerve growth factor) where available.
  6. What do you do for a perforation?
    Cyanoacrylate glue with a bandage contact lens for small perforations, a patch graft or tectonic keratoplasty for larger ones.
  7. Why is a graft risky in these eyes?
    The donor epithelium also fails to heal without corneal nerves, so grafts often melt; improve the surface first.

Station 26: Ophthalmia neonatorum Cornea

Examiner: A 3-day-old baby has swollen lids and copious purulent discharge from both eyes.

  1. What is ophthalmia neonatorum?
    Conjunctivitis in the first 28 days of life.
  2. How does the timing help diagnosis?
    Chemical (silver nitrate) within 24 hours; gonococcal 2–5 days; chlamydial 5–14 days; herpes simplex 1–2 weeks.
  3. Why is gonococcal infection an emergency?
    Neisseria gonorrhoeae can penetrate intact corneal epithelium and cause rapid ulceration and perforation.
  4. What investigations do you send?
    Conjunctival swabs for Gram stain (Gram-negative intracellular diplococci), culture on chocolate agar, and PCR for Chlamydia and HSV.
  5. How do you treat gonococcal disease?
    Single dose of ceftriaxone 25–50 mg/kg IV or IM (maximum 250 mg), frequent saline irrigation, admit, and treat the mother and partner.
  6. How do you treat chlamydial disease?
    Oral erythromycin 50 mg/kg/day in four divided doses for 14 days (topical treatment alone is not enough because of pneumonitis risk).
  7. How is it prevented?
    Antenatal screening and treatment of the mother, and prophylactic povidone-iodine 2.5% or antibiotic ointment at birth in high-risk settings.

Station 27: Glaucomatous optic disc Glaucoma

Examiner: You are shown a fundus photo of a disc with a vertical cup-to-disc ratio of 0.8 and thinning of the inferior rim.

  1. What are the signs of glaucomatous cupping?
    Raised or asymmetric cup-to-disc ratio (asymmetry > 0.2), focal rim thinning or notching, violation of the ISNT rule, disc haemorrhage, bayonetting and baring of circumlinear vessels, laminar dot sign, peripapillary atrophy (beta zone) and RNFL defects.
  2. What is the ISNT rule?
    In a normal disc the neuroretinal rim is thickest Inferiorly, then Superiorly, Nasally and thinnest Temporally. Glaucoma usually thins the inferior and superior rims first.
  3. Describe the progression of visual field defects.
    Paracentral scotoma or nasal step, then arcuate (Bjerrum) scotoma, double arcuate (ring) scotoma, leaving a central island and temporal crescent in end stage.
  4. What risk factors for conversion did OHTS identify?
    Older age, higher IOP, thinner central corneal thickness, larger vertical cup-to-disc ratio and higher pattern standard deviation.
  5. How do you set a target IOP?
    Usually a 20–30% reduction from baseline, lower for advanced damage, younger age, faster progression or normal-tension glaucoma, and reviewed with structural and field progression.
  6. What is first-line treatment today?
    A prostaglandin analogue drop, or selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT) as first-line, supported by the LiGHT trial.
  7. What does a disc haemorrhage signify?
    A strong marker of progression, especially in normal-tension glaucoma. It usually resolves in weeks and recurs at the site of rim loss.

Station 28: Acute angle closure Glaucoma

Examiner: A 60-year-old woman presents with a painful red right eye, haloes, headache and vomiting since the evening. The pupil is mid-dilated and the cornea hazy.

  1. What is the diagnosis and how do you confirm it?
    Acute primary angle closure. Confirm with raised IOP (often > 40 mmHg) and a closed angle on gonioscopy (after clearing corneal oedema with glycerine if needed); check the fellow eye angle.
  2. What is the mechanism?
    Pupil block: relative resistance to aqueous flow at the pupil makes the peripheral iris bow forward and close the angle. Plateau iris and lens-induced (phacomorphic) mechanisms also occur.
  3. What is your immediate medical treatment?
    Systemic acetazolamide 500 mg (IV or oral) and, if needed, IV mannitol; topical beta-blocker, alpha-agonist and steroid; then pilocarpine 1–2% once the IOP has started to fall and the iris sphincter is no longer ischaemic. Lie the patient supine.
  4. What is the definitive treatment?
    Laser peripheral iridotomy in both eyes (the fellow eye has a high risk of attack). If the attack does not break or the cornea is too hazy, surgical iridectomy or lens extraction.
  5. What did the EAGLE trial show?
    Clear lens extraction gave better IOP control, fewer medications and better quality of life than laser iridotomy in primary angle closure and angle-closure glaucoma with IOP of 30 mmHg or more.
  6. Which drugs can precipitate angle closure?
    Dilating drops, anticholinergics (antihistamines, tricyclics, antispasmodics), adrenergic agonists, and topiramate (which causes bilateral angle closure by ciliary body swelling, not pupil block).
  7. How do you differentiate it from neovascular glaucoma?
    Neovascular glaucoma shows new vessels on the iris and angle, with a history of diabetes or vein occlusion; the angle is closed by fibrovascular membrane, not by iris bowing.

Station 29: Normal-tension glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A 58-year-old with migraine and cold hands has glaucomatous cupping and an arcuate field defect, but IOP never exceeds 18 mmHg.

  1. How do you define normal-tension glaucoma?
    Glaucomatous optic neuropathy with IOP consistently within the normal range (21 mmHg or less) and open angles.
  2. What must you exclude?
    Diurnal IOP spikes, thin corneas, previous steroid use, burnt-out pigmentary or steroid glaucoma, compressive lesions, previous ischaemic events and B12 deficiency.
  3. When do you order neuroimaging?
    Age under 50, rapid progression, pallor out of proportion to cupping, field defects respecting the vertical midline, or reduced central acuity or colour vision.
  4. Which vascular risk factors are associated?
    Migraine, Raynaud phenomenon, nocturnal hypotension, sleep apnoea and disc haemorrhages.
  5. What did the Collaborative NTG Study show?
    Lowering IOP by 30% slowed field progression, but about half of untreated patients did not progress over five years.
  6. How do you treat it?
    Aim for a 30% reduction with prostaglandin analogue or SLT; avoid aggressive antihypertensives at night; trabeculectomy if progression continues.
  7. What is the significance of disc haemorrhages here?
    They are more frequent in NTG and predict progression.

Station 30: Pseudoexfoliation glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A 70-year-old with an IOP of 34 mmHg in one eye and 18 in the other. There is flaky material on the lens and pupil margin.

  1. Why is IOP raised?
    Pseudoexfoliation material and pigment block the trabecular meshwork.
  2. How does it differ from POAG?
    Often unilateral or asymmetric, higher IOP with larger diurnal swings, faster progression and poorer response to drops.
  3. What gonioscopic signs are typical?
    Heavy irregular trabecular pigmentation and a Sampaolesi line.
  4. Which treatment options work well?
    Prostaglandin analogues first; SLT or ALT works particularly well; trabeculectomy or tube for progression; cataract surgery can lower IOP.
  5. What did the Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial find about pseudoexfoliation?
    Pseudoexfoliation was a strong risk factor for progression.
  6. Which genes are linked?
    LOXL1 variants, which are common in the general population, so testing is not useful clinically.
  7. What systemic associations have been reported?
    Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, hearing loss and abdominal aortic aneurysm, though causation is not proven.

Station 31: Pigment dispersion syndrome Glaucoma

Examiner: A 28-year-old myopic man notices blurred vision and haloes after playing football. Slit lamp shows a vertical spindle of pigment on the endothelium.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Pigment dispersion syndrome, which may progress to pigmentary glaucoma.
  2. What is the classic triad?
    Krukenberg spindle, mid-peripheral spoke-like iris transillumination defects, and dense homogeneous trabecular pigmentation.
  3. What is the mechanism?
    Reverse pupil block: a posteriorly bowed concave iris rubs on the zonules and releases pigment.
  4. Why are symptoms triggered by exercise?
    Exercise and pupil dilation release a shower of pigment that acutely raises IOP.
  5. How do you treat pigmentary glaucoma?
    Prostaglandin analogues and other drops, SLT (use lower energy as the pigmented meshwork absorbs more), and surgery if needed.
  6. Is laser iridotomy helpful?
    It flattens the iris, but trials have not shown that it prevents progression to glaucoma, so it is not routinely advised.
  7. Why might IOP improve with age?
    Lens growth and physiological miosis reduce iridozonular contact, so pigment release slows (burnt-out phase).

Station 32: Neovascular glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A man with an ischaemic CRVO three months ago now has a painful red eye, IOP 48 mmHg, and new vessels on the iris.

  1. Why does NVG develop after CRVO?
    Retinal ischaemia releases VEGF, which drives new vessels on the iris and angle; a fibrovascular membrane then closes the angle.
  2. What is the 100-day glaucoma?
    NVG classically appears around three months after ischaemic CRVO.
  3. Which other causes do you know?
    Proliferative diabetic retinopathy, ocular ischaemic syndrome (carotid stenosis), long-standing RD and tumours.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF to regress vessels quickly, full panretinal photocoagulation for the ischaemia, IOP-lowering drops and acetazolamide, cycloplegic and steroid.
  5. Which surgery do you choose if IOP remains high?
    A glaucoma drainage device (Ahmed or Baerveldt), or cyclophotocoagulation in an eye with poor visual potential. Trabeculectomy has a high failure rate.
  6. Which drops should you avoid?
    Pilocarpine and prostaglandins may worsen inflammation; miotics are avoided.
  7. Why must you gonioscope at-risk patients?
    Angle new vessels can appear before iris new vessels, giving a chance to treat before the angle closes.

Station 33: Primary congenital glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A 4-month-old with watering, photophobia, blepharospasm and large hazy corneas.

  1. What is the classic triad?
    Epiphora, photophobia and blepharospasm, with buphthalmos and corneal clouding.
  2. What are Haab striae?
    Horizontal or curvilinear breaks in Descemet membrane caused by corneal stretching (unlike vertical birth-trauma striae).
  3. Which gene is most often involved?
    CYP1B1, inherited autosomal recessively, common in consanguineous families.
  4. How do you examine the child?
    Examination under anaesthesia: IOP (lowered by most anaesthetics except ketamine), corneal diameter, axial length, gonioscopy and optic disc cupping.
  5. What is the treatment?
    Surgery: goniotomy (clear cornea) or trabeculotomy, or combined trabeculotomy-trabeculectomy, which is often preferred in India; drops are only a temporary measure.
  6. What corneal diameter is abnormal?
    More than 11 mm in a newborn or more than 12 mm in the first year should raise suspicion.
  7. Why is long-term follow-up essential?
    Amblyopia, myopia and anisometropia are common, and IOP can rise again years later.

Station 34: Trabeculectomy and the bleb Glaucoma

Examiner: Three weeks after trabeculectomy you see a flat bleb and IOP of 4 mmHg, with a shallow anterior chamber.

  1. What is the principle of trabeculectomy?
    A guarded partial-thickness scleral flap creates a fistula from the anterior chamber to a sub-conjunctival bleb.
  2. What are the causes of low IOP with a flat bleb?
    A wound leak (check with Seidel test), ciliary body shutdown, or choroidal detachment; overfiltration gives a low IOP with a high bleb.
  3. How do you manage a bleb leak?
    Pressure patch, bandage contact lens, aqueous suppressants, autologous blood or glue; surgical revision for persistent leaks.
  4. What antifibrotics are used and why?
    Mitomycin C or 5-fluorouracil reduce scarring and failure; MMC increases thin, avascular blebs and late leaks.
  5. What is blebitis?
    Infection of the bleb with a milky bleb and preserved vitreous; treat urgently with intensive topical antibiotics. Spread to the vitreous is bleb-related endophthalmitis and needs a tap and inject.
  6. How do you grade blebs?
    The Indiana or Moorfields bleb grading systems, describing height, extent, vascularity and leakage.
  7. What causes high IOP and a flat bleb with a deep chamber?
    Internal ostium block or flap fibrosis; options include massage, laser suture lysis, needling with 5-FU or MMC.

Station 35: Steroid-induced glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A 12-year-old with vernal keratoconjunctivitis has used over-the-counter dexamethasone drops for months. IOP is 38 mmHg with cupping.

  1. What is the mechanism?
    Steroids increase extracellular matrix deposition and reduce outflow through the trabecular meshwork.
  2. Who are steroid responders?
    About one-third of people show a moderate rise and 5% a high rise; risk is higher in POAG, first-degree relatives, high myopia, diabetes and children.
  3. Which routes raise IOP most?
    Intravitreal and periocular steroids and topical drops more than systemic; dexamethasone and prednisolone more than fluorometholone or loteprednol.
  4. When does IOP usually rise?
    Within 2 to 6 weeks of starting treatment, but it can be earlier or later.
  5. How do you manage it?
    Stop or reduce the steroid or switch to a weaker one, steroid-sparing therapy (cyclosporine or tacrolimus in VKC), IOP-lowering drops, SLT, and surgery if needed. A depot steroid may need removal.
  6. Why is it common in India?
    Steroid eye drops are easily available over the counter and are used for any red eye.
  7. What advice protects patients?
    Never use steroid drops without an eye doctor, and check IOP regularly during treatment.

Station 36: Aqueous misdirection Glaucoma

Examiner: The day after trabeculectomy for angle closure, the anterior chamber is uniformly flat and IOP is 40 mmHg despite a patent iridotomy.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Aqueous misdirection (malignant glaucoma): aqueous flows backwards into the vitreous, pushing the lens-iris diaphragm forward.
  2. How do you differentiate it from pupil block and choroidal haemorrhage?
    Pupil block has iris bombé with a deeper central chamber and resolves with a patent iridotomy; suprachoroidal haemorrhage shows dark choroidal elevations on fundus or B-scan.
  3. Who is at risk?
    Eyes with angle closure, small hyperopic eyes and nanophthalmos, especially after filtering or cataract surgery.
  4. What is the medical treatment?
    Intensive cycloplegia (atropine), aqueous suppressants, hyperosmotics, and avoiding miotics, which worsen it.
  5. What laser treatment helps in a pseudophakic eye?
    Nd:YAG disruption of the anterior hyaloid and posterior capsule to let aqueous pass forward.
  6. What is the definitive surgery?
    Pars plana vitrectomy with hyaloido-zonulo-iridectomy so the vitreous cavity communicates with the anterior chamber.
  7. Why is the fellow eye at risk?
    It shares the same anatomy, so plan prophylactic iridotomy and caution in its surgery.

Station 37: Uveitic glaucoma Glaucoma

Examiner: A 35-year-old with recurrent anterior uveitis has IOP 38 mmHg while on topical steroids, with mutton-fat keratic precipitates.

  1. What mechanisms raise IOP in uveitis?
    Open angle: trabeculitis, inflammatory cells and protein, and steroid response. Closed angle: peripheral anterior synechiae, posterior synechiae with iris bombé, and neovascularisation.
  2. How do you tell steroid response from inflammation?
    Steroid response usually starts 2–6 weeks after starting steroids with a quiet eye; inflammation-related rises occur with active cells. Sometimes both; try a lower-potency steroid such as loteprednol or fluorometholone.
  3. Which drops do you avoid?
    Prostaglandin analogues are traditionally used with caution (they may worsen inflammation and CME), and pilocarpine is avoided because it increases inflammation and synechiae. Beta-blockers, CAIs and alpha-agonists are first line.
  4. How do you treat iris bombé?
    Laser or surgical peripheral iridotomy (often needs to be large or repeated), with intensive anti-inflammatory treatment.
  5. Which syndromes cause high IOP with mild inflammation?
    Posner-Schlossman syndrome (glaucomatocyclitic crisis), Fuchs uveitis syndrome, and herpetic (HSV, VZV, CMV) anterior uveitis.
  6. What surgery is preferred?
    Control inflammation for at least 3 months if possible; trabeculectomy with mitomycin C has a higher failure rate, so glaucoma drainage devices are often chosen.
  7. Why does CMV matter?
    CMV anterior uveitis causes recurrent high IOP with few cells and endothelial damage in immunocompetent patients; aqueous PCR confirms it and ganciclovir treats it.

Station 38: Refractory glaucoma: drainage devices and cyclodestruction Glaucoma

Examiner: A patient with neovascular glaucoma after CRVO has IOP 45 mmHg on maximum medication and has had anti-VEGF.

  1. What makes glaucoma "refractory"?
    Failure of, or high risk of failure with, trabeculectomy: neovascular, uveitic, post-keratoplasty, iridocorneal endothelial syndrome, previous failed trabeculectomy, aphakia, and extensive conjunctival scarring.
  2. What types of glaucoma drainage device are there?
    Valved (Ahmed) and non-valved (Baerveldt, Molteno, and the low-cost Aurolab aqueous drainage implant made in India).
  3. What did the ABC and AVB studies show?
    Baerveldt implants gave lower long-term IOP and fewer failures, with more hypotony-related complications; the Ahmed gives immediate IOP control.
  4. Why must a non-valved tube be restricted initially?
    To prevent early hypotony before a fibrous capsule forms around the plate; use a ligature or an internal ripcord stent.
  5. What are the complications of tubes?
    Hypotony, tube exposure, tube–cornea touch with corneal decompensation, plate encapsulation (hypertensive phase with Ahmed), diplopia and endophthalmitis.
  6. What is cyclophotocoagulation?
    Destruction of the ciliary body with diode laser (transscleral continuous wave or micropulse) or endoscopically, to reduce aqueous production.
  7. When do you choose cyclodestruction?
    In eyes with poor visual potential, painful blind eyes, or when surgery is unsuitable; micropulse is gentler and can be repeated, but phthisis is a risk with aggressive treatment.

Station 39: Diabetic retinopathy Retina

Examiner: A 52-year-old with type 2 diabetes for 12 years. The fundus shows haemorrhages in four quadrants, venous beading in two, and new vessels at the disc.

  1. Classify this retinopathy.
    Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (new vessels at the disc). The NPDR features alone would make it very severe NPDR (two or more criteria of the 4-2-1 rule).
  2. What is the 4-2-1 rule?
    Severe NPDR: haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants, or venous beading in 2 or more quadrants, or IRMA in 1 or more quadrant. Two or more of these make very severe NPDR.
  3. What are high-risk characteristics (DRS)?
    NVD ≥ one-quarter to one-third disc area; any NVD with vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage; or NVE ≥ half disc area with haemorrhage.
  4. How do you treat PDR?
    Panretinal photocoagulation, or anti-VEGF injections as an alternative (DRCR Protocol S showed ranibizumab was non-inferior at 2 years), particularly with coexisting DME. Vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage or tractional detachment involving the macula.
  5. What was clinically significant macular oedema (ETDRS)?
    Retinal thickening within 500 µm of the foveal centre; hard exudates within 500 µm with adjacent thickening; or thickening of one disc area or more, any part of which is within one disc diameter of the centre. Today we classify DME on OCT as centre-involving or not.
  6. What is first-line treatment for centre-involving DME with vision loss?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF. Protocol T: with vision 20/50 or worse, aflibercept gave better vision than bevacizumab at 1 year.
  7. When should screening start?
    Type 2: at diagnosis. Type 1: within 5 years of diagnosis (usually from puberty). Pregnancy: early in each trimester for women with pre-existing diabetes.

Station 40: Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment Retina

Examiner: A 55-year-old myope reports flashes, a shower of floaters and a curtain coming up from below in the left eye for two days.

  1. What sign do you look for in the anterior vitreous?
    Shafer's sign (tobacco dust): pigment cells in the anterior vitreous, highly suggestive of a retinal break.
  2. Where would you look for the break (Lincoff rules)?
    A superior temporal or nasal detachment: the break is within 1.5 clock hours of its highest border in 98%. A total or superior detachment crossing 12 o'clock: break at 12 or within a triangle 1.5 clock hours either side. An inferior detachment: the higher side shows which side of the disc the break is on. Inferior bullae arise from a superior break.
  3. How do you tell rhegmatogenous from tractional and exudative detachment?
    Rhegmatogenous: convex, corrugated, mobile, with a break and tobacco dust. Tractional: concave, immobile, with fibrovascular bands. Exudative: smooth, shifting fluid that moves with posture, no break.
  4. What are the surgical options?
    Pneumatic retinopexy (single superior break in the upper two-thirds; PIVOT trial), scleral buckle (young phakic patients, dialysis, no PVD), or pars plana vitrectomy (pseudophakic eyes, posterior or multiple breaks, giant tears, vitreous haemorrhage, PVR).
  5. Why does macular status matter?
    Macula-on detachments need urgent surgery to save central vision; for macula-off, visual outcome depends on how long the macula has been detached.
  6. What is PVR and how is it graded?
    Proliferative vitreoretinopathy: membranes forming on and under the retina, the main cause of surgical failure. Grade A (haze, pigment clumps), B (rolled edges, wrinkling), C (full-thickness fixed folds), with posterior or anterior location.
  7. Which tamponade agents do you know?
    Air, SF6 (lasts about 2 weeks), C3F8 (about 8 weeks) and silicone oil (removed later). Patients must not fly or have nitrous oxide anaesthesia with gas in the eye.

Station 41: Central retinal vein occlusion Retina

Examiner: A 66-year-old hypertensive with sudden painless blurring in one eye. The fundus shows haemorrhages in all four quadrants, dilated tortuous veins and disc swelling.

  1. How do you classify CRVO?
    Non-ischaemic (perfused) and ischaemic. Ischaemic features include poor acuity (often worse than 6/60), RAPD, extensive cotton-wool spots, more than 10 disc areas of non-perfusion on FFA and reduced ERG b-wave.
  2. What systemic work-up do you do?
    Blood pressure, glucose and HbA1c, lipids, full blood count and ESR. In younger patients add a thrombophilia and hyperviscosity screen.
  3. Which ocular risk factor must you check?
    Raised IOP and glaucoma, in both eyes.
  4. How do you treat macular oedema?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab, faricimab), with dexamethasone implant as second line or in pseudophakic patients.
  5. When do you do panretinal photocoagulation?
    When neovascularisation of the iris, angle or retina appears; not prophylactically, but with close monthly follow-up in ischaemic eyes for the first months.
  6. What did the CVOS show?
    Prophylactic PRP did not prevent NVI reliably; treat promptly when new vessels appear. Grid laser did not improve vision in CRVO macular oedema.
  7. What is the prognosis?
    Initial acuity is the best predictor; about one-third of non-ischaemic CRVOs convert to ischaemic.

Station 42: Branch retinal vein occlusion Retina

Examiner: A 60-year-old with blurred vision has a wedge of flame haemorrhages in the superotemporal quadrant arising at an arteriovenous crossing.

  1. Why does BRVO occur at AV crossings?
    The artery and vein share an adventitial sheath, so a thickened sclerotic artery compresses the vein and causes turbulence and thrombosis.
  2. Which quadrant is most commonly affected?
    Superotemporal, because it has the most AV crossings.
  3. What are the main causes of visual loss?
    Macular oedema, macular ischaemia and vitreous haemorrhage from neovascularisation.
  4. What did the BVOS show?
    Grid laser improved vision in macular oedema persisting for 3 months with vision 6/12 or worse, and scatter laser reduced vitreous haemorrhage once new vessels developed.
  5. What is first-line treatment for macular oedema now?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF, which gave better vision than sham injection with rescue grid laser (BRAVO trial).
  6. When do you laser the ischaemic area?
    Sectoral scatter laser when retinal or disc new vessels develop.
  7. Which systemic diseases do you check?
    Hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and smoking.

Station 43: Central retinal artery occlusion Retina

Examiner: A 70-year-old woke with sudden painless loss of vision to counting fingers. There is a cherry-red spot and a pale retina.

  1. Why is there a cherry-red spot?
    The inner retina becomes opaque and white from ischaemia, while the thin foveola lets the normal choroid show through.
  2. Why may some vision be preserved?
    A cilioretinal artery, present in about 15 to 30% of people, can supply the papillomacular bundle.
  3. What is the most urgent systemic question?
    Is this giant cell arteritis? Check ESR, CRP and symptoms (jaw claudication, headache, scalp tenderness) immediately in anyone over 50.
  4. Why is CRAO a stroke equivalent?
    It is usually embolic from carotid or cardiac disease, and carries a high risk of cerebral stroke in the following weeks; refer immediately to a stroke unit.
  5. What investigations follow?
    Carotid Doppler, ECG and echocardiogram, blood pressure, lipids, glucose and brain imaging.
  6. Do conservative treatments work?
    Ocular massage, AC paracentesis and IOP lowering have no proven benefit; IV thrombolysis showed no visual benefit in the THEIA (alteplase) and TenCRAOS (tenecteplase) trials, so it is not standard care. Urgent stroke work-up is the priority.
  7. What late complication can occur?
    Neovascularisation of the iris and neovascular glaucoma, usually within weeks.

Station 44: Neovascular AMD Retina

Examiner: A 74-year-old reports distortion of straight lines for two weeks. There is a grey-green subretinal lesion with haemorrhage at the macula.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration with choroidal neovascularisation (macular neovascularisation).
  2. How do you classify macular neovascularisation?
    Type 1 (under the RPE, occult), type 2 (above the RPE, classic) and type 3 (retinal angiomatous proliferation); polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy is a variant of type 1.
  3. Which investigations do you do?
    OCT (fluid, PED, hyper-reflective material), OCT angiography, and FFA or ICG (ICG for polyps).
  4. What is the treatment?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF: ranibizumab, aflibercept 2 or 8 mg, bevacizumab, brolucizumab or faricimab, given monthly then on treat-and-extend.
  5. What did the AREDS trials show?
    AREDS: the antioxidant and zinc formula reduced progression to advanced AMD by about 25% in people with intermediate AMD or advanced AMD in one eye. AREDS2: lutein and zeaxanthin can replace beta-carotene (which raised lung cancer risk in smokers), and omega-3 added no benefit.
  6. Why should smokers not take beta-carotene?
    It increased lung cancer risk, which is why AREDS2 replaced it with lutein and zeaxanthin.
  7. What is the role of the Amsler grid?
    Home monitoring of the fellow eye for new distortion, prompting an urgent review.

Station 45: Geographic atrophy Retina

Examiner: An 80-year-old with slowly worsening reading vision has sharply demarcated areas of RPE loss through which large choroidal vessels are visible.

  1. What is geographic atrophy?
    The advanced dry form of AMD: well-defined areas of RPE, photoreceptor and choriocapillaris loss.
  2. How do you image it?
    Fundus autofluorescence (dark areas, with hyperautofluorescent margins predicting growth) and OCT (complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy, cRORA).
  3. Why does reading suffer before acuity?
    Atrophy often spares the fovea initially and surrounds it, so central acuity is preserved while the reading field is lost.
  4. Which treatments are approved?
    Complement inhibitors: pegcetacoplan (C3) and avacincaptad pegol (C5), given intravitreally, slow growth of atrophy by about 20% but do not improve vision.
  5. What risks do these drugs carry?
    Conversion to neovascular AMD, intraocular inflammation and, with pegcetacoplan, rare occlusive retinal vasculitis.
  6. Which genes are linked to AMD?
    Complement factor H (CFH), ARMS2/HTRA1 and C3.
  7. How do you help these patients now?
    Low vision aids, magnification and eccentric viewing training, good lighting, smoking cessation and AREDS2 supplements where indicated.

Station 46: Central serous chorioretinopathy Retina

Examiner: A 38-year-old stressed executive notices a dim central patch and micropsia in one eye. OCT shows subretinal fluid under the fovea.

  1. Which risk factors do you ask about?
    Corticosteroids by any route (including skin creams and inhalers), type A personality and stress, pregnancy, Cushing syndrome and phosphodiesterase inhibitors.
  2. What does FFA show?
    A focal leak expanding as an ink-blot or, less often, a smoke-stack pattern.
  3. What does ICG show and why does it matter?
    Choroidal hyperpermeability; it guides photodynamic therapy and suggests the pachychoroid spectrum.
  4. What is the natural history?
    Most acute cases resolve within 3 months; chronic disease causes RPE atrophy and permanent loss.
  5. How do you treat persistent or chronic CSCR?
    Half-dose or half-fluence photodynamic therapy with verteporfin, which was better than micropulse laser in the PLACE trial; focal laser for an extrafoveal leak.
  6. Do eplerenone or spironolactone work?
    The VICI trial found no benefit of eplerenone over placebo for chronic CSCR.
  7. What complication may need anti-VEGF?
    Secondary choroidal neovascularisation (pachychoroid neovasculopathy).

Station 47: Macular hole Retina

Examiner: A 67-year-old woman notices a central gap in her vision. OCT shows a full-thickness foveal defect of 300 microns with vitreous still attached at its edges.

  1. How do you stage it?
    International Vitreomacular Traction Study classification: small (250 microns or less), medium (250 to 400) or large (over 400), with or without vitreomacular traction. Gass stages 1 to 4 are the older system.
  2. What is the Watzke-Allen test?
    A thin slit beam across the hole: the patient sees a break in the line in a full-thickness hole.
  3. What is the treatment?
    Pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peel and gas tamponade (SF6 or C2F6), with face-down or reading positioning in some cases.
  4. Which factors predict closure?
    Smaller diameter, shorter duration and a smaller minimum linear diameter; large chronic holes may need an inverted ILM flap.
  5. What is the role of ocriplasmin?
    Pharmacological vitreolysis for small holes with VMT; rarely used now because of modest success and side effects.
  6. What happens to the lens after vitrectomy?
    Nuclear sclerotic cataract develops in most phakic eyes within two years.
  7. How do you tell a lamellar hole from a full-thickness hole?
    A lamellar hole has an irregular foveal contour with intraretinal splitting but intact photoreceptor layer.

Station 48: Retinopathy of prematurity Retina

Examiner: You are asked to screen a baby born at 29 weeks weighing 1100 g, now four weeks old, on supplemental oxygen.

  1. Who should be screened in India?
    Babies born under 34 weeks or under 2000 g, and older or heavier babies with an unstable neonatal course (sepsis, oxygen, transfusions).
  2. When should the first examination be?
    By day 20 to 30 of life, or 31 weeks postmenstrual age, whichever is later; in India by day 20 to catch aggressive disease.

    Zone (I to III, with posterior zone II), stage (1 demarcation line to 5 total detachment), extent in clock hours, plus disease, and aggressive ROP (A-ROP).
  4. What is plus disease?
    Dilated, tortuous posterior pole vessels in at least two quadrants, indicating active disease that usually needs treatment.
  5. What is type 1 ROP and how do you treat it?
    Zone I any stage with plus, zone I stage 3, or zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus (ETROP); treat within 48 to 72 hours with laser to avascular retina or intravitreal anti-VEGF.
  6. Advantages and disadvantages of anti-VEGF?
    Good for zone I and A-ROP and less myopia, but needs long follow-up for late reactivation and has uncertain systemic effects.
  7. What long-term problems follow?
    Myopia, strabismus, amblyopia, retinal detachment and glaucoma.

Station 49: Diabetic macular oedema Retina

Examiner: A 55-year-old diabetic with 6/18 vision. OCT shows central thickening of 450 microns with cysts and hard exudates near the fovea.

  1. How do you classify DME today?
    By OCT: centre-involving or non-centre-involving. The older term clinically significant macular oedema came from ETDRS.

    Intravitreal anti-VEGF. Protocol T found aflibercept better than bevacizumab when vision was 6/15 or worse.

    Pseudophakic eyes, poor responders to anti-VEGF, patients who cannot attend frequently, and recent cardiovascular events.

Station 50: Post-operative endophthalmitis Retina

Examiner: Three days after cataract surgery, a patient has pain, hand-movement vision, a hypopyon and no red reflex.

  1. What is the diagnosis and the commonest organism?
    Acute post-operative endophthalmitis; coagulase-negative staphylococci are commonest.
  2. What do you do immediately?
    Same-day vitreous tap (or vitrectomy) for culture, and intravitreal vancomycin 1 mg/0.1 mL and ceftazidime 2.25 mg/0.1 mL (amikacin 0.4 mg if allergic).
  3. What did the EVS show?
    Immediate vitrectomy was better than tap and inject only in eyes with light-perception vision; systemic antibiotics added no benefit.
  4. Is the EVS still applied rigidly?
    Many surgeons now do early vitrectomy at lower thresholds because modern small-gauge vitrectomy is safer.
  5. What does intracameral antibiotic prophylaxis achieve?
    The ESCRS study showed intracameral cefuroxime reduced endophthalmitis about fivefold; moxifloxacin is widely used in India.
  6. What causes chronic post-operative endophthalmitis?
    Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes, with a white plaque in the capsular bag and granulomatous inflammation months later.
  7. How do you prevent it?
    Povidone-iodine 5% to the conjunctival sac and 10% to the skin, lid hygiene, sterile draping of lashes, and intracameral antibiotic.

Station 51: Retinitis pigmentosa Retina

Examiner: A 25-year-old with night blindness since childhood and tunnel vision. The fundus shows bone-spicule pigmentation, attenuated arterioles and a waxy pale disc.

  1. What is the classic triad?
    Bone-spicule pigmentation, arteriolar attenuation and waxy disc pallor.
  2. Which inheritance patterns occur?
    Autosomal dominant (best prognosis), autosomal recessive (commonest) and X-linked (worst prognosis, RPGR).

    Reduced or extinguished rod responses early, then cone responses, with delayed implicit times.

    Cystoid macular oedema (carbonic anhydrase inhibitors) and posterior subcapsular cataract (surgery).
  7. What gene therapy is available?
    Voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna) for biallelic RPE65 mutations.

Station 52: Choroidal melanoma Retina

Examiner: A 60-year-old has a pigmented dome-shaped choroidal lesion 4 mm thick with orange pigment and subretinal fluid.

  1. Which features suggest melanoma rather than a naevus?
    TFSOM-UHHD: thickness over 2 mm, subretinal fluid, symptoms, orange pigment, margin near the disc, ultrasound hollowness, absent halo and absent drusen.

    Tebentafusp improves survival in HLA-A*02:01-positive metastatic uveal melanoma.

Station 53: Eales disease Retina

Examiner: A healthy 25-year-old man from India has sudden floaters in one eye with vitreous haemorrhage; the other eye shows peripheral vein sheathing.

  1. What is Eales disease?
    An idiopathic occlusive retinal periphlebitis of young, otherwise healthy adults (mainly men from the Indian subcontinent), with peripheral non-perfusion and neovascularisation.
  2. What are its three stages?
    Inflammation (periphlebitis), ischaemia (peripheral non-perfusion), and proliferation (neovascularisation with vitreous haemorrhage and tractional detachment).
  3. What is the link with tuberculosis?
    A hypersensitivity to Mycobacterium tuberculosis proteins is suspected; a positive Mantoux or IGRA is common, and some patients have TB DNA in vitreous on PCR.
  4. Which investigations do you do?
    Wide-field FFA (peripheral non-perfusion and leakage), chest X-ray, Mantoux or IGRA, and tests to exclude sarcoidosis, Behçet disease, sickle cell disease and diabetes.
  5. How do you treat the inflammatory stage?
    Oral corticosteroids (and periocular steroids); anti-tubercular therapy is considered when there is evidence of TB.
  6. How do you treat the proliferative stage?
    Scatter laser photocoagulation to the non-perfused retina, anti-VEGF as an adjunct, and vitrectomy for non-clearing haemorrhage or tractional detachment.
  7. What is the differential diagnosis?
    Sarcoidosis, Behçet, tuberculosis, sickle cell retinopathy, BRVO, diabetic retinopathy and radiation retinopathy.

Station 54: Hypertensive retinopathy Retina

Examiner: A 40-year-old man with headache has BP 220/130 mmHg, disc swelling, flame haemorrhages, cotton wool spots and a macular star.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Malignant (accelerated) hypertension with grade 4 hypertensive retinopathy (Keith-Wagener-Barker) or severe retinopathy (Wong-Mitchell).
  2. What is the Wong-Mitchell classification?
    Mild: arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping and copper wiring; moderate: haemorrhages, microaneurysms, cotton wool spots and exudates; malignant: moderate signs plus disc swelling.
  3. Is this an emergency?
    Yes: admit under medicine for controlled blood pressure lowering and look for kidney, heart and brain damage.
  4. Why should blood pressure not be lowered too fast?
    Sudden falls can cause ischaemic optic neuropathy and stroke, because autoregulation has reset; aim for about a 25% reduction over the first hours.
  5. What is hypertensive choroidopathy?
    Elschnig spots (choroidal infarcts), Siegrist streaks and serous retinal detachment, seen in acute hypertension such as pre-eclampsia.
  6. What else must you exclude in a young patient?
    Secondary hypertension: kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, phaeochromocytoma, Conn syndrome and coarctation of the aorta.
  7. What is the visual prognosis?
    Usually good when BP is controlled; exudates take months to clear, and optic atrophy can follow if the disc was involved.

Station 55: Myopic maculopathy Retina

Examiner: A 50-year-old with −14.00 D myopia has sudden distortion and a small grey lesion with haemorrhage at the fovea.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Myopic choroidal neovascularisation (myopic CNV).
  2. How is myopic maculopathy classified (META-PM)?
    Category 0 no lesions; 1 tessellated fundus; 2 diffuse chorioretinal atrophy; 3 patchy atrophy; 4 macular atrophy; plus lesions: lacquer cracks, myopic CNV and Fuchs spot.
  3. What are lacquer cracks?
    Yellowish linear breaks in Bruch membrane; they can bleed (simple haemorrhage without CNV) and predispose to CNV.
  4. How do you confirm myopic CNV?
    OCT (type 2 CNV above the RPE with fluid that is often minimal) and OCT angiography or FFA (early leakage).
  5. How do you treat it?
    Intravitreal anti-VEGF, often fewer injections than in AMD (a 1+PRN regimen: one injection, then as needed).
  6. What other complications occur in high myopia?
    Myopic traction maculopathy (foveoschisis), macular hole and detachment, dome-shaped macula, posterior staphyloma, retinal detachment, glaucoma and early cataract.
  7. How do you counsel such a patient?
    Use an Amsler grid at home, report new distortion urgently, and have regular dilated examinations; in children, myopia control reduces the lifetime risk.

Station 56: Epiretinal membrane and vitreomacular traction Retina

Examiner: A 68-year-old complains that straight lines look wavy in the right eye; acuity is 6/18 and the macula looks wrinkled.

  1. What is your diagnosis?
    Epiretinal membrane (macular pucker): a fibrocellular membrane on the inner retinal surface that contracts.
  2. What causes it?
    Most are idiopathic after posterior vitreous detachment; secondary ERMs follow retinal detachment surgery, tears, laser, vascular disease and uveitis.
  3. What does OCT show?
    A hyperreflective line on the retinal surface, loss of the foveal contour, retinal thickening, and sometimes ectopic inner foveal layers (a sign of worse prognosis).
  4. When do you operate?
    For symptomatic distortion or reduced vision affecting daily life; there is no fixed acuity threshold.
  5. What surgery is done?
    Pars plana vitrectomy with peeling of the membrane, usually with internal limiting membrane peeling (stained with dyes such as brilliant blue).
  6. What is vitreomacular traction?
    Incomplete posterior vitreous detachment with persistent adhesion at the fovea pulling on it; can resolve spontaneously, or be treated with vitrectomy or pneumatic vitreolysis.
  7. What are the risks of surgery?
    Cataract (very common in phakic eyes), retinal tears and detachment, recurrence, endophthalmitis and limited recovery of distortion.

Station 57: Acute anterior uveitis Uvea

Examiner: A 30-year-old man with a painful red photophobic eye and low back stiffness in the mornings. There are fine KPs, 2+ cells and a small hypopyon.

  1. What is the likely association?
    HLA-B27-associated acute anterior uveitis, here with ankylosing spondylitis (inflammatory back pain). Fibrinous reaction and hypopyon are typical.
  2. Granulomatous vs non-granulomatous: how do you tell?
    Granulomatous: large greasy mutton-fat KPs, iris nodules (Koeppe at the pupil margin, Busacca on the iris surface) and chronic course (sarcoid, TB, VKH, syphilis). Non-granulomatous: fine KPs, acute course (HLA-B27).
  3. How do you grade anterior chamber cells (SUN)?
    Cells in a 1 mm × 1 mm slit beam: 0 (< 1), 0.5+ (1–5), 1+ (6–15), 2+ (16–25), 3+ (26–50), 4+ (> 50).
  4. How do you treat it?
    Frequent topical steroid (prednisolone acetate 1%) with a cycloplegic (homatropine or cyclopentolate, atropine if severe) to relieve ciliary spasm and break or prevent posterior synechiae. Taper steroids slowly.
  5. Which investigations would you order?
    For a first, typical unilateral HLA-B27 picture: HLA-B27 and sacroiliac imaging. For recurrent, bilateral, granulomatous or atypical uveitis: syphilis serology, chest X-ray or CT, ACE and lysozyme, Mantoux or IGRA, and others guided by history.
  6. What are the complications?
    Posterior synechiae, cataract, secondary glaucoma (inflammatory or steroid-induced), cystoid macular oedema, band keratopathy (chronic, especially in JIA) and hypotony.
  7. Which systemic drug is approved as steroid-sparing for non-infectious uveitis?
    Adalimumab (VISUAL I and II; SYCAMORE for JIA uveitis with methotrexate).

Station 58: Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada disease Uvea

Examiner: A 32-year-old woman with headache and tinnitus followed by bilateral blurring. There are multiple serous retinal detachments and disc hyperaemia.

  1. What is VKH?
    A bilateral granulomatous panuveitis caused by autoimmunity against melanocytes, more common in pigmented races.
  2. What are the phases?
    Prodromal (headache, meningism, tinnitus), acute uveitic (exudative detachments), convalescent (sunset-glow fundus, vitiligo, poliosis) and chronic recurrent.
  3. What must you exclude by history?
    Penetrating injury or eye surgery, which would suggest sympathetic ophthalmia.
  4. What does FFA show?
    Multiple pinpoint leaks in the acute phase that pool into the detachments (starry sky), with disc leakage.
  5. What do OCT and ICG show?
    Subretinal fluid with septae and a thickened choroid on OCT; hypofluorescent dark dots on ICG.
  6. How do you treat it?
    Early high-dose systemic steroid (often IV methylprednisolone then oral prednisolone tapered over at least 6 months) with early immunosuppressives to prevent chronic disease.
  7. What are the late complications?
    Cataract, glaucoma, choroidal neovascularisation and subretinal fibrosis.

Station 59: Behçet disease Uvea

Examiner: A 28-year-old man with recurrent mouth ulcers and genital ulcers has a hypopyon in one eye and retinal infiltrates with vasculitis.

  1. What are the diagnostic criteria?
    ICBD scoring: oral ulcers, genital ulcers, ocular lesions, skin lesions, neurological signs, vascular lesions and a positive pathergy test.
  2. What type of uveitis is typical?
    A non-granulomatous panuveitis with an occlusive necrotising retinal vasculitis; the hypopyon shifts with head position and the eye may be quiet.
  3. Which HLA is associated?
    HLA-B51, common along the old Silk Road.
  4. How do you treat posterior Behçet?
    Steroids plus azathioprine; anti-TNF agents (infliximab or adalimumab) or interferon-alpha for sight-threatening or resistant disease.
  5. Which drug should you avoid?
    Cyclosporine in patients with neuro-Behçet, as it can worsen neurological disease.
  6. Why is prognosis guarded?
    Recurrent occlusive vasculitis causes retinal ischaemia, optic atrophy and blindness, especially in young men.
  7. What is the pathergy test?
    A pustule appearing 24 to 48 hours after a sterile needle prick of the skin.

Station 60: Ocular toxoplasmosis Uvea

Examiner: A 22-year-old with floaters has a fluffy white focus of retinitis next to an old pigmented scar, with dense vitritis.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Recurrent ocular toxoplasmosis: active retinochoroiditis adjacent to an old scar (satellite lesion), with a headlight-in-the-fog appearance.
  2. How is it acquired?
    Undercooked meat, cat faeces or contaminated water and vegetables, and congenitally across the placenta.
  3. When do you treat?
    Lesions near the macula or disc, near a major vessel, large lesions, severe vitritis, and immunocompromised or pregnant patients.
  4. Which regimens are used?
    Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole twice daily, or pyrimethamine with sulfadiazine and folinic acid, or intravitreal clindamycin with dexamethasone; oral steroid added after 24 to 48 hours.
  5. Why not give steroids alone?
    They allow uncontrolled parasite proliferation and fulminant retinitis.
  6. How do you treat a pregnant woman?
    Spiramycin in the first trimester (avoid pyrimethamine, which is teratogenic); coordinate with obstetrics.
  7. How can recurrences be reduced?
    Long-term trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole every 2 or 3 days reduced recurrences in trials.

Station 61: CMV retinitis Uvea

Examiner: A man with advanced HIV (CD4 count 30) has floaters. The fundus shows a granular white retinitis with haemorrhages along the vessels.

  1. What is the typical appearance?
    Pizza-pie or cottage-cheese-and-ketchup: white granular necrosis with haemorrhages along vessels; a quieter granular form spreads at the edges.
  2. At what CD4 count does it occur?
    Usually under 50 cells per microlitre.
  3. How do you treat it?
    Oral valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily for induction, with intravitreal ganciclovir 2 mg for sight-threatening lesions, then maintenance; restart antiretroviral therapy.
  4. What is immune recovery uveitis?
    Inflammation, CME and ERM when antiretrovirals restore immunity in eyes with previous CMV retinitis.
  5. When can maintenance be stopped?
    When CD4 counts stay above 100 for 3 to 6 months on antiretrovirals and the retinitis is inactive.
  6. What complication causes late visual loss?
    Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment through atrophic retina, often needing vitrectomy with silicone oil.
  7. Who else is at risk now?
    Transplant and chemotherapy patients, and those on biologics.

Station 62: Sympathetic ophthalmia Uvea

Examiner: Six weeks after an open globe injury to the left eye, the patient develops blurring and photophobia in the uninjured right eye.

  1. What is sympathetic ophthalmia?
    A bilateral granulomatous panuveitis after penetrating trauma or surgery to one eye, caused by autoimmunity to uveal antigens.
  2. What are the terms for the two eyes?
    The exciting eye (injured) and the sympathising eye (fellow).
  3. What fundus signs occur?
    Dalen-Fuchs nodules (yellow-white spots of epithelioid cells between RPE and Bruch membrane) and exudative detachments.
  4. When does it occur?
    Usually within 3 months of the injury (80% within a year), but it can appear decades later.
  5. How do you treat it?
    High-dose systemic steroid plus early steroid-sparing immunosuppression.
  6. Is enucleation of the exciting eye helpful?
    Enucleation within 2 weeks of injury may prevent it, but once SO has started, removing an eye with useful vision is not advised.
  7. Which surgery commonly triggers it today?
    Vitreoretinal surgery is now a major cause, along with trauma.

Station 63: JIA-associated uveitis Uvea

Examiner: A 5-year-old girl with oligoarticular arthritis and positive ANA is referred for screening. Her eyes are white but there are cells in the anterior chamber.

  1. Why is this uveitis dangerous?
    It is chronic, painless and white, so it is missed until band keratopathy, cataract or glaucoma appear.
  2. Which children are at highest risk?
    Oligoarticular JIA, ANA positive, onset under 6 years, and within 4 years of arthritis onset.
  3. How often do you screen?
    High-risk children every 3 months, decreasing over time; screening continues for years after arthritis onset.
  4. What is the classic triad of complications?
    Band keratopathy, cataract and glaucoma (with posterior synechiae).
  5. How do you treat it?
    Topical steroid, then methotrexate as first steroid-sparing drug, then adalimumab (supported by SYCAMORE).
  6. Why is cataract surgery risky in these children?
    High risk of inflammation, membranes and hypotony; the eye must be quiet for at least 3 months, often with no IOL in young children.
  7. How is it different from HLA-B27 uveitis in children?
    Enthesitis-related arthritis gives acute, painful, red unilateral anterior uveitis.

Station 64: Tubercular uveitis Uvea

Examiner: A 40-year-old from an endemic area has granulomatous anterior uveitis with broad posterior synechiae and serpiginous-like choroiditis.

  1. Which ocular signs suggest TB?
    Broad-based posterior synechiae, granulomatous KPs, serpiginous-like choroiditis, choroidal tubercles or tuberculoma, occlusive retinal vasculitis.
  2. Which tests do you use?
    Interferon-gamma release assay (QuantiFERON) or tuberculin skin test, chest imaging (CT more sensitive), and PCR of ocular fluid where possible.
  3. How is the diagnosis usually made?
    Presumed: a compatible clinical picture with evidence of TB infection and exclusion of other causes; the COTS group gives guidance on when to start ATT.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Antitubercular therapy for at least 6 to 9 months with corticosteroids to control inflammation.
  5. What is a paradoxical reaction?
    Temporary worsening after starting ATT due to an immune response to released antigens.
  6. Which drug in ATT is toxic to the eye?
    Ethambutol causes optic neuropathy (dose-related, check vision and colour at baseline and monthly); rifabutin causes uveitis.
  7. What is Eales disease?
    An idiopathic occlusive periphlebitis in young Indian men, associated with TB hypersensitivity.

Station 65: Scleritis Uvea

Examiner: A 55-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis has a deep, boring eye pain that wakes her at night and a violaceous red eye.

  1. How do you tell scleritis from episcleritis?
    Scleritis is painful and tender with a bluish-red hue; deep vessels do not move or blanch with phenylephrine 10%. Episcleritis is mildly uncomfortable and blanches.
  2. How do you classify scleritis?
    Anterior (diffuse, nodular, necrotising with inflammation, scleromalacia perforans) and posterior.
  3. Which systemic diseases do you look for?
    Rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (ANCA), relapsing polychondritis, SLE, IBD and infections such as herpes zoster, TB and syphilis.
  4. How do you treat it?
    Oral NSAIDs for diffuse or nodular disease, systemic steroids, then immunosuppressives or rituximab for necrotising or ANCA-associated disease.
  5. Why is necrotising scleritis an emergency?
    It signals active systemic vasculitis with high mortality if untreated, and can perforate.
  6. How does posterior scleritis present?
    Pain, choroidal folds, exudative detachment and disc swelling; B-scan shows thickened sclera with fluid in Tenon space (T-sign).
  7. What is scleromalacia perforans?
    Painless scleral thinning in long-standing rheumatoid arthritis, without inflammation.

Station 66: Intermediate uveitis Uvea

Examiner: A 20-year-old has bilateral floaters; there are vitreous cells, snowballs and inferior snowbanking.

  1. What is intermediate uveitis?
    Inflammation mainly in the vitreous and peripheral retina (pars plana); pars planitis is the idiopathic form with snowbanks or snowballs.
  2. Which systemic diseases are associated?
    Multiple sclerosis (check for neurological symptoms and HLA-DR15), sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, syphilis, Lyme disease and lymphoma in older patients.
  3. What causes loss of vision?
    Cystoid macular oedema (the main cause), vitreous opacities, cataract, epiretinal membrane, glaucoma, vasculitis and neovascularisation.
  4. How do you investigate?
    Chest X-ray or CT, serum ACE, Mantoux or IGRA, syphilis serology, and MRI brain if there are neurological symptoms; OCT for CME and FFA for vasculitis.
  5. How do you treat it?
    Treat if vision is affected (usually CME): periocular or intravitreal steroid (including dexamethasone implant), then oral steroids and steroid-sparing drugs or adalimumab for bilateral or chronic disease.
  6. Which drug class do you avoid in MS-associated uveitis?
    Anti-TNF drugs can worsen demyelination, so are avoided in patients with MS.
  7. What is the role of peripheral laser or vitrectomy?
    Laser to snowbanks with neovascularisation, and vitrectomy for persistent opacities, complications or diagnostic biopsy.

Station 67: Acute retinal necrosis Uvea

Examiner: A healthy 45-year-old has a painful red eye with vitritis and confluent peripheral white retinitis with occlusive arteritis.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Acute retinal necrosis (ARN): a necrotising herpetic retinitis, usually VZV or HSV, in immunocompetent people.
  2. What are the diagnostic criteria?
    One or more foci of peripheral retinal necrosis with discrete borders, rapid circumferential spread without treatment, occlusive vasculopathy with arteritis, and marked vitritis and anterior chamber inflammation (American Uveitis Society).
  3. How do you confirm it?
    Aqueous or vitreous tap for PCR (VZV, HSV-1, HSV-2, CMV), before or at the start of treatment.
  4. How do you treat it?
    High-dose antivirals urgently: IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg three times daily or oral valaciclovir 2 g three times daily, plus intravitreal ganciclovir or foscarnet; oral steroids once antivirals are established.
  5. What is the main late complication?
    Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in about half of eyes, often with PVR; prophylactic laser is debated.
  6. How does PORN differ?
    Progressive outer retinal necrosis occurs in severe immunosuppression (AIDS), with little inflammation and early posterior involvement, and a very poor prognosis.
  7. Why monitor the other eye?
    The fellow eye is involved in about a third of untreated cases, sometimes weeks or years later; long-term oral antivirals reduce this risk.

Station 68: Optic neuritis vs papilloedema Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 28-year-old woman has reduced vision in one eye for 4 days with pain on eye movement. The disc looks normal.

  1. What is the diagnosis and the key signs?
    Typical (demyelinating) retrobulbar optic neuritis: unilateral subacute vision loss, pain on eye movement, RAPD, reduced colour vision (red desaturation), central or diffuse field loss, normal disc in two-thirds.
  2. What did ONTT show?
    IV methylprednisolone followed by oral steroids sped recovery but did not change final vision; oral prednisolone alone at standard dose increased the recurrence rate. MRI white-matter lesions predicted multiple sclerosis (about 50% at 15 years if present).
  3. What are atypical features that suggest NMOSD or MOGAD?
    Severe or bilateral loss, marked disc swelling, poor recovery, long-segment or chiasmal involvement on MRI (NMOSD), perineural enhancement (MOGAD), and absence of pain. Test aquaporin-4 and MOG antibodies.
  4. How does papilloedema differ?
    Papilloedema is bilateral disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure, with good central vision and colour vision early, enlarged blind spot, headache, transient visual obscurations, and no RAPD unless asymmetric.
  5. How do you grade papilloedema?
    Frisén scale 0–5, from a C-shaped halo of nasal swelling (grade 1) to obscuration of the vessels on the disc (grades 4–5).
  6. How do you manage idiopathic intracranial hypertension?
    Brain MRI with venography to exclude a venous sinus thrombosis or mass, then lumbar puncture (raised opening pressure, normal CSF). Weight loss and acetazolamide (IIHTT), with optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting for threatened vision.
  7. What is pseudopapilloedema?
    Elevated disc without true swelling: optic disc drusen (detected with B-scan, autofluorescence or OCT), tilted discs, hypermetropic crowded discs and myelinated nerve fibres.

Station 69: Horner syndrome Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 45-year-old with neck pain after a gym workout has a mild left ptosis and a smaller left pupil, more obvious in dim light.

  1. What are the signs?
    Mild ptosis, miosis with dilation lag in the dark, apparent enophthalmos, inverse ptosis of the lower lid, and anhidrosis depending on the level.
  2. How do you confirm it pharmacologically?
    Apraclonidine 0.5% reverses the anisocoria (the Horner pupil dilates); cocaine 4 to 10% fails to dilate the Horner pupil.
  3. How do you localise it?
    Hydroxyamphetamine dilates a first- or second-order Horner pupil but not a third-order one; clinical clues help more in practice.
  4. What is the urgent concern in this patient?
    Carotid artery dissection: painful acute Horner needs same-day CT or MR angiography of the head and neck.
  5. Which causes affect each neuron?
    First: brainstem stroke, syringomyelia. Second: Pancoast tumour, neck surgery, neuroblastoma in children. Third: carotid dissection, cavernous sinus lesions, cluster headache.
  6. What is special about congenital Horner?
    Iris heterochromia (lighter iris on the affected side); in acquired childhood Horner, exclude neuroblastoma.
  7. Why is the ptosis mild?
    Only Müller muscle (sympathetic) is affected; the levator is intact.

Station 70: Third nerve palsy Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 50-year-old with sudden headache has complete ptosis, an eye that is down and out, and a dilated unreactive pupil.

  1. What is your first concern?
    A posterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing the nerve; this needs emergency CT angiography or MRA.
  2. Why is the pupil involved in compression?
    Pupillomotor fibres run superficially in the nerve and are affected first by compression; ischaemia damages the core and usually spares the pupil.
  3. What causes a pupil-sparing palsy?
    Microvascular ischaemia from diabetes, hypertension or hyperlipidaemia, usually painful and recovering within 3 months; image if under 50 or not resolving.
  4. What is aberrant regeneration?
    Misdirected fibres cause lid elevation on adduction or depression (pseudo-von Graefe) and pupil constriction on eye movement; it points to compression or trauma, not ischaemia.
  5. How does a nuclear third nerve palsy present?
    Bilateral ptosis (single central caudal nucleus) and contralateral superior rectus weakness.
  6. Name some brainstem syndromes involving the third nerve.
    Weber (contralateral hemiparesis), Benedikt (contralateral tremor), Claude (contralateral ataxia).
  7. How do you manage diplopia long term?
    Prisms or occlusion first, then strabismus surgery and ptosis surgery once stable for 6 to 12 months.

Station 71: Sixth nerve palsy Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 30-year-old woman with headaches and horizontal double vision worse at distance cannot abduct the right eye.

  1. Why is the sixth nerve a false localising sign?
    Its long intracranial course over the petrous apex makes it vulnerable to raised intracranial pressure from any cause.
  2. What do you check in this patient?
    Optic discs for papilloedema, blood pressure and BMI; consider idiopathic intracranial hypertension and urgent imaging.
  3. What is Gradenigo syndrome?
    Petrous apicitis from otitis media causing sixth nerve palsy, facial pain (fifth nerve) and ear discharge.
  4. What are other causes?
    Microvascular ischaemia, trauma, tumours (clivus chordoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), cavernous sinus disease and, in children, post-viral palsy.
  5. How do you tell it from Duane syndrome?
    Duane is congenital with globe retraction and palpebral fissure narrowing on adduction, and usually no diplopia.
  6. How do you treat persistent palsy?
    Prisms, botulinum toxin to the medial rectus, and later transposition surgery for complete palsy.
  7. Why does a child with acute sixth nerve palsy need imaging?
    To exclude pontine glioma and other posterior fossa tumours.

Station 72: Fourth nerve palsy Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A man after a motorcycle accident has vertical double vision, worse on looking down to read, and tilts his head to the left.

  1. How do you confirm the side?
    Parks-Bielschowsky three-step test: right hypertropia, worse on left gaze and on right head tilt indicates right superior oblique palsy.
  2. Why is the fourth nerve vulnerable to trauma?
    It is long and thin and exits the dorsal midbrain, where both nerves can be damaged together against the tentorium.
  3. How do you tell congenital from acquired?
    Old photographs with a head tilt, large vertical fusional amplitudes, and facial asymmetry suggest congenital decompensated palsy.
  4. What is the double Maddox rod test for?
    Measuring excyclotorsion; more than 10 degrees suggests bilateral palsy.
  5. Which surgery corrects it?
    Inferior oblique weakening for overaction, superior oblique tuck, or contralateral inferior rectus recession depending on the pattern.
  6. Why does reading worsen symptoms?
    The superior oblique is the main depressor in adduction, which is needed for reading.
  7. What does a nuclear fourth nerve lesion cause?
    A contralateral superior oblique palsy, because the nerve decussates.

Station 73: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: An overweight 26-year-old woman with daily headaches, pulse-synchronous tinnitus and transient visual obscurations has bilateral disc swelling.

  1. What are the diagnostic criteria?
    Papilloedema, normal neurological exam (except sixth nerve palsy), normal MRI with MR venography, normal CSF composition and raised opening pressure (25 cm H2O or more in adults).
  2. Why is MR venography essential?
    To exclude cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, which can mimic IIH.
  3. Which drugs are linked?
    Tetracyclines, vitamin A and retinoids, lithium, growth hormone and steroid withdrawal.
  4. What did the IIH Treatment Trial show?
    Acetazolamide plus weight loss improved visual fields more than weight loss alone in mild visual loss.
  5. How do you monitor vision?
    Visual fields (perimetry), acuity, colour, disc photographs and OCT of the RNFL.
  6. When is surgery needed?
    Fulminant or progressive visual loss: optic nerve sheath fenestration, CSF shunting or venous sinus stenting.
  7. What newer treatment is under study?
    GLP-1 agonists such as exenatide reduce intracranial pressure and help weight loss.

Station 74: Giant cell arteritis Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 75-year-old with a new headache, scalp tenderness and jaw pain on chewing has sudden loss of vision. The disc is chalky white and swollen.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy from giant cell arteritis.
  2. Which blood tests help?
    ESR and CRP (CRP is more sensitive), and platelets (thrombocytosis).
  3. What do you do before the biopsy?
    Start high-dose steroid immediately: IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days in visual loss, then oral prednisolone. Do not wait for the biopsy.
  4. How long after starting steroids is a biopsy still useful?
    Up to about 2 weeks; take at least 1.5 to 2 cm because of skip lesions. Temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign) is an alternative.
  5. Why is the fellow eye at risk?
    Without treatment the second eye is affected in days to weeks in a large proportion of patients.
  6. Which steroid-sparing drug is approved?
    Tocilizumab (IL-6 inhibitor), shown in the GiACTA trial to reduce steroid dose and relapses.
  7. How does arteritic AION differ from NAION?
    Arteritic: older, systemic symptoms, chalky white disc, severe loss, choroidal non-perfusion on FFA. NAION: hyperaemic swelling and a small crowded disc in the other eye.

Station 75: Non-arteritic AION Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 58-year-old diabetic woke up with an inferior field loss in one eye. The disc is sectorally swollen with flame haemorrhages, and the other disc has no cup.

  1. What is the mechanism?
    Hypoperfusion of the short posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head, in a small crowded disc (disc at risk).
  2. Why on waking?
    Nocturnal hypotension, worsened by evening antihypertensives, is thought to contribute.
  3. Which risk factors do you ask about?
    Hypertension, diabetes, sleep apnoea, hyperlipidaemia, and drugs such as PDE-5 inhibitors and semaglutide (association under study).
  4. What is the typical field defect?
    Altitudinal, more often inferior.
  5. Is there an effective treatment?
    No proven treatment; the Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy Decompression Trial showed optic nerve sheath decompression was harmful. Control vascular risk factors.
  6. What is the prognosis?
    Usually stable after a few weeks; about 40% improve slightly; the fellow eye is affected in about 15% over 5 years.
  7. Why must you always exclude GCA?
    Arteritic AION threatens both eyes within days and needs steroids immediately.

Station 76: Chiasmal compression Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 45-year-old man bumps into door frames and has headaches. Confrontation suggests loss of both temporal fields.

  1. What is the field defect and the commonest cause?
    Bitemporal hemianopia from a pituitary adenoma compressing the chiasm from below, so the defect usually begins superotemporally.
  2. How do craniopharyngiomas differ?
    They compress from above, so the defect often starts inferotemporally; they occur in children and older adults and calcify.
  3. What is band atrophy?
    Horizontal (bow-tie) pallor of the optic disc from loss of nasal retinal fibres crossing in the chiasm.
  4. What is pituitary apoplexy?
    Sudden haemorrhage or infarction of a pituitary tumour causing headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia and hypopituitarism; an emergency needing steroids and often surgery.
  5. Which endocrine tests do you request?
    Prolactin, growth hormone and IGF-1, cortisol, TSH and free T4, LH, FSH and sex hormones.
  6. How are prolactinomas treated?
    With dopamine agonists (cabergoline), which shrink the tumour; other adenomas usually need trans-sphenoidal surgery.
  7. What does OCT add?
    Ganglion cell layer thinning in a binasal pattern, and a thin RNFL predicts poorer visual recovery after surgery.

Station 77: Ocular myasthenia gravis Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 60-year-old has variable ptosis and double vision that are worse in the evening. The ptosis worsens on sustained upgaze.

  1. What is myasthenia gravis?
    An autoimmune disease with antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (or MuSK, LRP4) at the neuromuscular junction.
  2. Which bedside tests help?
    Ice pack test (ptosis improves after 2 minutes of cooling), sustained upgaze fatigue, Cogan lid twitch and the sleep test.
  3. Which investigations confirm it?
    Acetylcholine receptor antibodies (often negative in purely ocular disease), single-fibre EMG (most sensitive) and repetitive nerve stimulation.
  4. Which other test is important?
    CT of the chest for thymoma, and thyroid function (associated thyroid eye disease).
  5. How do you treat ocular myasthenia?
    Pyridostigmine for symptoms, then low-dose steroids or immunosuppressants; the EPITOME trial supported prednisolone.
  6. What proportion becomes generalised?
    About half within 2 years; steroids may reduce this risk.
  7. Which drugs can worsen myasthenia?
    Aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, beta-blockers (including timolol drops), magnesium and checkpoint inhibitors.

Station 78: Toxic and nutritional optic neuropathy Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 50-year-old on treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis for 3 months notices gradual blurring in both eyes and dull colours.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Ethambutol toxic optic neuropathy.
  2. What are the typical features?
    Bilateral, painless, symmetrical loss of central vision, reduced colour vision (often red-green), central or caecocentral scotomas, and initially normal or slightly swollen discs, later temporal pallor.
  3. Which risk factors increase ethambutol toxicity?
    Toxicity is dose-related: standard dosing is about 15–20 mg/kg/day and risk rises at higher doses; kidney disease (the drug accumulates), long duration, older age and diabetes add to the risk.
  4. What do you do?
    Stop ethambutol in discussion with the TB physician (who will substitute another drug), and monitor; vision may recover over months but often incompletely.
  5. What screening is recommended?
    Baseline acuity, colour vision (Ishihara) and fields before starting, and regular checks, with patients told to report blurring at once.
  6. Which other drugs and toxins cause optic neuropathy?
    Isoniazid, linezolid, amiodarone, methanol (acute, with metabolic acidosis), tobacco-alcohol amblyopia, and nutritional deficiency (B12, thiamine, folate, copper).
  7. What is the main differential?

Station 79: Abnormal pupils: Adie, Argyll Robertson and light-near dissociation Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 30-year-old woman notices that her right pupil is larger than the left; it reacts poorly to light but slowly constricts on near effort.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Adie tonic pupil: damage to the ciliary ganglion or short ciliary nerves.
  2. How do you confirm it?
    Dilute pilocarpine 0.125% constricts the tonic pupil (cholinergic denervation supersensitivity) but not a normal pupil; slit lamp shows sectoral iris sphincter palsy with vermiform movements.
  3. What is Holmes-Adie syndrome?
    Adie pupil with reduced deep tendon reflexes; some patients also have abnormal sweating (Ross syndrome).
  4. What are Argyll Robertson pupils?
    Small, irregular pupils that react poorly to light but constrict to near (light-near dissociation), usually bilateral; classically neurosyphilis.
  5. What other causes of light-near dissociation do you know?
    Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome, diabetes, aberrant regeneration of the third nerve, Adie pupil, and severe bilateral afferent loss.
  6. How do you approach anisocoria?
    Compare in light and dark: if the difference is larger in the dark, the small pupil is abnormal (Horner); if larger in the light, the large pupil is abnormal (third nerve palsy, Adie, pharmacological).
  7. How do you recognise a pharmacological pupil?
    A large pupil that does not constrict even to 1% pilocarpine, often with a history of exposure to atropine, scopolamine patches or plants.

Station 80: Homonymous visual field defects Neuro-ophthalmology

Examiner: A 66-year-old bumps into things on her left side; fields show a left homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.

  1. Where is the lesion?
    Right occipital cortex, usually a posterior cerebral artery infarct; macular sparing occurs because of dual blood supply to the occipital pole.
  2. How does the location change the field defect?
    Optic tract: incongruous hemianopia with a RAPD and band atrophy; temporal lobe (Meyer loop): superior "pie in the sky" quadrantanopia; parietal lobe: inferior quadrantanopia; occipital: highly congruous defects.
  3. What does congruity mean?
    How similar the defects are in the two eyes; the more posterior the lesion, the more congruous the defect.
  4. What is the temporal crescent?
    The far temporal field of each eye is seen only by that eye and represented in the anterior occipital cortex; it can be spared or lost alone.
  5. Which investigations are needed?
    Urgent brain imaging (MRI), vascular risk work-up, and check for neglect and other neurological signs.
  6. Can this patient drive?
    A complete homonymous hemianopia usually fails driving standards; check national rules (in India, the RTO; in the UK, DVLA).
  7. How do you help her?
    Explain the defect, scanning training, prism devices (Peli prisms) for awareness, and stroke rehabilitation; bilateral occipital damage can cause cortical blindness and Anton syndrome.

Station 81: Childhood esotropia Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: A 3-year-old whose parents noticed an inturning of the right eye, worse when looking at near objects, for six months.

  1. How do you examine the deviation?
    Corneal reflexes (Hirschberg), cover-uncover test (tropia vs phoria), alternate cover test with prisms at distance and near, ocular movements, and assessment of fixation and vision in each eye.
  2. How do you estimate the angle with the Hirschberg test?
    Each 1 mm of decentration of the corneal reflex is about 7° (roughly 15 prism dioptres). A reflex at the pupil margin is about 15°, at the mid-iris about 30°, at the limbus about 45°.
  3. What is the most important next step?
    Cycloplegic refraction (cyclopentolate 1%, or atropine in very young or dark irides) and fundus examination; correct hypermetropia fully.
  4. What is refractive accommodative esotropia?
    An esotropia caused by uncorrected hypermetropia (often +2 to +6 D) with normal AC/A, fully corrected by glasses. A high AC/A type has a larger deviation at near, treated with bifocals.
  5. How do you treat amblyopia?
    Correct the refractive error first (refractive adaptation), then patching (2 hours a day as effective as 6 hours for moderate amblyopia in PEDIG studies) or atropine penalisation; binocular treatments are an option.
  6. When is surgery indicated?
    For the residual non-accommodative deviation after full glasses correction and amblyopia treatment. Infantile esotropia (onset before 6 months) is usually operated early.
  7. What are the features of infantile esotropia?
    Onset before 6 months, large stable angle (often > 30 Δ), cross-fixation, little refractive error, latent nystagmus, dissociated vertical deviation and inferior oblique overaction.

Station 82: Leukocoria and retinoblastoma Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: A mother brings her 18-month-old because the left pupil looks white in flash photographs.

  1. Give the differential diagnosis of leukocoria.
    Retinoblastoma (must be excluded first), congenital cataract, persistent fetal vasculature, Coats disease, retinopathy of prematurity (stage 5), toxocariasis, retinal detachment and coloboma.
  2. What is the genetics of retinoblastoma?
    The RB1 tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 13q14. Knudson's two-hit hypothesis: heritable cases (about 40%) carry one germline mutation and are often bilateral and earlier; non-heritable cases need two somatic hits and are unilateral.

    Examination under anaesthesia with dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy, B-scan ultrasound (calcification), and MRI of the orbits and brain (avoid CT because of radiation in heritable disease). Never biopsy an eye with suspected retinoblastoma.
  4. What does the international classification grade?
    ICRB groups A to E: A small tumours away from the fovea and disc, through to E with neovascular glaucoma, massive haemorrhage or tumour filling the eye (usually enucleation).
  5. What treatment options exist?
    Intravenous or intra-arterial chemotherapy, focal treatment (laser, cryotherapy, plaque brachytherapy), intravitreal chemotherapy for seeds, and enucleation for advanced eyes.
  6. What is trilateral retinoblastoma?
    Bilateral retinoblastoma with a midline intracranial primitive neuroectodermal tumour, usually pinealoblastoma.
  7. Which second cancers are a risk?
    In heritable disease: osteosarcoma (the commonest), soft-tissue sarcomas and melanoma, more so after external beam radiotherapy. Siblings and offspring need screening.

Station 83: Amblyopia Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: A 6-year-old fails a school vision test: 6/36 in the right eye and 6/6 in the left. Refraction shows +4.00 D in the right and +0.50 D in the left.

  1. What type of amblyopia is this?
    Anisometropic amblyopia, from a constantly blurred image in the more hypermetropic eye.
  2. What are the other types?
    Strabismic, stimulus deprivation (cataract, ptosis), meridional (astigmatism), and bilateral ametropic.
  3. What is the first step in treatment?
    Full cycloplegic refraction and glasses worn full time; refractive adaptation alone improves many children over 16 weeks (PEDIG).
  4. How much patching is needed?
    PEDIG showed 2 hours a day was as effective as 6 hours for moderate amblyopia, and 6 hours as effective as full time for severe.
  5. What alternatives to patching exist?
    Atropine 1% penalisation of the better eye (daily or weekends), Bangerter filters and dichoptic digital therapy.
  6. Up to what age can treatment work?
    Best under 7, but PEDIG showed improvement in many children aged 7 to 12 and some teenagers who had not been treated before.
  7. What risk follows stopping treatment?
    Recurrence in about a quarter within a year, so taper rather than stop suddenly.

Station 84: Intermittent exotropia Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: Parents notice their 4-year-old's eye drifting outward when tired or daydreaming, and the child closes one eye in bright sunlight.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Intermittent exotropia, the commonest form of exotropia in children.
  2. How do you classify it?
    Basic, divergence excess (larger at distance) and convergence insufficiency type (larger at near); simulated divergence excess becomes basic after patching or +3.00 D lenses.
  3. How do you assess control?
    The Newcastle Control Score (home and clinic control at distance and near) or the office control score.
  4. Why does the child close one eye in sunlight?
    Bright light disrupts fusion and causes the deviation, so closing one eye avoids diplopia.
  5. What non-surgical options exist?
    Observation, correcting refractive error, overminus lenses and part-time patching.
  6. When do you operate?
    Poor or deteriorating control, a deviation that is manifest most of the time, or loss of stereopsis; usually bilateral lateral rectus recession.
  7. What did PEDIG find about observation?
    Most children with good control do not deteriorate quickly over 3 years, so observation is reasonable.

Station 85: Duane retraction syndrome Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: A 7-year-old girl cannot move her left eye outward, and the eye retracts with narrowing of the lid opening when she looks to the right.

  1. What is the mechanism?
    Congenital absence of the sixth nerve nucleus with misdirected innervation of the lateral rectus by the third nerve, causing co-contraction on adduction.
  2. How is it classified (Huber)?
    Type 1 limited abduction (commonest), type 2 limited adduction, type 3 limited abduction and adduction.
  3. Why is diplopia uncommon?
    It is congenital, and children adopt a face turn to keep binocular vision in primary position.
  4. Which features are typical?
    Left eye, female predominance, globe retraction, upshoots or downshoots on adduction.
  5. What associations do you check?
    Hearing loss, Goldenhar syndrome, Klippel-Feil anomaly and Okihiro syndrome (radial ray defects).
  6. When is surgery indicated?
    A significant face turn, deviation in primary position, or large upshoots; medial rectus recession on the affected side is typical. Never resect the lateral rectus.
  7. How do you tell it from a sixth nerve palsy?
    Duane has retraction and fissure narrowing on adduction, little deviation in primary position and no diplopia.

Station 86: Brown syndrome Paediatrics and strabismus

Examiner: A child cannot elevate the right eye when it is turned inward, but elevation in abduction is normal.

  1. What is Brown syndrome?
    Restricted movement of the superior oblique tendon through the trochlea, limiting elevation in adduction.
  2. How do you confirm restriction?
    Positive forced duction test when the eye is pulled up in adduction.
  3. How do you tell it from inferior oblique palsy?
    Brown syndrome has a positive forced duction test, a V pattern and no superior oblique overaction; inferior oblique palsy has an A pattern and positive head tilt findings.
  4. What acquired causes exist?
    Inflammation of the trochlea in rheumatoid arthritis, sinusitis, trauma and after superior oblique tuck.
  5. How do you treat acquired inflammatory Brown?
    Oral NSAIDs or a steroid injection near the trochlea.
  6. When does congenital Brown need surgery?
    Hypotropia in primary position, an abnormal head posture or loss of binocular vision; superior oblique tenotomy with a spacer is typical.
  7. What is the natural history?
    Many congenital cases improve spontaneously in childhood.

    At the valve of Hasner at the lower end of the nasolacrimal duct, which has failed to canalise.

    Most cases (about 90%) resolve by 12 months of age.

    Crigler massage (downward pressure over the sac) and topical antibiotic for infections.

    A bluish swelling below the medial canthus at birth from distension of the sac; it can become infected and obstruct the nose, so early probing is often needed.

    High myopia raises the lifetime risk of myopic maculopathy, retinal detachment, glaucoma and cataract.

    Yes, for example atropine with orthokeratology or defocus spectacles, which may add benefit.

Station 90: Ptosis Oculoplasty and orbit

Examiner: A 25-year-old with drooping of the left upper lid since birth. The lid lags on downgaze.

  1. What is the likely type of ptosis?
    Congenital (dystrophic) myogenic ptosis: lid lag on downgaze and poor levator function due to a fibrotic levator muscle.
  2. Which measurements do you take?
    MRD1 (normal 4–5 mm), palpebral fissure height, levator function (lid excursion from downgaze to upgaze with the brow fixed; normal 12–15 mm), skin crease height, Bell's phenomenon, corneal sensation and jaw-winking.
  3. How does levator function guide surgery?
    Good (10 mm or more): levator aponeurosis advancement or Müller muscle resection. Fair (5–9 mm): levator resection. Poor (4 mm or less): frontalis sling.

    Synkinesis between the trigeminal (pterygoid) and oculomotor nerves: the ptotic lid elevates on jaw movement.
  5. How do you differentiate the causes of acquired ptosis?
    Aponeurotic (elderly, high skin crease, good function), neurogenic (III nerve palsy with down-and-out eye; Horner with miosis and mild ptosis), myasthenic (variable, fatigable, Cogan lid twitch, positive ice-pack test) and mechanical (tumour, oedema).

    A lid covering the visual axis causes deprivation amblyopia, and ptosis can induce astigmatism, so check vision and refraction in every child.
  7. What is pseudoptosis?
    Apparent ptosis from hypotropia, enophthalmos, contralateral lid retraction, dermatochalasis or brow ptosis.

    Bilateral signs, multiple cranial nerve palsies, reduced consciousness and septic features.

    Suspected intraocular tumour (retinoblastoma, melanoma) or an eye with unknown contents; evisceration is avoided because of tumour spread.
  3. What are the advantages of evisceration?
    Quicker, less disruption of orbital tissue, better implant motility and cosmesis; it is often used for endophthalmitis and blind painful eyes.
  4. What is the concern about sympathetic ophthalmia?
    A small theoretical risk after evisceration if uveal tissue is left behind, so remove all uvea carefully.
  5. What is exenteration?
    Removal of the whole orbital contents, including the lids if needed, for malignant orbital or periocular tumours and invasive fungal infection (mucormycosis).
  6. What orbital implants are used?
    Spherical implants of silicone, PMMA, hydroxyapatite or porous polyethylene, usually 18–22 mm, to replace volume; a conformer is placed and an ocular prosthesis fitted after 6–8 weeks.
  7. What problems occur in an anophthalmic socket?
    Implant exposure or extrusion, post-enucleation socket syndrome (deep upper sulcus, ptosis, enophthalmos, lower lid laxity), contracted socket and discharge.

Station 101: Eyelid laceration and canalicular injury Oculoplasty and orbit

Examiner: A child bitten by a dog has a laceration through the medial part of the lower lid.

  1. What must you suspect with a medial lid laceration?
    Canalicular laceration: probe the canaliculus to confirm.
  2. How do you assess the eye and orbit?
    Check the globe for open injury first, acuity, pupils and motility, and arrange imaging if a foreign body or fracture is suspected.
  3. When should a canalicular injury be repaired?
    Ideally within 24–48 hours, under the microscope, with a silicone stent (monocanalicular or bicanalicular) left for about 3 months.
  4. How do you repair a full-thickness lid margin laceration?
    Align the lid margin precisely with vertical mattress sutures through the grey line and lash line, repair the tarsus with partial-thickness absorbable sutures, then the skin.
  5. What happens if the margin is badly aligned?
    Notching and trichiasis, so precise margin alignment is the key step.
  6. What else must you give after a dog bite?
    Tetanus cover, antibiotics (for example amoxicillin-clavulanate), and rabies post-exposure prophylaxis with vaccine and immunoglobulin as per national guidelines.
  7. When do you suspect deeper injury?
    Fat in the wound (orbital septum breached), ptosis (levator injury), and medial canthal tendon avulsion (rounded canthus).

Station 102: Open globe injury Trauma

Examiner: A metal worker hammering steel felt something hit his eye. Vision is 6/60, the pupil is peaked, and there is a small corneal wound with a positive Seidel test.

  1. How do you classify the injury (BETT)?
    An open globe: penetrating (entry only), perforating (entry and exit), intraocular foreign body, or rupture (blunt). Zone I cornea, zone II up to 5 mm behind the limbus, zone III beyond.
  2. What do you do in the emergency room?
    Rigid shield (no pad or pressure), no drops or ointment, nil by mouth, analgesia and antiemetics, tetanus cover and systemic antibiotics (for example oral moxifloxacin).
  3. Which imaging do you request?
    CT orbits with thin slices to look for a foreign body; MRI is contraindicated until a metallic body is excluded. Avoid B-scan with an open wound unless done gently.
  4. What is the ocular trauma score?
    A score from initial acuity, rupture, endophthalmitis, perforation, retinal detachment and RAPD that predicts final vision.
  5. When should the wound be repaired?
    As soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours, to reduce endophthalmitis risk.
  6. Why is a peaked pupil important?
    It points towards the wound, where iris is often prolapsed.
  7. Which late risk must you warn about?
    Sympathetic ophthalmia in the fellow eye.

Station 103: Traumatic hyphema Trauma

Examiner: A 12-year-old hit by a shuttlecock has blurred vision and a 2 mm level of blood in the anterior chamber.

  1. What is your initial management?
    Bed rest with head elevated, a shield, topical steroid and cycloplegic, IOP control, and avoiding antiplatelet drugs and NSAIDs.
  2. What is the main risk in the first days?
    Rebleeding, usually at 3 to 5 days, which carries a worse prognosis.
  3. Why must sickle cell status be checked?
    Sickled cells block the trabecular meshwork, so IOP rises at lower levels; avoid carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and hyperosmotics, and operate earlier.
  4. What is corneal blood staining?
    Haemoglobin entering the stroma, risked by a total hyphema with raised IOP and endothelial damage; it can cause amblyopia in children.
  5. When do you wash out the hyphema?
    IOP over 50 mmHg for 5 days or over 35 mmHg for 7 days (lower thresholds with sickle disease), early corneal staining or a total hyphema for 5 days.
  6. Which late complication should be screened for?
    Angle recession glaucoma; gonioscopy after a few weeks, and lifelong IOP checks.
  7. Which posterior segment injuries can accompany it?
    Commotio retinae, choroidal rupture, retinal dialysis and vitreous haemorrhage.

Station 104: Intraocular foreign body Trauma

Examiner: A man drilling metal without goggles has a small self-sealing corneal entry wound and a hole in the iris; vision is 6/12.

  1. Which history raises suspicion of an IOFB?
    Hammering metal on metal, drilling, grinding or explosions.
  2. How do you locate it?
    CT is the investigation of choice; B-scan can help; X-ray if CT is unavailable. MRI is contraindicated for metallic bodies.
  3. What is siderosis bulbi?
    Iron toxicity: iris heterochromia (darker), dilated pupil, rust-coloured lens deposits, pigmentary retinopathy and reduced ERG.
  4. What does copper do?
    Pure copper causes acute endophthalmitis; copper alloys cause chalcosis with a sunflower cataract and Kayser-Fleischer ring.
  5. Which inert foreign bodies are tolerated?
    Glass, plastic, gold, silver, platinum and stone.
  6. How do you remove a posterior IOFB?
    Pars plana vitrectomy with forceps or an intraocular magnet, ideally within 24 hours, with intravitreal antibiotics.
  7. What organism is feared after soil-contaminated injury?
    Bacillus cereus, which causes fulminant endophthalmitis within hours.

Station 105: Chemical injury Trauma

Examiner: A worker had lime (chuna) splashed into his eyes ten minutes ago. He is in severe pain with a white limbus.

  1. What is your first action?
    Immediate copious irrigation with saline or tap water for at least 30 minutes, until the pH is neutral (7 to 7.5), and sweep the fornices to remove particles.
  2. Why are alkalis worse than acids?
    Alkalis saponify cell membranes and penetrate deeply; acids coagulate surface proteins, which limits penetration.
  3. How do you grade it?
    Roper-Hall (corneal haze and limbal ischaemia in clock hours) or Dua (limbal involvement in clock hours and conjunctival involvement as a percentage).
  4. Why is limbal ischaemia important?
    It indicates limbal stem cell loss, which predicts persistent epithelial defects, vascularisation and conjunctivalisation.
  5. What is the medical treatment?
    Topical steroid for the first 1 to 2 weeks, antibiotic, cycloplegic, preservative-free lubricants, topical and oral vitamin C, and doxycycline to reduce collagenase.
  6. When is amniotic membrane used?
    Early in moderate to severe burns to promote healing and reduce inflammation and symblepharon.
  7. How do you rehabilitate chronic stem cell deficiency?
    Limbal stem cell transplantation (CLAU, SLET for unilateral cases) followed by keratoplasty if needed.

Station 106: Retrobulbar haemorrhage Trauma

Examiner: After a fall, an elderly patient on warfarin has a tense proptosed eye, IOP 55 mmHg, a RAPD and hand-movement vision.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Orbital compartment syndrome from retrobulbar haemorrhage.
  2. What is the immediate treatment?
    Lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis at the bedside, without waiting for imaging.
  3. Why the urgency?
    Raised orbital pressure compresses the optic nerve and its blood supply; irreversible damage can occur within about 90 minutes.
  4. How do you do a lateral canthotomy?
    Local anaesthetic, clamp the lateral canthus, cut horizontally to the orbital rim, then cut the inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon until the lid moves freely.
  5. Which signs show success?
    The lower lid becomes lax, IOP drops and vision or the RAPD improves.
  6. Which other measures help?
    IV acetazolamide, mannitol and steroid, and reversing anticoagulation.
  7. When else can this occur?
    After retrobulbar or peribulbar anaesthesia, orbital or sinus surgery, and in bleeding disorders.

Station 107: Traumatic optic neuropathy Trauma

Examiner: A motorcyclist with a forehead injury above the eyebrow has 6/60 vision in one eye, a RAPD and a normal-looking disc.

  1. Why does the disc look normal?
    The injury is usually indirect, in the optic canal, so the disc becomes pale only after 3 to 6 weeks.
  2. What is the mechanism of indirect injury?
    Force transmitted from the frontal bone to the optic canal shears the nerve and its vessels.
  3. Which investigation do you request?
    CT of the orbits and optic canal to look for fractures, bone fragments and haematoma.
  4. What did the CRASH trial show?
    High-dose steroids in head injury increased mortality, so megadose steroids are no longer recommended.
  5. What did the International Optic Nerve Trauma Study find?
    No clear benefit for steroids or optic canal decompression over observation.
  6. When is surgery considered?
    Bone fragments impinging on the nerve, or an orbital haematoma causing compression.
  7. What is the prognosis?
    Poor initial vision (no light perception) predicts a poor outcome; some patients improve spontaneously.

Station 108: Retinoscopy Optics and refraction

Examiner: You are asked to perform retinoscopy at a working distance of 66 cm. In one meridian you see a with movement.

  1. What does with movement mean?
    The far point lies behind the observer: the eye is hypermetropic, emmetropic or myopic by less than the working distance dioptres; add plus lenses.
  2. What does against movement mean?
    The far point lies between the observer and the eye (myopia greater than the working distance); add minus lenses.
  3. How do you correct for working distance?
    Subtract the dioptric value of the working distance: 1.5 D at 66 cm, 2 D at 50 cm.
  4. What does a scissoring reflex suggest?
    Irregular astigmatism, as in keratoconus.
  5. What does cycloplegia add in children?
    It relaxes accommodation and reveals latent hypermetropia; cyclopentolate 1% is used, atropine in very young or esotropic children.
  6. How do you find the axis of astigmatism?
    Rotate the streak until the reflex aligns with the streak (no break or skew); neutralise each principal meridian separately.
  7. What is dynamic retinoscopy used for?
    Assessing accommodation at near, for example the MEM technique.

Station 109: Subjective refraction and the cross-cylinder Optics and refraction

Examiner: After retinoscopy gives -1.00/-1.50 x 180, you refine the cylinder with a Jackson cross-cylinder.

  1. What is a Jackson cross-cylinder?
    A lens with equal plus and minus cylinders at right angles (for example +0.25/-0.50), so the spherical equivalent is zero.
  2. How do you refine the axis?
    Place the JCC handle along the cylinder axis and flip; rotate the axis towards the preferred minus-cylinder position, in smaller steps, until both views are equal.
  3. How do you refine the power?
    Align the JCC axes with the cylinder axis and flip; add minus cylinder if the minus position is preferred, and adjust the sphere by half the change to keep the circle of least confusion on the retina.
  4. Why refine the axis before the power?
    An axis error causes a residual cylinder that makes power refinement inaccurate.
  5. What is the duochrome test?
    Letters on red and green backgrounds: if red is clearer, add minus; if green is clearer, add plus (RAM-GAP). It relies on chromatic aberration.
  6. What is the fogging technique?
    Adding plus to relax accommodation before refining the sphere, especially in younger patients.
  7. How do you finish the refraction?
    Binocular balancing (prism dissociation or alternate occlusion) and checking the maximum plus for best acuity.

Station 110: Presbyopia and the near add Optics and refraction

Examiner: A 45-year-old hypermetrope has difficulty reading small print and holds the phone at arm's length.

  1. What is presbyopia?
    Age-related loss of accommodation, mainly from reduced lens elasticity; symptoms usually begin in the early forties.
  2. Why do hypermetropes notice it earlier?
    They use part of their accommodation for distance, leaving less for near.
  3. How do you determine the near add?
    From age, amplitude of accommodation (keep half in reserve) and the patient's working distance; refine with the near cross-cylinder or trial.
  4. What are typical adds?
    About +1.00 D at 40 to 45 years, +1.50 to +2.00 D at 50, and +2.50 D at 60 for 40 cm.
  5. Which spectacle options exist?
    Single-vision readers, bifocals, progressive addition lenses and office (occupational) lenses.
  6. Which pharmacological option is approved?
    Pilocarpine 1.25% drops create a pinhole effect; aceclidine is newer. Watch for retinal detachment risk and poor night vision.
  7. What surgical options are available?
    Monovision with LASIK or IOLs, multifocal or EDOF IOLs, and corneal inlays (largely abandoned).

Station 111: Anisometropia and aniseikonia Optics and refraction

Examiner: A 50-year-old needs -1.00 D in the right eye and -5.00 D in the left, and complains of strain and double vision with new glasses.

  1. What is anisometropia?
    A difference in refractive error between the two eyes, usually significant at 1 D or more for hypermetropia and astigmatism, and more for myopia.
  2. What is aniseikonia?
    A difference in image size between the eyes; about 1% magnification difference per dioptre of anisometropia with spectacles. Differences over 5% are often not tolerated.
  3. What is Knapp law?
    In axial anisometropia, a lens at the anterior focal point (about 15 mm) gives equal image sizes; in practice contact lenses often work better because most anisometropia also has a refractive component.
  4. What causes vertical diplopia when reading?
    Induced vertical prism when looking down through different powers (Prentice rule); slab-off prism or separate readers help.
  5. Why are contact lenses useful?
    They reduce aniseikonia and prismatic effects compared with spectacles.
  6. How do you handle it at cataract surgery?
    Aim to reduce the difference to under 2 to 3 D, or plan the second eye soon after the first.
  7. Why is it important in children?
    It causes anisometropic amblyopia, which needs full correction and patching.

Station 112: Low vision aids Optics and refraction

Examiner: A 70-year-old with advanced dry AMD has 6/60 vision in both eyes and wants to read the newspaper.

  1. How is low vision defined in India?
    Under the RPwD Act 2016, low vision is best-corrected acuity of 6/18 or worse, down to 3/60, in the better eye, or a field of 10 to 40 degrees; blindness is acuity worse than 3/60 or a field under 10 degrees.
  2. How do you estimate the magnification needed?
    Kestenbaum rule: the reciprocal of the distance acuity gives the add needed to read N5 print (6/60 = 10/1, so about +10 D).
  3. What types of near aids are available?
    High-plus spectacles, hand-held and stand magnifiers, electronic video magnifiers and smartphone apps.
  4. What distance aids are available?
    Hand-held telescopes and spectacle-mounted telescopes.
  5. What non-optical measures help?
    Good lighting, contrast enhancement, large print, typoscopes and eccentric viewing training.
  6. What is the drawback of high-power spectacles?
    A very short working distance, which reduces comfort and needs prisms for binocular use.
  7. Which certificates should you discuss?
    The disability certificate under the RPwD Act, which gives access to benefits.

Station 113: Contact lenses Optics and refraction

Examiner: A 22-year-old with -6.00 D myopia wants to switch from glasses to contact lenses and also plays sports.

  1. What is the vertex distance correction for -6.00 D?
    About -5.50 D at the cornea (spectacles at 12 mm); correction is needed for powers above about 4 D.
  2. How do soft and RGP lenses differ?
    Soft lenses are comfortable and easy to adapt; RGP lenses correct corneal astigmatism and irregular corneas better, give sharper vision and allow more oxygen.
  3. What is the LARS rule?
    For toric soft lenses: if the marking rotates Left, Add the rotation to the axis; if Right, Subtract.
  4. Which complications should you warn about?
    Microbial keratitis (Pseudomonas, Acanthamoeba), giant papillary conjunctivitis, corneal neovascularisation and contact lens-induced red eye.
  5. What is the biggest risk factor for keratitis?
    Overnight wear; daily disposable lenses have the lowest risk.
  6. Which lenses suit keratoconus?
    RGP, hybrid, piggyback, Rose K and scleral lenses.
  7. What advice keeps lenses safe?
    Hand washing, no water contact, no sleeping in lenses, case replacement monthly, and removal if the eye is red or painful.

Station 114: Interpreting a visual field Investigations

Examiner: You are given a Humphrey 24-2 printout with a superior arcuate defect respecting the horizontal midline.

  1. How do you read a field printout systematically?
    Patient name, test and date; reliability indices; grey scale; total and pattern deviation plots; global indices (MD, PSD, VFI); GHT.
  2. Which reliability indices matter?
    Fixation losses (under 20%), false positives (under 15%) and false negatives.
  3. What does a defect respecting the horizontal midline suggest?
    Glaucoma or another retinal nerve fibre layer cause (branch occlusion, AION); vertical midline defects suggest chiasmal or retrochiasmal lesions.
  4. What is the pattern deviation plot?
    The total deviation corrected for generalised depression (cataract, small pupil), showing focal defects.
  5. How do you define a glaucomatous field defect (Hodapp-Parrish-Anderson)?
    GHT outside normal limits, or a cluster of 3 or more non-edge points at p under 5% with one under 1% on pattern deviation, or PSD at p under 5%, confirmed on repeat.
  6. When do you use a 10-2 test?
    Advanced glaucoma, paracentral defects, hydroxychloroquine screening and macular disease.
  7. How do you detect progression?
    Guided progression analysis and trend analysis of VFI or MD over at least 5 reliable fields.

Station 115: Reading a macular OCT Investigations

Examiner: You are shown an OCT scan through the fovea with a dome-shaped elevation of the RPE and a small pocket of fluid above it.

  1. Name the layers you see from inside out.
    ILM, RNFL, ganglion cell layer, IPL, INL, OPL, ONL, external limiting membrane, ellipsoid zone, interdigitation zone, RPE-Bruch complex and choroid.
  2. What does a dome-shaped RPE elevation represent?
    A pigment epithelial detachment: serous, fibrovascular (in neovascular AMD) or drusenoid.
  3. How do you tell intraretinal from subretinal fluid?
    Intraretinal fluid forms cysts within the neurosensory retina; subretinal fluid lies between the photoreceptors and the RPE.
  4. What is the double-layer sign?
    Separation of the RPE from Bruch membrane by a shallow irregular PED, suggesting type 1 neovascularisation, often non-exudative.
  5. Which feature suggests a good visual prognosis?
    An intact ellipsoid zone and external limiting membrane.
  6. What does OCT angiography add?
    Shows flow in vessels without dye: neovascular networks, capillary dropout and foveal avascular zone size.
  7. Which artefacts mislead OCT readers?
    Segmentation errors, decentration, motion and shadowing from vitreous opacities or haemorrhage.

Station 116: Fundus fluorescein angiography Investigations

Examiner: You are asked to explain an FFA showing areas of hyperfluorescence that increase in size and intensity over time.

  1. What are the phases of an FFA?
    Choroidal (pre-arterial, about 10 seconds), arterial, arteriovenous (laminar flow), venous and late (recirculation) phases.
  2. What does increasing hyperfluorescence in size and intensity mean?
    Leakage, for example from new vessels, CNV or a CSCR leak.
  3. What are the causes of hyperfluorescence?
    Leakage, pooling, staining, window defect (transmission) and autofluorescence.
  4. What causes hypofluorescence?
    Blocked fluorescence (blood, pigment) and filling defects (non-perfusion, occlusion).
  5. What side effects of fluorescein should you warn about?
    Yellow skin and urine, nausea, vasovagal episodes and rarely anaphylaxis; have resuscitation equipment ready.
  6. When is ICG preferred?
    For choroidal disease: polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, CSCR, choroiditis and tumours, as ICG is protein-bound and fluoresces in infrared.
  7. What does a window defect look like?
    Early hyperfluorescence from RPE atrophy that fades with the choroidal flush and does not increase in size.

Station 117: Ocular B-scan ultrasound Investigations

Examiner: A patient with a dense vitreous haemorrhage after a fall has no fundal view. You perform a B-scan.

  1. What are you looking for in vitreous haemorrhage?
    Retinal detachment, retinal tears, posterior vitreous detachment, a foreign body or a tumour.
  2. How do you tell RD from PVD on B-scan?
    RD is thick, highly reflective (100% spike on A-scan), attached at the disc and ora, and moves less; PVD is thin, mobile, may be attached at the disc and has lower reflectivity.

    Smooth, thick, dome-shaped elevations that do not attach to the disc and may touch in the middle (kissing choroidals).

    Fluid in Tenon space around the optic nerve, seen in posterior scleritis.
  7. What are the A-scan uses?
    Axial length for biometry and tissue characterisation of masses (standardised echography).

Station 118: Electrophysiology Investigations

Examiner: A 12-year-old with poor night vision and a near-normal fundus is referred for electrophysiology.

  1. Which test assesses the whole retina?
    The full-field ERG (ISCEV standard), separating rod and cone function.
  2. What do the a-wave and b-wave represent?
    The a-wave comes from photoreceptors; the b-wave from bipolar and Müller cells.
  3. What is an electronegative ERG?
    A b-wave smaller than the a-wave, showing inner retinal dysfunction: X-linked retinoschisis, congenital stationary night blindness, CRAO and melanoma-associated retinopathy.
  4. What does the EOG measure?
    The standing potential of the RPE; the Arden ratio (light peak to dark trough) is normal above 1.8. It is abnormal in Best disease even with a normal ERG.
  5. What does the VEP assess?
    The visual pathway up to the occipital cortex: delayed P100 in demyelination, reduced amplitude in compression.
  6. When is multifocal ERG useful?
    Localised macular dysfunction: hydroxychloroquine toxicity, occult macular dystrophy and early cone disease.
  7. Which conditions give an extinguished ERG?
    Advanced retinitis pigmentosa, Leber congenital amaurosis, total retinal detachment and ophthalmic artery occlusion.

Station 119: Ocular anaesthesia General and basic sciences

Examiner: Before cataract surgery, a patient on clopidogrel asks which anaesthetic you will use and what the risks are.

  1. Which anaesthetic techniques do you know?
    Topical (with or without intracameral lidocaine), sub-Tenon, peribulbar, retrobulbar and general anaesthesia.

    Topical or sub-Tenon anaesthesia, which avoid sharp needles and have the lowest risk of haemorrhage.

    The trochlear nerve, which lies outside the muscle cone, so the superior oblique may still move.
  7. What does the hyaluronidase add?
    Faster and wider spread of the anaesthetic through tissues.

Station 120: Ocular side effects of systemic drugs General and basic sciences

Examiner: A patient with rheumatoid arthritis has taken hydroxychloroquine for seven years and is referred for screening.

    Tamsulosin and other alpha-1A blockers, making cataract surgery harder.

    Topiramate; treat by stopping the drug and with cycloplegics and steroids, not iridotomy.

Station 122: Eye donation and eye banking General and basic sciences

Examiner: A relative of a patient who has just died asks how eye donation works.

  1. Who can donate eyes?
    Almost anyone, of any age, including people who wore glasses or had cataract surgery; consent is given by the family after death.
  2. Within what time should the eyes be retrieved?
    Within about 6 hours of death; keep the eyelids closed, raise the head, and put wet cotton over the eyes while waiting.
  3. What are absolute contraindications?
    Death from unknown cause, rabies, HIV, hepatitis B or C, syphilis, CJD and other prion diseases, septicaemia, leukaemia and lymphoma, and active infection or tumours of the eye.
  4. What screening is done?
    Donor history and blood tests (HIV, HBV, HCV, syphilis), slit lamp and specular microscopy of the cornea.
  5. How are corneas preserved?
    Short term: moist chamber at 4°C for 24–48 hours; intermediate term: McCarey-Kaufman medium at 4°C for up to about 4 days, or Optisol GS for up to about 14 days; long term: organ culture at 31–37°C for up to 4 weeks.
  6. Is the whole eye or only the cornea removed?
    Either the whole globe (enucleation) or an in-situ corneoscleral rim; there is no disfigurement and funeral arrangements are not delayed.
  7. How does India organise this?
    Under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act; eye banks are registered and follow Eye Bank Association of India standards; National Eye Donation Fortnight is 25 August to 8 September.

Station 123: Informed consent and medicolegal practice General and basic sciences

Examiner: You are about to consent a 70-year-old for cataract surgery in one eye; she has advanced glaucoma in the other.

  1. What are the elements of valid consent?
    Given voluntarily, by a competent patient, who has been informed of the nature, benefits, material risks and alternatives, including no treatment.
  2. What risks do you mention for cataract surgery?
    Common ones (inflammation, raised IOP, PCO needing YAG) and serious ones even if rare (endophthalmitis about 1 in 1,000, retinal detachment, posterior capsule rupture, loss of vision).
  3. Why does her other eye matter?
    She is effectively a one-eyed patient, so the consequences of a complication are greater; this is a material risk she must understand, and the decision may need extra care.
  4. What if the patient lacks capacity?
    Follow local law: decisions in the patient's best interests with the legal guardian or nominated representative, documented clearly; emergency treatment to save sight or life can proceed.
  5. What should be documented?
    The discussion, risks explained, questions asked, the patient's decision, the eye to be operated (marked), the language used and an interpreter if one was needed.
  6. What are common causes of complaints in ophthalmology?
    Wrong IOL or wrong eye, poor communication about expectations, endophthalmitis, delayed diagnosis (giant cell arteritis, retinal detachment) and missed glaucoma.
  7. What safety steps prevent wrong-IOL and wrong-site surgery?
    A WHO surgical safety checklist with time-out, checking biometry against the patient and the eye, marking the eye, and having one person responsible for IOL selection.
