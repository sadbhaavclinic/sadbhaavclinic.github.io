MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Basic Sciences (59 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

Embryology & Development

BS 1. Describe the development of the eye. Discuss the retinal and other ocular disorders related to anomalies of development. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Development of the eye

  • Optic vesicle (neuroectoderm, day 22–25) evaginates from the forebrain; induces the lens placode from surface ectoderm.
  • Optic vesicle invaginates to form the double-layered optic cup: outer layer → RPE, inner layer → neurosensory retina; the optic (choroidal) fissure closes by week 7.
  • Surface ectoderm: lens, corneal and conjunctival epithelium, lacrimal gland, lid skin. Neural crest: corneal stroma and endothelium, trabecular meshwork, iris stroma, sclera, choroid stroma. Mesoderm: extraocular muscles, vascular endothelium.
  • Hyaloid artery and tunica vasculosa lentis regress by birth; macula matures after birth (fovea by about 4 years).

Developmental anomalies (retinal and others)

  • Optic cup: anophthalmia, microphthalmos, coloboma (failed fissure closure), optic pit, morning glory disc, retinal dysplasia.
  • Neural crest: Axenfeld–Rieger, Peters anomaly, congenital glaucoma, sclerocornea.
  • Vascular: persistent fetal vasculature, Bergmeister papilla, Mittendorf dot, persistent pupillary membrane.
  • Lens: congenital cataract, lenticonus, coloboma of the lens.
  • Retina: foveal hypoplasia (albinism, aniridia), myelinated nerve fibres, ROP (incomplete vascularisation).

BS 2. Iridofundal coloboma — embryology (failure of closure of the optic fissure), clinical types, systemic associations (CHARGE, microphthalmos), complications such as retinal detachment, and management. Coloboma of the uveal tract. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Embryology

  • Failure of closure of the optic (embryonic) fissure in weeks 5–7; inferonasal position.

Clinical types

  • Iris (keyhole pupil), ciliary body, lens notch, choroid–retina (white bare sclera with pigmented margins), optic disc; complete or partial; uni- or bilateral.
  • Typical (inferonasal) vs atypical (other meridians, other causes).

Associations

  • Microphthalmos and orbital cyst; CHARGE (coloboma, heart defects, choanal atresia, retarded growth, genital and ear anomalies; CHD7 gene); trisomy 13, cat-eye syndrome, Goldenhar, PAX2 (renal coloboma syndrome).

Complications

  • Retinal detachment (breaks at the coloboma margin, often hard to see); choroidal neovascularisation at the edge; amblyopia, nystagmus, field defects (superior).

Management

  • Refraction and amblyopia therapy; tinted or iris-printed contact lenses for photophobia; prophylactic laser along the margin is debated.
  • RD: vitrectomy with laser along the coloboma margin and silicone oil.
  • Genetic counselling and systemic evaluation.

BS 3. Congenital anomalies of the iris — aniridia, coloboma, persistent pupillary membrane, Peters anomaly: embryology and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Embryology

  • Iris stroma from neural crest; pigment epithelium, sphincter and dilator from neuroectoderm (optic cup rim); pupillary membrane (tunica vasculosa lentis) regresses by the 8th month.

Aniridia

  • PAX6 mutation; iris stump, foveal hypoplasia, nystagmus, keratopathy, cataract, glaucoma; WAGR screening for Wilms tumour in sporadic cases.

Coloboma

  • Failure of closure of the embryonic fissure (5th–7th week), inferonasal; typical (with choroidal and optic disc coloboma) or atypical; associations: CHARGE, Patau, cat-eye syndrome.

Persistent pupillary membrane

  • Strands from the collarette; usually no treatment; laser or surgery if obstructing vision.

Peters anomaly

  • Central corneal opacity with posterior defect (Descemet and endothelium), iridocorneal adhesions (type 1) or lens adhesion (type 2); PAX6, FOXC1, PITX2; Peters plus syndrome.

Management

  • Refraction and amblyopia treatment, tinted or iris contact lenses, optical iridectomy or keratoplasty (Peters), glaucoma surveillance, genetic counselling, systemic evaluation.

BS 4. Describe the anterior segment of the newborn and its clinical significance; fundus changes in the newborn. · outline

Anterior segment of the newborn

  • Cornea: horizontal diameter about 9.5–10.5 mm (over 11 mm suggests congenital glaucoma), clearer by 2 weeks; steeper curvature.
  • Anterior chamber shallow; angle not fully developed; iris pale blue (pigmentation develops in the first year); pupil small (immature dilator).
  • Lens more spherical; hyperopia about +2 D; axial length about 16.5–17 mm.
  • Lacrimal secretion limited for first weeks; nasolacrimal duct may be blocked.

Clinical significance

  • Congenital glaucoma detection (corneal size, Haab striae), ROP-related anterior segment changes, IOL power choices in infants (rapid axial growth), difficult dilatation, congenital NLDO.

Fundus in the newborn

  • Pale disc, poorly developed foveal reflex (fovea matures by 4–6 years), retinal haemorrhages after vaginal delivery (common, resolve within weeks; distinguish from abusive head trauma), peripheral avascular retina in preterm babies.
BS 5. Persistent pupillary membrane. · outline

Persistent pupillary membrane

  • Remnant of the anterior tunica vasculosa lentis; fine iris strands arising from the collarette crossing the pupil, sometimes attached to the lens (with anterior capsular cataract).

Significance

  • Usually visually insignificant; large membranes may cause amblyopia; distinguish from posterior synechiae (which arise from the pupil margin).

Management

  • Observation; dilatation and amblyopia therapy; Nd:YAG laser lysis or surgical excision for dense central membranes.
BS 6. Microphthalmos and nanophthalmos. DNB / UNIV · outline

Microphthalmos

  • Axial length 2 standard deviations below normal for age (under 20 mm in adults); simple (normal structure) or complex (with coloboma, cataract, PFV); associations: CHARGE, fetal alcohol, rubella, SOX2, OTX2.
  • Management: refraction, amblyopia therapy, expanding orbital implants or conformers to stimulate orbital growth when severe.

Nanophthalmos

  • Small eye (axial length under 20.5 mm) with normal shape, thick sclera, high hyperopia, crowded anterior segment; MFRP and other genes.
  • Complications: angle-closure glaucoma, uveal effusion (especially after surgery), choroidal folds.
  • Management: LPI, careful lens surgery with prophylactic sclerotomies, avoid miotics, low threshold for treating angle closure.
BS 7. Congenital anomalies of the optic disc — morning glory anomaly, optic pit and disc coloboma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Morning glory anomaly

  • Large funnel-shaped excavated disc with central glial tuft and radial vessels; unilateral; associated with basal encephalocele, moyamoya disease (MRI and MRA), serous RD.

Optic disc pit

  • Grey oval depression usually inferotemporal on the disc; maculopathy (schisis and serous detachment) in up to half; treatment: observation, laser, vitrectomy with gas, ILM flap.

Optic disc coloboma

  • Glistening white excavation inferiorly due to incomplete closure of the embryonic fissure; associations: CHARGE, renal coloboma syndrome (PAX2); RD risk.

Others

  • Optic nerve hypoplasia (double-ring sign, septo-optic dysplasia: endocrine tests), tilted disc, peripapillary staphyloma, megalopapilla.

Revise from: Congenital Optic Disc Anomalies: Hypoplasia, Coloboma, Morning Glory, Optic Pit and More

BS 8. Congenital anomalies of the eyelids and cornea — cryptophthalmos, megalocornea and others. DNB / UNIV · outline

Eyelid anomalies

  • Cryptophthalmos (Fraser syndrome: skin continuous over the globe), ablepharon, coloboma of the lid (Treacher Collins, Goldenhar), epicanthus, blepharophimosis (FOXL2: ptosis, telecanthus, epicanthus inversus), distichiasis, euryblepharon, congenital ptosis, entropion and epiblepharon, ankyloblepharon.

Corneal anomalies

  • Megalocornea (over 13 mm, X-linked, normal IOP; distinguish from buphthalmos), microcornea (under 10 mm), cornea plana, sclerocornea, Peters anomaly, posterior embryotoxon and Axenfeld–Rieger, congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy, dermoids (Goldenhar), keratectasia.

Management

  • Protect cornea (cryptophthalmos, coloboma: urgent lid repair), treat amblyopia, monitor glaucoma, keratoplasty for opacities, genetic counselling.

BS 9. Describe the development, anatomy, composition, metabolism and attachments of the vitreous, and the methods of its clinical examination. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Development

  • Primary vitreous (hyaloid system), secondary vitreous (definitive, avascular collagen), tertiary vitreous (zonules).

Anatomy and attachments

  • About 4 mL, 80% of globe volume; cortex and central vitreous; Cloquet’s canal.
  • Attachments: vitreous base (strongest, straddling the ora serrata), optic disc margin, macula, along retinal vessels, Wieger’s ligament (to the posterior lens capsule, Berger’s space).

Composition and metabolism

  • 98–99% water; type II collagen fibrils and hyaluronan; ascorbate, low protein; ageing causes liquefaction (synchysis) and collapse (syneresis) leading to posterior vitreous detachment.

Clinical examination

  • Slit lamp with a +90 D or contact lens; indirect ophthalmoscopy; B-scan ultrasound (opacities, PVD, haemorrhage); OCT for vitreomacular interface; Weiss ring for PVD.
BS 10. Describe the genetic control of ocular development (PAX6, SOX2, OTX2) and its relevance to congenital anomalies. · outline

Genetic control of eye development

  • PAX6: master control gene for eye development (lens, cornea, iris, retina); mutations cause aniridia, Peters anomaly, foveal hypoplasia.
  • SOX2: optic cup and retina development; mutations cause anophthalmia and microphthalmia.
  • OTX2: anterior neural plate and RPE; anophthalmia, microphthalmia, pituitary problems.
  • Others: RAX (eye field), VSX2 (CHX10: microphthalmia), PITX2 and FOXC1 (Axenfeld–Rieger), PAX2 (renal coloboma), SHH (holoprosencephaly, cyclopia), MITF (RPE).

Relevance

  • Molecular diagnosis of congenital anomalies, genetic counselling, systemic associations (pituitary with OTX2, renal with PAX2), future gene therapy.

Anatomy & Physiology

BS 11. Describe the structure and function of the pigmented epithelium throughout the eye. Discuss the retinal pigment epithelium and its functional significance. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pigmented epithelia of the eye

  • RPE (retina), pigmented ciliary epithelium (outer layer), iris pigment epithelium (posterior two layers): all from the outer layer of the optic cup.

RPE: structure

  • Single layer of hexagonal cells, apical microvilli interdigitate with photoreceptor outer segments, basal infoldings on Bruch’s membrane, tight junctions (outer blood–retinal barrier), melanosomes and lipofuscin.

RPE: functions

  • Phagocytosis of shed outer segments; visual cycle (11-cis retinal regeneration; RPE65); transport of nutrients and ions, pumping fluid out of the subretinal space (keeps retina attached); absorbing stray light; secreting growth factors (VEGF, PEDF); immune privilege.

Clinical significance

  • AMD, RPE65-associated Leber congenital amaurosis (voretigene gene therapy), Best disease, CSCR, retinitis pigmentosa, RPE tears, CHRPE, drug toxicity (hydroxychloroquine).
BS 12. Describe the blood-aqueous and blood-retinal barriers. Discuss their role in health and disease, with special reference to drugs acting on them. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Blood–aqueous barrier

  • Tight junctions of the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium and iris vascular endothelium.

Blood–retinal barrier

  • Inner: tight junctions of retinal capillary endothelium (with pericytes). Outer: RPE tight junctions.

In health

  • Maintain clear media, protein-poor aqueous, immune privilege, stable retinal environment.

In disease

  • Breakdown: uveitis (flare, cells), diabetes (macular oedema), CRVO, surgery (CME), trauma; fluorescein leakage on angiography.

Drugs and barriers

  • Barriers limit systemic drugs reaching the eye: lipophilic, small, unbound drugs penetrate better (fluoroquinolones, linezolid, voriconazole); inflamed eyes allow more entry.
  • Hence intravitreal, periocular and implant delivery; steroids and anti-VEGF stabilise a broken barrier.

BS 13. Describe the anatomy and physiology of rods and cones (including electron microscopy) and the photochemistry of vision. What factors influence dark and light adaptation? HIGH-YIELD · outline

Rods and cones

  • Rods (about 120 million): rhodopsin, scotopic vision, high sensitivity, peripheral; peak density about 20° from fixation.
  • Cones (about 6 million): three opsins (S, M, L), photopic and colour vision, central (fovea only cones).
  • Electron microscopy: outer segment (stacked discs in rods, folded membrane in cones), connecting cilium, inner segment (ellipsoid with mitochondria, myoid), nucleus, synaptic terminal (rod spherule, cone pedicle).

Photochemistry (phototransduction)

  • Light isomerises 11-cis retinal to all-trans → activated rhodopsin (metarhodopsin II) → transducin → phosphodiesterase → cGMP falls → cation channels close → hyperpolarisation → reduced glutamate release.
  • Visual cycle regenerates 11-cis retinal in the RPE (RPE65) or via Müller cells for cones.

Dark and light adaptation

  • Dark adaptation curve: cone limb (about 7–10 min), rod–cone break, rod limb (about 30 min).
  • Influences: pre-adapting light intensity, vitamin A status, age, pupil size, retinal disease (RP, congenital stationary night blindness, fundus albipunctatus), drugs.

Revise from: Electro-Oculography (EOG): Arden Ratio and Best Disease

BS 14. Microanatomy of the retina and the macula, with diagrams. DNB / UNIV · outline

Layers of the retina (outer to inner)

  • RPE, photoreceptor outer and inner segments, external limiting membrane, outer nuclear layer, outer plexiform layer (Henle fibre layer at the macula), inner nuclear layer, inner plexiform layer, ganglion cell layer, nerve fibre layer, internal limiting membrane.

Macula

  • Area 5.5 mm diameter between the arcades with xanthophyll pigment; fovea (1.5 mm), foveola (0.35 mm: cones only, no inner layers), foveal avascular zone (about 0.5 mm), umbo.
  • High cone density, midget ganglion cell circuits (1:1), displaced inner layers, Müller cell cone.

Clinical relevance on OCT

  • Layer-specific diseases: ellipsoid zone loss (dystrophies, hydroxychloroquine), INL cysts (DME), Henle fibre layer haemorrhage, GCL thinning (glaucoma).
BS 15. Anterior and posterior ciliary circulation, with a diagram; ocular blood flow. DNB / UNIV · outline

Anterior ciliary circulation

  • Muscular arteries along the four recti (two per muscle except LR) → anterior ciliary arteries → perforate the sclera → major arterial circle of the iris (with long posterior ciliary arteries) and episcleral and conjunctival vessels.

Posterior ciliary circulation

  • Two long posterior ciliary arteries (horizontal meridian) to the major arterial circle; 15–20 short posterior ciliary arteries around the optic nerve to the choroid and circle of Zinn–Haller.

Ocular blood flow

  • Retinal: autoregulated, low flow, high oxygen extraction.
  • Choroidal: very high flow (about 85% of ocular blood flow), not well autoregulated, cools and nourishes outer retina.
  • Ocular perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure (two-thirds) − IOP; measurement: laser Doppler, colour Doppler imaging, OCT angiography.

Clinical relevance

  • Anterior segment ischaemia (strabismus surgery on 3 or more recti), ciliary flush, ischaemic optic neuropathy, glaucoma perfusion.
BS 16. Anatomy of the iris and its function. DNB / UNIV · outline

Anatomy of the iris

  • Anterior border layer, stroma (collagen, melanocytes, vessels), sphincter pupillae (circular, parasympathetic), dilator pupillae (myoepithelium, sympathetic), two-layered posterior pigment epithelium.
  • Collarette divides pupillary and ciliary zones; crypts; root is thinnest (iridodialysis).
  • Blood supply: major arterial circle at the root, minor circle at the collarette.

Function

  • Controls light entering the eye (pupil diameter 2–8 mm), increases depth of focus, reduces aberrations, part of the blood–aqueous barrier; pigment protects against stray light.

Clinical relevance

  • Heterochromia, iris atrophy (herpetic, ICE), transillumination defects (albinism, PDS), rubeosis, iris nodules and tumours.
BS 17. Anatomy of the ciliary body; ciliary ganglion. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ciliary body

  • Ring from the scleral spur to the ora serrata (6 mm): pars plicata (70–80 ciliary processes, aqueous production) and pars plana (4 mm; safe entry for vitrectomy).
  • Ciliary muscle: longitudinal (Brücke: to scleral spur), radial, circular (Müller: accommodation); parasympathetic via short ciliary nerves.
  • Double-layered epithelium (non-pigmented inner: aqueous secretion, blood–aqueous barrier; pigmented outer).

Functions

  • Aqueous production, accommodation, uveoscleral outflow, anchoring zonules, secretion of hyaluronic acid.

Ciliary ganglion

  • Parasympathetic ganglion lateral to the optic nerve about 1 cm in front of the apex.
  • Roots: motor (parasympathetic from the inferior division of the third nerve), sensory (nasociliary), sympathetic (passes through).
  • Branches: short ciliary nerves to sphincter and ciliary muscle.
  • Clinical: Adie tonic pupil, retrobulbar block, injury in orbital surgery.

Revise from: Anatomy of the Anterior Chamber Angle for Gonioscopy

BS 18. Choroid, choriocapillaris and Bruch's membrane — anatomy and functions. DNB / UNIV · outline

Choroid

  • Vascular layer between retina and sclera: suprachoroid, Haller (large vessels), Sattler (medium), choriocapillaris (fenestrated capillaries, lobular), Bruch’s membrane; thickest at the macula (about 250–300 µm).

Bruch’s membrane

  • Five layers: RPE basement membrane, inner collagenous, elastic, outer collagenous, choriocapillaris basement membrane.
  • Thickens with age (lipid deposition: drusen, AMD); breaks cause angioid streaks, lacquer cracks, CNV.

Functions

  • Nourishes outer retina, heat dissipation, absorbs stray light, regulates IOP and eye growth (choroidal thickness changes in myopia).

Clinical relevance

  • Pachychoroid disease (CSCR, PCV), choroidal thinning in myopia and ageing, choroidal tumours, choroidal detachment, EDI-OCT measurement.
BS 19. Autonomic innervation of the eye; drugs acting on the pupil. DNB / UNIV · outline

Parasympathetic supply

  • Edinger–Westphal nucleus → third nerve → ciliary ganglion → short ciliary nerves → sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle (miosis, accommodation); lacrimal gland via facial nerve (greater petrosal) and pterygopalatine ganglion.

Sympathetic supply

  • Hypothalamus → ciliospinal centre (C8–T2) → superior cervical ganglion → carotid plexus → long ciliary nerves (dilator), Müller muscle, blood vessels.

Drugs acting on the pupil

  • Miotics: pilocarpine (muscarinic agonist), carbachol, physostigmine and echothiophate (anticholinesterases), opioids, brimonidine (mild).
  • Mydriatics: phenylephrine (alpha-1 agonist), cocaine (blocks noradrenaline reuptake), apraclonidine (in Horner), hydroxyamphetamine.
  • Cycloplegic mydriatics: atropine, homatropine, cyclopentolate, tropicamide.
  • Systemic drugs: tamsulosin (floppy iris syndrome), antihistamines and antidepressants (angle closure risk).
BS 20. Supports of the eyeball and Tenon's capsule. DNB / UNIV · outline

Supports of the eyeball

  • Tenon’s capsule (fascia bulbi): fibrous sheath from the limbus to the optic nerve, separating the globe from orbital fat; pierced by muscles and vessels.
  • Suspensory ligament of Lockwood: hammock below the globe formed by the inferior rectus and inferior oblique sheaths with Tenon; maintains globe position after maxillectomy.
  • Check ligaments of medial and lateral recti.
  • Orbital fat, extraocular muscles and their pulleys, orbital septum.

Clinical relevance

  • Sub-Tenon anaesthesia and injections, Tenon cysts after glaucoma surgery, enucleation implant placement inside Tenon’s capsule, posterior Tenon in strabismus surgery, Lockwood ligament preserves globe level when floor is removed.
BS 21. Ageing changes in the anterior and posterior segments of the eye. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ageing changes: anterior segment

  • Lids: dermatochalasis, laxity (ectropion, entropion), ptosis (aponeurotic).
  • Conjunctiva: conjunctivochalasis, pinguecula; dry eye (meibomian gland dysfunction).
  • Cornea: arcus senilis, endothelial cell loss (about 0.6% per year), Hassall–Henle bodies, increased against-the-rule astigmatism.
  • Iris and pupil: senile miosis, iris atrophy.
  • Lens: increasing size, loss of accommodation (presbyopia), cataract, shallowing AC (angle closure risk), yellowing.

Posterior segment

  • Vitreous liquefaction and PVD, asteroid hyalosis.
  • Retina: drusen, RPE changes, reduced photoreceptor and ganglion cell numbers, thinner RNFL, paving-stone degeneration.
  • Choroid: thinning; Bruch’s membrane thickening.
  • Optic nerve: loss of axons; reduced dark adaptation, contrast sensitivity and colour discrimination.

Revise from: Geriatric Optometry: Ageing Eye Changes, Prescribing for Older Adults and Falls

BS 22. Macular pigments in health and disease. · outline

Macular pigments

  • Lutein, zeaxanthin and meso-zeaxanthin (carotenoids from diet); concentrated in the Henle fibre layer and inner retina at the fovea.

Functions

  • Filter blue light (peak absorption 460 nm), antioxidant protection, reduce chromatic aberration and glare, may improve contrast.

Measurement

  • Heterochromatic flicker photometry, autofluorescence, Raman spectroscopy.

In disease

  • Low levels in AMD (AREDS2: lutein and zeaxanthin replaced beta-carotene), macular telangiectasia type 2 (loss of pigment), Sjögren–Larsson syndrome, smokers; supplements raise levels.
BS 23. Discuss visual perception and its evaluation. · outline

Visual perception

  • Light sense (threshold, dark adaptation), form sense (visual acuity, contrast sensitivity), colour sense, depth perception (stereopsis), motion perception, visual field, recognition and interpretation (higher cortical function).

Processing pathways

  • Parvocellular (detail, colour), magnocellular (motion, contrast), koniocellular (blue-yellow); dorsal stream (where: parietal) and ventral stream (what: temporal).

Evaluation

  • Acuity (Snellen, logMAR), contrast (Pelli–Robson), colour (Ishihara, Farnsworth–Munsell 100 hue, anomaloscope), dark adaptation, fields (perimetry), stereopsis tests, electrophysiology (ERG, VEP), higher visual function tests (object recognition, prosopagnosia, visual neglect).
BS 24. Protective mechanisms of the eye. DNB / UNIV · outline

Protective mechanisms of the eye

  • Bony orbit and orbital rim, eyelids and blink reflex (corneal reflex, menace reflex), eyelashes, Bell phenomenon.
  • Tear film: mechanical washing, lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA, beta-lysin, complement.
  • Conjunctiva: CALT (lymphoid tissue), normal flora, epithelial barrier, goblet cell mucins.
  • Cornea: tight epithelial junctions, rapid healing, immune privilege, avascularity.
  • Blood–ocular barriers; immune privilege and ACAID; anterior chamber-associated immune deviation.
  • Pupil (light regulation), macular pigments and lens (UV and blue filtering), antioxidants.
BS 25. Describe the circadian and non-image-forming visual system (intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells) and its clinical relevance. · outline

Non-image-forming visual system

  • Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs; about 1–2% of ganglion cells) contain melanopsin (peak about 480 nm, blue).
  • Project via the retinohypothalamic tract to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian clock), pretectal olivary nucleus (pupillary light reflex), and others.

Functions

  • Circadian entrainment, melatonin suppression, pupil light reflex (sustained), sleep regulation, mood, photophobia (migraine).

Clinical relevance

  • Blind patients without light perception may still have pupil reflexes and circadian entrainment (if ipRGCs survive) and those with enucleation get non-24-hour sleep disorder.
  • Cataract surgery and blue-filtering IOLs (sleep), chromatic pupillometry (glaucoma, LHON spares ipRGCs relatively), screen-time and light exposure advice.
BS 57. The retinoid (visual) cycle and diseases caused by its disruption — RPE65 dystrophy, Stargardt disease and vitamin A deficiency; therapeutic targets. NEW · outline

The retinoid (visual) cycle

  • Light isomerises 11-cis retinal (bound to opsin) to all-trans retinal, triggering phototransduction (transducin → phosphodiesterase → closure of cGMP-gated channels → hyperpolarisation).
  • All-trans retinal leaves the outer segment (ABCA4 flippase helps), is reduced to all-trans retinol (RDH8) and moves to the RPE (bound to IRBP).
  • In the RPE: esterified by LRAT, isomerised by RPE65 to 11-cis retinol, oxidised (RDH5) to 11-cis retinal and returned to photoreceptors.
  • Cones also use a faster intraretinal (Müller cell) cycle.
  • Vitamin A is delivered from the liver bound to retinol-binding protein (STRA6 receptor).

Diseases from disruption

  • RPE65 and LRAT mutations: Leber congenital amaurosis and early-onset retinal dystrophy (no chromophore).
  • ABCA4 (Stargardt): toxic bisretinoids (A2E) accumulate as lipofuscin in the RPE.
  • RDH5: fundus albipunctatus (slow dark adaptation); RLBP1: retinitis punctata albescens, Bothnia dystrophy.
  • Vitamin A deficiency: night blindness, xerophthalmia (also after bariatric surgery and in liver disease).
  • Drugs: isotretinoin (night vision problems), fenretinide.

Therapeutic targets

  • Gene therapy (voretigene neparvovec for RPE65), oral synthetic chromophore (9-cis retinyl acetate, trials), visual cycle modulators to slow bisretinoid build-up (emixustat, tinlarebant), deuterated vitamin A (gildeuretinol), vitamin A supplementation for deficiency; avoid vitamin A supplements in Stargardt.

Revise from: Inherited Retinal Dystrophies: RP, Stargardt, Best and More

Ocular Pharmacology & Therapeutics

BS 26. Classify immunosuppressants and immunomodulators (including cyclosporine). Discuss their role in various ophthalmic disorders. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Corticosteroids; antimetabolites (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate); T-cell inhibitors (calcineurin inhibitors: cyclosporine, tacrolimus); alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, chlorambucil); biologics (anti-TNF: adalimumab, infliximab; anti-IL-6: tocilizumab; anti-CD20: rituximab); immunomodulators (interferon alfa).

Cyclosporine

  • Inhibits calcineurin and IL-2 production by T cells; systemic in uveitis (Behçet, VKH); topical 0.05–0.1% in dry eye and vernal keratoconjunctivitis; nephrotoxicity and hypertension systemically.

Role in eye disease

  • Non-infectious uveitis: steroid-sparing (methotrexate, mycophenolate), adalimumab (VISUAL trials), JIA uveitis (methotrexate, adalimumab).

BS 28. Factors affecting corneal drug penetration — corneal barriers, partition coefficient and formulation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneal barriers

  • Epithelium (lipophilic, tight junctions: main barrier to hydrophilic drugs), stroma (hydrophilic, barrier to lipophilic drugs), endothelium (minor).
  • Ideal drug: biphasic (both lipophilic and hydrophilic: partition coefficient), small molecular weight, unionised form crosses epithelium.

Factors

  • Drug: concentration, molecular size, partition coefficient, pKa and pH, prodrugs (dipivefrin, latanoprost ester).
  • Formulation: viscosity agents (prolong contact), gels and ointments, suspensions, preservatives (BAK opens tight junctions), penetration enhancers, nanoparticles.
  • Ocular: tear turnover and drainage (only about 5% of drop reaches the AC), epithelial defects increase penetration, inflammation.
  • Patient: drop size (about 30–50 µL vs cul-de-sac capacity 7–10 µL), punctal occlusion, spacing drops by 5 minutes.

  • Inhibit cyclo-oxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2), reducing prostaglandin synthesis.

Agents

  • Topical: ketorolac 0.4–0.5%, diclofenac 0.1%, nepafenac 0.1% and 0.3% (prodrug), bromfenac 0.09%, flurbiprofen 0.03% (intraoperative miosis prevention).

  • Prevention of intraoperative miosis, post-operative inflammation and pain, prevention and treatment of pseudophakic CME (especially diabetics), allergic conjunctivitis (ketorolac), pain after PRK, non-necrotising scleritis and episcleritis.

  • Stinging, corneal melt (especially with generic diclofenac, dry eye, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis), delayed epithelial healing; systemic: gastric, renal, cardiovascular.

BS 34. Role of antioxidants in the eye and in ophthalmology. · outline

Oxidative stress in the eye

  • Light, high oxygen tension and metabolic activity generate reactive oxygen species; damage lens proteins (cataract), RPE and photoreceptors (AMD), trabecular meshwork and ganglion cells (glaucoma), cornea (dry eye, keratoconus).

Antioxidants in the eye

  • Enzymatic: superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase.
  • Non-enzymatic: glutathione (lens), ascorbate (aqueous: high concentration), vitamin E, carotenoids (lutein, zeaxanthin), melanin.

Role in ophthalmology

  • AREDS and AREDS2 supplements reduce progression to advanced AMD by about 25% in intermediate disease.
  • Cataract prevention: no proven benefit from supplements.
  • Glaucoma: nicotinamide (vitamin B3) neuroprotection under trial; corneal cross-linking (riboflavin is a photosensitiser).
  • Dry eye: omega-3 fatty acids (DREAM: no benefit).
BS 35. Collagen shields — principles and uses. · outline

Collagen shields

  • Porcine or bovine collagen shaped like a contact lens; dissolve in 12, 24 or 72 hours.

Uses

  • Drug delivery (soaked in antibiotics or steroids: sustained release), bandage after surgery or trauma (epithelial healing), corneal protection (dry eye, exposure), after refractive surgery.

Limitations

  • Discomfort, blurred vision, may dislodge, reduced oxygen; largely replaced by bandage contact lenses and sustained-release devices.
BS 36. Pulse steroid therapy in ophthalmology — indications (optic neuritis, traumatic optic neuropathy, early graft rejection, VKH, AION) and protocol. · outline

Pulse steroid therapy

  • High-dose IV methylprednisolone (usually 500 mg–1 g daily for 3 days) followed by oral prednisolone; rapid, powerful anti-inflammatory action through non-genomic effects.

Indications

  • Optic neuritis (ONTT protocol: 250 mg every 6 hours for 3 days then oral 1 mg/kg for 11 days), NMOSD and MOGAD attacks, AAION from giant cell arteritis (1 g daily for 3 days), VKH (acute phase), early severe graft rejection (single dose of 500 mg), sympathetic ophthalmia, severe scleritis, thyroid eye disease (EUGOGO weekly protocol: 4.5 g cumulative), traumatic optic neuropathy (no proven benefit; CRASH showed harm in head injury).

Protocol and precautions

  • Baseline blood sugar, BP, electrolytes, weight, chest X-ray, TB and infection screen; give in hospital or day care; monitor glucose and BP; gastric protection.
  • Side effects: hyperglycaemia, hypertension, mood change, insomnia, infection, avascular necrosis, arrhythmia, liver injury (cumulative doses over 8 g).

BS 38. Botulinum toxin in ophthalmology — mechanism, preparation, indications, contraindications and adverse effects. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Mechanism

  • Botulinum toxin A cleaves SNAP-25 and blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction; effect begins in 2–3 days, lasts about 3 months.

Preparation

  • 100-unit vial reconstituted with preservative-free saline (for example 2.5 mL gives 4 U per 0.1 mL); use within the time stated in the product information.

Indications

  • Blepharospasm, hemifacial spasm, strabismus (sixth nerve palsy, small-angle and infantile esotropia), protective ptosis for corneal exposure, lacrimal gland hypersecretion, cosmetic.

Contraindications

  • Myasthenia gravis and Lambert–Eaton, pregnancy and breastfeeding, infection at site, aminoglycoside use, allergy.

Adverse effects

  • Ptosis, diplopia, lagophthalmos and exposure keratitis, ectropion, dry eye, bruising; antibody formation with frequent high doses.

BS 39. Tissue adhesives in ophthalmology — cyanoacrylate and fibrin glue. DNB / UNIV · outline

Cyanoacrylate glue

  • Butyl or octyl cyanoacrylate: polymerises on contact with moisture, strong, bacteriostatic; applied as a thin layer to a dry de-epithelialised bed and covered with a bandage contact lens.

  • Fibrinogen and thrombin mixture (Tisseel): biological, biodegradable, faster and less toxic, less tensile strength.

Anti-TNF agents

BS 42. Principles of ocular pharmacokinetics — routes of drug delivery, sustained-release systems and nanotechnology-based drug delivery. · outline

Routes of drug delivery

  • Topical (drops, gels, ointments): anterior segment; about 5% reaches AC.
  • Periocular (subconjunctival, sub-Tenon, peribulbar, retrobulbar): bypass corneal barrier.
  • Intracameral and intravitreal: high local levels, posterior segment.
  • Suprachoroidal: triamcinolone (Xipere) for uveitic macular oedema.
  • Systemic: limited by blood–ocular barriers; lipophilic drugs penetrate better.

  • Non-biodegradable: fluocinolone implants (Retisert, Iluvien, Yutiq), port delivery system (ranibizumab).
  • Biodegradable: dexamethasone implant (Ozurdex), bimatoprost implant (Durysta), travoprost intracameral implant (iDose).
  • Punctal plugs releasing drugs (dexamethasone insert: Dextenza), drug-eluting contact lenses.

Nanotechnology

  • Nanoparticles, nanomicelles (cyclosporine: Cequa), liposomes, dendrimers, niosomes: better penetration and longer residence; gene therapy vectors (AAV).

BS 43. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and their role in ocular inflammation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pro-inflammatory cytokines

  • IL-1 (fever, acute inflammation), IL-6 (acute phase, B-cell growth; uveitic CME), TNF-alpha (macrophage activation, granulomas), IL-17 and IL-23 (Th17 cells: uveitis, scleritis), IFN-gamma (Th1 response), IL-8 (neutrophil chemotaxis), VEGF (permeability and neovascularisation), MCP-1 (monocytes).

BS 44. Hypersensitivity reactions in the eye — examples, features and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types (Gell and Coombs)

  • Type I (IgE, immediate): seasonal and perennial allergic conjunctivitis, VKC, AKC (partly), drug allergy.
  • Type II (cytotoxic antibody): ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, Mooren’s ulcer (partly).
  • Type III (immune complex): scleritis and peripheral ulcerative keratitis in rheumatoid arthritis, marginal keratitis, Stevens–Johnson (partly).
  • Type IV (delayed, T cell): phlyctenulosis, contact dermatoblepharitis, corneal graft rejection, sympathetic ophthalmia.

BS 45. Ocular immune privilege and anterior chamber-associated immune deviation (ACAID). DNB / UNIV · outline

Ocular immune privilege

  • Reduced immune response in the eye to protect vision from inflammatory damage.
  • Mechanisms: blood–ocular barriers, absence of lymphatics in the cornea and AC, immunosuppressive aqueous (TGF-beta, alpha-MSH, VIP), Fas ligand on ocular cells (apoptosis of activated T cells), low MHC class II expression on corneal cells, complement regulatory proteins.

ACAID

  • Antigens introduced into the AC induce systemic tolerance: suppression of delayed-type hypersensitivity, with preserved antibody response.
  • Pathway: F4/80+ antigen-presenting cells carry antigen through the blood to the spleen, where regulatory T cells are generated (with NKT cells and B cells).

Clinical relevance

  • High success of corneal grafts (immune privilege), failure in vascularised or inflamed beds; sympathetic ophthalmia when privilege breaks down; tumour tolerance (uveal melanoma); gene therapy and cell transplant design.

BS 47. Apoptosis and its role in ocular disease. DNB / UNIV · outline

Apoptosis

  • Programmed, energy-dependent cell death without inflammation: cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, apoptotic bodies phagocytosed.
  • Pathways: intrinsic (mitochondrial: cytochrome c, Bcl-2 family) and extrinsic (death receptors: Fas, TNF), both activate caspases.

Role in ocular disease

  • Glaucoma (ganglion cell apoptosis), retinitis pigmentosa and inherited dystrophies (photoreceptor apoptosis), AMD (RPE), diabetic retinopathy (pericytes and endothelium), corneal keratocyte apoptosis after epithelial injury and refractive surgery (haze), immune privilege (Fas ligand), lens development (organelle loss), retinoblastoma (loss of RB1 control of the cell cycle).

Therapeutic relevance

  • Neuroprotection research (anti-apoptotic agents, neurotrophic factors such as CNTF), gene therapy.

BS 58. The ocular surface microbiome and its role in eye disease. NEW · outline

Ocular surface microbiome

  • Low-biomass, relatively stable community on the conjunctiva and lids; core genera: Corynebacterium, Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium), coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, Streptococcus; also fungi (Malassezia) and viruses (torque teno virus); Demodex on lids.
  • Studied with culture and 16S rRNA or metagenomic sequencing (contamination is a major problem).

Role in health

  • Competes with pathogens, trains local immunity (IL-17 response in conjunctiva shown with Corynebacterium mastitidis in mice), maintains the barrier with tear proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA).

Role in disease

  • Dysbiosis reported in dry eye, MGD and blepharitis (Demodex, Staphylococcus), contact lens wear (more skin-like flora), trachoma, Sjögren syndrome, diabetes, allergic disease; source of endophthalmitis organisms (coagulase-negative Staphylococcus) after surgery.
  • Gut–eye axis: gut dysbiosis linked with uveitis (HLA-B27 disease), AMD, glaucoma and Sjögren in studies.

Clinical implications

  • Povidone-iodine before surgery and avoiding unnecessary topical antibiotics (resistance, dysbiosis); lid hygiene; probiotics and diet under research.

BS 53. Gene therapy in ophthalmology — principles, vectors, voretigene neparvovec and the current pipeline. · outline

Principles of gene therapy

  • Gene augmentation (adding a normal copy for recessive loss-of-function mutations), gene editing (CRISPR-Cas9: EDIT-101 for CEP290), gene silencing (antisense oligonucleotides: sepofarsen), optogenetics (light-sensitive proteins into surviving cells), neurotrophic factor delivery.

Why the eye suits gene therapy

  • Small, accessible, immune privileged, the other eye can serve as a control, measurable outcomes (OCT, ERG, vision).

Vectors

  • Adeno-associated virus (AAV: commonest, safe, limited cargo about 4.7 kb), lentivirus (larger cargo), non-viral (nanoparticles); delivered subretinally (vitrectomy and bleb) or intravitreally.

Voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna)

  • AAV2 carrying RPE65; approved 2017 (FDA) for biallelic RPE65-mediated retinal dystrophy with viable cells; improves light sensitivity and mobility (MLMT test).

Pipeline

  • X-linked RP (RPGR), choroideremia (CHM), achromatopsia, LHON (ND4), anti-VEGF gene therapy for wet AMD (ixo-vec, RGX-314), complement gene therapy for GA, optogenetic therapy for advanced RP.
BS 54. Genetic counselling in inherited eye disease — modes of inheritance, genetic testing and ethical issues. · outline

Modes of inheritance

  • Autosomal dominant (50% risk to children; variable expressivity, reduced penetrance), autosomal recessive (25% risk for siblings; consanguinity common in India), X-linked recessive (carrier mothers: 50% of sons affected; daughters carriers), mitochondrial (maternal).

Genetic testing

  • Gene panels, exome or genome sequencing, targeted testing for family members, prenatal and pre-implantation genetic diagnosis; results: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance.

Counselling process

  • Pedigree (three generations), confirm the diagnosis, explain inheritance and risks, testing options, prognosis, available treatments and trials, reproductive options, support groups; non-directive counselling.

Ethical issues

  • Consent (especially children: test only if it helps management), confidentiality and family disclosure, incidental findings, discrimination (employment, insurance), cost, prenatal testing decisions, data privacy.
BS 59. Oculomics — the retina as a marker of systemic disease (cardiovascular, kidney and neurodegenerative disease). NEW · outline

Oculomics

  • Use of eye images and data (fundus photographs, OCT, OCTA) with artificial intelligence to detect or predict systemic disease; the retina is the only place where microvasculature and neural tissue can be seen directly.

Examples

  • Cardiovascular: retinal vessel calibre and AI-estimated cardiovascular risk, “retinal age gap” predicts mortality.
  • Kidney disease and diabetes: estimate of eGFR and HbA1c from fundus images.
  • Neurodegenerative: retinal nerve fibre and ganglion cell thinning in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease, MS (OCT as a biomarker of neurodegeneration).
  • Others: anaemia (haemoglobin estimation), hypertension, age and sex, smoking status, liver disease.
  • Large datasets: UK Biobank, foundation models (RETFound).

Limitations

  • Mostly association and prediction studies; generalisability across populations and cameras, explainability, ethics and consent for secondary use of data, need for prospective validation before clinical use.

Relevance to ophthalmologists

  • Opportunistic screening during eye visits, collaboration with physicians, data privacy obligations.

Stem Cells & Regenerative Medicine

BS 55. Role of stem cells in ophthalmology — limbal, retinal and other applications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Limbal stem cells

  • Located in the palisades of Vogt; deficiency causes conjunctivalisation of the cornea.
  • Treatment: conjunctival-limbal autograft, keratolimbal allograft, cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation (CLET), simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET, developed in India), cultivated oral mucosal epithelium (COMET).

Retinal applications

  • RPE cell sheets from embryonic or induced pluripotent stem cells for AMD; photoreceptor replacement and retinal organoids (experimental).

Others

  • Corneal endothelial cell injection therapy, trabecular meshwork and optic nerve regeneration (research).

Concerns

  • Tumour formation, immune rejection, ethics, unproven commercial “stem cell” clinics.
BS 56. Induced pluripotent stem cells and RPE transplantation — current status and challenges. · outline

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)

  • Adult cells (skin fibroblasts, blood) reprogrammed with Yamanaka factors (OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, c-MYC) into pluripotent cells; can be differentiated into RPE and photoreceptors; autologous (no rejection) or HLA-matched banks.

RPE transplantation

  • First autologous iPSC-RPE sheet transplant in wet AMD (Takahashi, Japan, 2014); embryonic stem cell-derived RPE patches on scaffolds for AMD (California Project, London Project); safety shown, efficacy limited.

Challenges

  • Tumour risk (teratoma, genetic instability), cost and time for autologous cells, immune rejection with allogeneic cells, integration and survival, surgical delivery, scaling up, regulation.

Other uses

  • Disease modelling (retinal organoids), drug screening, limbal stem cells (cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation), corneal endothelial cell injection (Kinoshita).
