Lasers in Ophthalmology: Wavelengths, Settings and Safety

Last updated: October 2026

A quick reference to laser and light wavelengths used in ophthalmology, typical starting settings and key safety facts. For how lasers affect tissue and why the colour matters, see Lasers in Ophthalmology Made Easy.

Typical starting settings

These are common starting points from standard texts. Always titrate to the tissue response, and follow your laser's manual and local protocols.

ProcedureLaserStarting settings
Nd:YAG peripheral iridotomyQ-switched Nd:YAG2–8 mJ per pulse, 1–3 pulses per burst; Abraham lens; superior or temporal
Pre-treatment of thick brown iridesArgon or 532 nm50 µm, 0.02–0.05 s, 600–1000 mW, then YAG
Selective laser trabeculoplastyQ-switched 532 nm400 µm (fixed), 3 ns, 0.6–1.2 mJ (just below bubble formation); about 100 spots over 360°
Argon laser trabeculoplastyArgon50 µm, 0.1 s, 400–1200 mW; 50 spots per 180°
Peripheral iridoplastyArgon or 532 nm200–500 µm, 0.3–0.5 s, 200–400 mW; contraction burns on the far periphery
Transscleral cyclophotocoagulationDiode 810 nm (G-probe)1250–2000 mW, 2000 ms; probe edge at the limbus (fibre about 1.2 mm behind); spare 3 and 9 o'clock
Panretinal photocoagulation532 nm200–500 µm on the retina, 100–200 ms (20–30 ms with pattern lasers); power to a light grey-white burn
Focal / grid macular laser532 or 577 nm50–100 µm, 50–100 ms, mild burns (modified ETDRS)

Laser physics in brief

TermMeaning
Continuous waveConstant output, measured in watts (photocoagulation)
Q-switchedEnergy released in nanosecond pulses; high peak power (Nd:YAG capsulotomy, SLT)
Mode-lockedEven shorter (pico- or femtosecond) pulses with very high peak power, not more total energy
Solid-state lasersLasing ions in a crystal or glass: ruby, Nd:YAG, Nd:YLF, Er:YAG
Gas lasersArgon, krypton, helium–neon (aiming beams), carbon dioxide
Excimer ("excited dimer") lasersNoble gas plus halogen (ArF, XeCl) emitting ultraviolet
Dye lasersOrganic dyes in solution; tunable wavelength
Diode (semiconductor) lasersCompact, efficient, usually red to infrared; usually grouped separately from crystal lasers

Safety

Lasers are classified under IEC 60825-1 into classes 1, 1M, 2, 2M, 3R, 3B and 4. Aiming beams are class 2 or 3R; nearly all treatment lasers are class 4, so everyone in the room needs wavelength-specific eyewear. Full table: laser safety classes.

Where residents slip

  • Thinking a mode-locked laser delivers more energy: it delivers shorter pulses with higher peak power.
  • Forgetting that SLT energy is set just below the threshold for bubble formation.
  • Using a laser's nominal spot size without allowing for the contact lens magnification.
