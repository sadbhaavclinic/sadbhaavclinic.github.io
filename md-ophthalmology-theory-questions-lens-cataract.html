MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Lens and Cataract (58 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

Lens Development, Anatomy & Biochemistry

LC 1. Describe the development of the human lens and its structure (sutures, zonules), with reference to the types of congenital cataract and methods for detailed clinical examination and documentation (optical section). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Development

  • Lens placode (surface ectoderm) → lens vesicle (week 5); posterior cells elongate to form primary lens fibres (embryonic nucleus, by week 7); equatorial cells form secondary fibres throughout life (fetal, infantile, adult nucleus, cortex).
  • Y sutures: upright anterior and inverted posterior Y in the fetal nucleus; more complex star sutures in adults.
  • Zonules (tertiary vitreous) from the ciliary epithelium to the equatorial capsule.

Relation to congenital cataract types

  • Embryonic: anterior and posterior polar, central pulverulent; fetal: sutural, nuclear; infantile and later: lamellar (zonular), coronary, blue dot.

Clinical examination and documentation

  • Dilated slit lamp: diffuse, optic section (zones of discontinuity: capsule, cortex, adult, infantile, fetal and embryonic nuclei), retro-illumination; LOCS III grading; Scheimpflug and retroillumination photography; red reflex (Brückner) in children.

LC 2. Functional and surgical anatomy of the lens, and maintenance of transparency of the lens throughout life. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Functional anatomy

  • Biconvex, avascular, about 9–10 mm diameter, 4–5 mm thick, power about 18–20 D in situ; capsule (thickest anteriorly, thinnest at posterior pole: about 4 µm), anterior epithelium, fibres (cortex and nucleus); zonules.

Surgical anatomy

  • Posterior capsule is thin (risk of rupture); anterior capsule elasticity (capsulorhexis); zonular insertion pre-equatorial; Berger space and Wieger ligament; nucleus hardness increases with age.

Maintenance of transparency

  • Regular packing of fibres with loss of organelles, short-range order of crystallins (α, β, γ) at high concentration, minimal extracellular space, avascularity.
  • Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump-leak system, glutathione and ascorbate antioxidants, α-crystallin chaperone activity, gap junctions (connexins) for intercellular transport.
LC 3. Lens metabolism and biochemistry (proteins, glucose pathways); pathogenesis of senile cataract; mechanisms of diabetic and galactosaemic cataract. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Metabolism

  • Glucose via glycolysis (about 80%; anaerobic), hexose monophosphate shunt (NADPH for glutathione reduction), sorbitol pathway (aldose reductase), citric acid cycle (epithelium only).

Proteins

  • Water-soluble crystallins (α: chaperone; β and γ: structural) and insoluble proteins; with age, aggregation and insolubilisation increase.

Pathogenesis of senile cataract

  • Oxidative stress (UV, reduced glutathione), protein cross-linking and aggregation, yellow-brown pigment (nuclear), calcium increase and pump failure (cortical), posterior migration of epithelial cells (posterior subcapsular).

Diabetic cataract

  • Aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol, which accumulates (osmotic swelling, “snowflake” cataract); non-enzymatic glycation of proteins.

Galactosaemic cataract

  • Galactose → galactitol (dulcitol) via aldose reductase; “oil droplet” cataract in infants; reversible early with a galactose-free diet.
LC 4. Ageing changes in the lens; nuclear grading of cataract. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ageing changes in the lens

  • Continuous growth (new fibres added; lens thickens and weight increases), nucleus compaction and hardening (loss of accommodation: presbyopia), yellowing (absorbs blue light), reduced antioxidants (glutathione), protein aggregation and crosslinking (light scatter), anterior movement of the lens (shallower AC: angle closure), change of refractive index (index myopia or hyperopic shift).

Nuclear grading

  • Clinical: grades 1–5 by colour (Emery–Little: soft to black: grade I white or greenish-yellow, II yellow, III amber, IV brown, V black).
  • LOCS III: nuclear opalescence (NO) and colour (NC) graded 0.1–6.9, cortical (C) and posterior subcapsular (P) 0.1–5.9, by comparison with standard photographs.
  • Others: Oxford, Wilmer, WHO simplified systems; objective Scheimpflug densitometry (Pentacam nucleus staging), OCT.

Relevance

  • Choice of technique (phaco energy, MSICS for hard cataracts), counselling about risk.
LC 5. Cataract associated with systemic conditions; metabolic cataract. DNB / UNIV · outline

Cataract with systemic conditions

  • Diabetes: snowflake cataract (young uncontrolled diabetics: osmotic), early senile cataract; sorbitol accumulation by aldose reductase.
  • Galactosaemia: oil-droplet cataract (galactitol); reversible with galactose-free diet.
  • Hypocalcaemia (hypoparathyroidism): punctate iridescent cortical opacities.
  • Wilson disease: sunflower cataract (copper).
  • Myotonic dystrophy: multicoloured Christmas-tree cataract, then posterior subcapsular.
  • Atopic dermatitis: anterior subcapsular shield cataract.
  • Neurofibromatosis 2: posterior subcapsular.
  • Lowe syndrome, Fabry disease (spoke-like), Down syndrome, Alport (anterior lenticonus), homocystinuria (dislocation), rubella.
  • Drugs: steroids (posterior subcapsular), phenothiazines, amiodarone, miotics.

Metabolic cataract

  • Diabetic, galactosaemic, hypocalcaemic, Wilson, Fabry, mannosidosis, Refsum, cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis.

Management

  • Treat the metabolic disorder; cataract surgery when visually significant; screen children with bilateral cataract metabolically (galactose, calcium, urine amino acids, TORCH).
LC 6. Ciliary sulcus — anatomy and surgical relevance. · outline

Ciliary sulcus

  • Space between the posterior surface of the iris and the anterior surface of the ciliary body (pars plicata); bounded by the zonules behind.
  • Diameter about 11–12 mm (larger than the capsular bag: about 10.5 mm); variable (measure with UBM or AS-OCT).

Surgical relevance

  • Sulcus IOL placement when the posterior capsule is torn with intact anterior rim: use a three-piece IOL (not single-piece acrylic: UGH syndrome, pigment dispersion), reduce power by 0.5–1.0 D, optic capture through the anterior capsulorhexis.
  • Sulcus-fixated phakic IOLs (ICL), sulcus sutured IOLs (ab externo 0.8–1 mm behind the limbus), add-on (supplementary) IOLs, sulcus rings and segments.
  • Complications of sulcus placement: decentration, iris chafing, uveitis-glaucoma-hyphaema syndrome, pigment dispersion.

Paediatric Cataract & Aphakia

LC 7. Developmental and congenital cataract — aetiological and morphological classification (zonular, flake and others); critically evaluate its management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aetiological classification

  • Idiopathic; hereditary (autosomal dominant commonest); intrauterine infection (rubella, CMV, toxoplasma, varicella); metabolic (galactosaemia, hypocalcaemia, Lowe syndrome, Fabry); chromosomal (Down, Edwards); ocular associations (PFV, aniridia, microphthalmos); trauma, drugs (steroids).

Morphological classification

  • Lamellar (zonular, with riders): commonest visually significant; nuclear; polar (anterior and posterior); sutural; coronary; blue dot (punctate); flake and coralliform; membranous; total.

Critical evaluation of management

  • Visually significant (dense central over 3 mm, poor red reflex, nystagmus, strabismus) needs early surgery: unilateral by 6 weeks, bilateral by 8–10 weeks.
  • Surgery: lensectomy with primary posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy; IOL from about 1–2 years (IATS); aphakic contact lenses in infants.
  • Amblyopia therapy and refraction lifelong; complications: glaucoma (10–25%), visual axis opacification, strabismus.
LC 8. Management of unilateral congenital cataract in a 2-3 year old — surgery, IOL and postoperative visual rehabilitation; bilateral cataract in a 2-year-old. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Unilateral cataract in a 2–3-year-old

  • Assess visual significance, amblyopia, nystagmus, associated anomalies; biometry under anaesthesia.
  • Surgery: lens aspiration with primary posterior capsulorhexis and anterior vitrectomy (or optic capture), in-the-bag foldable hydrophobic acrylic IOL.
  • IOL power: aim for hypermetropia (about +2 to +3 D at age 2–3) to allow for myopic shift.
  • Postoperative: frequent topical steroids, glasses or bifocals for residual refraction, occlusion therapy (part-time patching), follow-up for glaucoma and visual axis opacification.

Bilateral cataract in a 2-year-old

  • Operate both eyes within a short interval (or immediate sequential surgery in selected cases), IOL implantation, glasses with near add, regular amblyopia review.

LC 9. Differences between the paediatric and adult eye; precautions in paediatric cataract surgery and the relationship with glaucoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Differences between paediatric and adult eyes

  • Smaller eye with shorter axial length and steeper cornea (rapid growth: myopic shift), low scleral rigidity (AC collapse), elastic anterior capsule (rhexis runs out), soft lens (aspiration only), more inflammation (fibrinous uveitis, synechiae), rapid posterior capsule opacification (almost universal), amblyopia risk.

Precautions in paediatric cataract surgery

  • Timing: dense unilateral by 4–6 weeks, bilateral by 6–8 weeks (sensitive period).
  • Technique: high-viscosity OVD, manual or vitrectorhexis anterior capsulotomy (two-incision push-pull), lens aspiration, primary posterior capsulotomy with anterior vitrectomy in children under about 6 years, IOL in the bag (hydrophobic acrylic), sutured incisions, intensive steroids, intracameral antibiotics.
  • IOL power: under-correct for myopic shift (IATS, Enyedi or Dahan nomograms).
  • Post-operative: refractive correction, amblyopia treatment, close review.

Relationship with glaucoma

  • Glaucoma following cataract surgery: 10–25% (higher with surgery in the first month of life, microcornea, PFV, aphakia); open-angle mostly; lifelong IOP monitoring.
LC 10. Congenital rubella syndrome. · outline

Congenital rubella syndrome

  • Maternal rubella infection in the first trimester (highest risk under 12 weeks).

Ocular features

  • Cataract (pearly nuclear, often bilateral; live virus in the lens for years), microphthalmos, salt-and-pepper pigmentary retinopathy (commonest; good vision), congenital glaucoma (rarely with cataract), iris hypoplasia, corneal clouding, strabismus, nystagmus.

Systemic features

  • Sensorineural deafness (commonest), congenital heart disease (PDA, pulmonary stenosis), microcephaly, intellectual disability, growth retardation, hepatosplenomegaly, thrombocytopenic purpura.

Management

  • Cataract surgery with thorough lens removal (inflammation from live virus), treat glaucoma, amblyopia, hearing and cardiac care.

Prevention

  • MMR or MR vaccination (India’s measles-rubella campaign), screening of women of child-bearing age.
LC 11. Posterior capsulorhexis. · outline

Posterior capsulorhexis (primary posterior continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis, PCCC)

  • Controlled circular opening of the posterior capsule after lens removal.

Indications

  • Paediatric cataract (prevents visual axis opacification, with anterior vitrectomy in young children), dense posterior capsular plaque, posterior polar cataract with capsular defect (convert to PCCC), optic capture techniques.

Technique

  • Fill capsular bag with OVD to flatten posterior capsule, puncture with cystotome or needle, extend with forceps (Utrata) to about 4–5 mm; vitrectorhexis in children; posterior optic capture (optic pushed through PCCC) reduces need for vitrectomy.

Complications

  • Extension of the tear, vitreous prolapse, IOL decentration, CME, RD (higher in adults with vitreous disturbance).

LC 12. Management of aphakia in children; IOLs in children. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Aphakia in children

  • Contact lenses (silicone elastomer, RGP): best in infants (IATS: similar acuity to IOL with fewer reoperations).
  • Aphakic glasses: bilateral aphakia; heavy, aberrations, aniseikonia in unilateral cases.
  • Secondary IOL implantation later (in-the-bag, sulcus, or scleral fixated).

IOLs in children

  • Primary IOL from about 1–2 years; hydrophobic acrylic single-piece in the bag; optic capture through posterior capsulorhexis.
  • Power: under-correct to allow for axial growth (age-based targets, for example Enyedi’s rule of 7); paediatric formula issues (SRK II, Holladay).
  • Complications: visual axis opacification, glaucoma, inflammation and synechiae, IOL decentration; lifelong follow-up.
LC 13. Infant Aphakia Treatment Study and IOL power selection in children. · outline

Infant Aphakia Treatment Study (IATS)

  • 114 infants (1–6 months) with unilateral congenital cataract randomised to primary IOL vs aphakia with contact lens.
  • Results: no difference in visual acuity at 4.5 and 10.5 years; more adverse events and re-operations with IOLs (visual axis opacification, pupillary membranes); glaucoma and glaucoma suspect common in both.
  • Conclusion: contact lens aphakia is preferred under 7 months; IOLs reasonable after that.

IOL power selection in children

  • Myopic shift with growth: aim for hyperopia (under-correction) depending on age (e.g. +6 to +8 D at 6 months, +3 to +4 D at 2 years, +1 to +2 D at 5 years, emmetropia by 8–10 years); consider fellow eye refraction (anisometropia), family myopia, compliance.
  • Formulas: SRK/T, Holladay 1, Hoffer Q, Barrett; less accurate in short eyes; biometry under anaesthesia.
  • Piggyback IOLs as an option to remove later.

Ectopia Lentis

LC 14. Subluxation of the crystalline lens — causes, tabulated differential diagnosis, systemic associations and a flow chart for management. Detail your examination, investigations and management of a subluxated lens in a 6-year-old boy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Causes

  • Trauma (commonest), Marfan syndrome (upwards and outwards), homocystinuria (downwards and inwards), Weill–Marchesani (microspherophakia, anterior), sulphite oxidase deficiency, Ehlers–Danlos, pseudoexfoliation, high myopia, buphthalmos, hypermature cataract, simple ectopia lentis, ectopia lentis et pupillae.

Differential table (key points)

  • Marfan: autosomal dominant, FBN1, tall, aortic dilatation; zonules stretched but present.
  • Homocystinuria: autosomal recessive, intellectual disability, thrombosis (anaesthesia risk), osteoporosis; zonules broken.
  • Weill–Marchesani: short stature, brachydactyly, microspherophakia, pupil block glaucoma.

6-year-old boy with a subluxated lens: workup

  • Visual acuity, refraction (lenticular and aphakic), amblyopia, IOP and gonioscopy, slit lamp (degree, phacodonesis, vitreous in AC), fundus.
  • Systemic: echocardiography, urine homocysteine (cyanide-nitroprusside test), plasma amino acids, genetics, paediatrician review.

Management flow chart

  • Mild, phakic portion clear of the axis → refraction for phakic or aphakic part, amblyopia therapy, follow-up.
  • Significant subluxation, poor vision, glaucoma or lens in AC → lensectomy (pars plana or limbal) with vitrectomy, then aphakic contact lens, or IOL (capsular tension segment or ring, scleral-fixated, iris-claw).
LC 15. Homocystinuria — biochemistry, ocular and systemic features, genetics and management; Marfan and Weill-Marchesani syndromes. DNB / UNIV · outline

Homocystinuria

  • Autosomal recessive deficiency of cystathionine beta-synthase; accumulation of homocysteine and methionine.
  • Ocular: bilateral inferonasal lens subluxation (zonules disintegrate), pupillary block glaucoma, myopia, RD.
  • Systemic: marfanoid habitus, intellectual disability, osteoporosis, thromboembolism (anaesthetic risk), malar flush.
  • Diagnosis: plasma homocysteine and amino acids, urine cyanide-nitroprusside test, genetic testing.
  • Management: pyridoxine (B6-responsive types), low methionine diet, betaine, folate and B12; anaesthesia precautions (thrombosis); lens surgery when needed.

Marfan syndrome

  • Autosomal dominant, FBN1 (fibrillin-1); superotemporal lens subluxation (zonules stretched but present; accommodation preserved), axial myopia, flat cornea, RD, glaucoma; aortic root dilatation (cardiac evaluation), tall stature, arachnodactyly.

Weill–Marchesani syndrome

  • Short stature, brachydactyly, stiff joints; microspherophakia (small spherical lens), lenticular myopia, pupil block glaucoma (avoid miotics: inverse glaucoma); ADAMTS10, FBN1.

Management of ectopia lentis

  • Refraction (aphakic or phakic portion), amblyopia treatment, lens extraction with CTR, CTS or scleral-fixated IOL (or aphakia with contact lens), laser iridotomy for pupil block.
LC 16. Capsular tension ring — indications and types. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Capsular tension ring (CTR)

  • Open PMMA ring placed in the capsular bag to distribute force evenly and support weak zonules; maintains bag contour and reduces PCO and decentration.

Indications

  • Zonular weakness or dialysis up to about 4 clock hours (pseudoexfoliation, trauma, Marfan), high myopia, during surgery for loose bags; supports toric IOL stability.

Types

  • Standard (Morcher, sizes by white-to-white), Cionni modified ring (eyelets for scleral fixation, larger dialysis), capsular tension segments (Ahmed), Malyugin modification, Henderson ring (easier cortex removal).

Contraindications

  • Posterior capsule rupture, anterior capsule tear running to the equator.

Cataract Surgery: Techniques

LC 17. Critically evaluate newer concepts in phacoemulsification, including bimanual (microincision) phaco; comparison of phaco, SICS and MICS. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Newer concepts in phacoemulsification

  • Torsional and transversal ultrasound (less energy, less chatter), active fluidics, smaller incisions (2.2 mm and under), chopping techniques, femtosecond assistance, intraoperative aberrometry, digital toric alignment.

Bimanual microincision cataract surgery (MICS)

  • Separate irrigation and sleeveless aspiration–phaco probes through two 1–1.5 mm incisions; less astigmatism; needs IOLs that pass through micro-incisions; wound burns and fluidic instability possible.

Comparison

  • Phaco: about 2.2–2.8 mm, astigmatism-neutral, quick recovery; costly machine, learning curve, endothelial loss in hard cataracts.
  • SICS: 6–7 mm self-sealing scleral tunnel, no machine, suits hard and mature cataracts and high-volume camps; slightly more astigmatism.
  • MICS: smallest wound and least induced astigmatism, but more technical demands.
  • Studies show similar long-term visual outcomes between phaco and SICS in experienced hands.

Revise from: Phacoemulsification Step by Step: A Beginner's Guide

LC 18. Phaco fluidics and power — flow, vacuum, rise time, surge, duty cycle, pulse and burst modes, and ultrasonic power modulation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Fluidics

  • Flow (aspiration flow rate: speed of fluid and material removal, attraction to tip), vacuum (holding force at occlusion), rise time (speed to reach set vacuum: set by flow in peristaltic pumps), bottle height or infusion pressure (AC stability).
  • Pumps: peristaltic (flow-based, vacuum builds on occlusion), venturi (vacuum-based, faster rise), hybrid.
  • Surge: after occlusion break, stored vacuum causes rapid outflow and AC collapse; reduced by smaller-bore tubing, active fluidics (pressure-controlled), lower vacuum, higher infusion, occlusion mode settings.

Ultrasound power

  • Longitudinal (jackhammer), torsional (Ozil: side-to-side, better followability, less repulsion), transversal (elliptical).
  • Modulation: continuous, pulse mode (fixed on and off times), burst mode (fixed on time with decreasing off time), hyperpulse; duty cycle (percentage of on time) lowers heat and energy.
  • Cumulative dissipated energy (CDE) measures energy used; lower is better for the endothelium.

Settings by step

  • Sculpting: low vacuum and flow, moderate power; chopping and quadrant removal: high vacuum, moderate flow; cortex: higher vacuum, linear.

Revise from: Phaco Machine Settings for Beginners: Peristaltic vs Venturi, Vacuum, Flow, Power and Fixing Surge

LC 19. Chop techniques in phacoemulsification. · outline

Chop techniques

  • Use mechanical force (chopper) to split the nucleus rather than ultrasound sculpting, reducing phaco energy and zonular stress.

Horizontal chop (Nagahara)

  • Phaco tip embeds in the nucleus centre; chopper passed under the anterior capsule to the equator and pulled towards the tip; best for soft to moderate nuclei.

Vertical chop (quick chop)

  • Tip embeds deep in the nucleus; sharp chopper pressed down just in front of the tip and the two are separated; good for dense nuclei.

Variations

  • Stop and chop (Koch: trench, then chop each half), phaco pre-chop (Akahoshi: manual pre-chop before phaco), prechopper devices, femtosecond laser fragmentation, miLOOP (nucleus disassembly).

Advantages

  • Less energy and time, less endothelial damage, less zonular stress; learning curve; risk of anterior capsule tear with horizontal chop.

Revise from: Phaco Chop Techniques: Horizontal, Vertical, Stop-and-Chop

LC 20. Manual small-incision cataract surgery — technique and advantages. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Technique

  • Peribulbar or topical anaesthesia; superior or temporal fornix-based flap; scleral tunnel (frown or straight, about 6–7 mm, 1.5–2 mm behind the limbus) into clear cornea (about 1 mm); side port.
  • Capsulorhexis (large) or can-opener; hydroprocedures; prolapse the nucleus into the AC; delivery by irrigating vectis, viscoexpression, fish-hook or sandwich technique.
  • Cortical aspiration (Simcoe), PMMA or foldable IOL in the bag; self-sealing wound.

Advantages

  • Low cost, no machine dependence, suits hard and mature cataracts, short learning curve, sutureless, high-volume suitable (Aravind model); outcomes close to phaco.

Disadvantages

  • Larger wound, more induced astigmatism, hyphaema, tunnel complications (premature entry, buttonholes).

Revise from: Manual Small Incision Cataract Surgery (MSICS): Steps and Pitfalls

LC 21. Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC)

  • Introduced by Gimbel and Neuhann; continuous tear of the anterior capsule giving a strong edge resistant to radial tears.

Technique

  • Fill AC with cohesive OVD (flattens the capsule), initiate with a cystotome or forceps, tear using shearing and ripping forces, regrasp often near the tearing edge, aim for 5–5.5 mm centred rhexis overlapping the IOL optic by 360°.

Difficult situations

  • Poor red reflex (white cataract: trypan blue, decompress intumescent lens to avoid Argentinian flag sign), children (elastic capsule: vitrectorhexis or two-incision technique), small pupils, weak zonules, high vitreous pressure.

Rescue of a running rhexis

  • Refill OVD, reduce pressure, Little’s manoeuvre (unfold and pull back towards the centre), start in the opposite direction, scissors cut; avoid hydrodissection forcefully.

Alternatives

  • Femtosecond laser capsulotomy, Zepto device (precision pulse capsulotomy).
LC 22. Methods of anaesthesia in cataract surgery — topical, peribulbar, retrobulbar and facial blocks: merits and complications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Topical (with or without intracameral lidocaine)

  • Merits: no injection risk, immediate vision, suitable for anticoagulated patients. Demerits: no akinesia, needs co-operation.

Peribulbar

  • 5–8 mL of lidocaine 2% and bupivacaine 0.5% with hyaluronidase; good akinesia; risks: chemosis, globe perforation (lower than retrobulbar), haematoma.

Retrobulbar

  • 3–4 mL into the muscle cone; rapid akinesia; risks: retrobulbar haemorrhage, globe perforation (long axial length), optic nerve injury, brainstem anaesthesia (apnoea), oculocardiac reflex.

Sub-Tenon

  • Blunt cannula; good anaesthesia, fewer serious complications; chemosis, subconjunctival haemorrhage.

Facial nerve blocks

  • Van Lint, O’Brien, Atkinson, Nadbath: lid akinesia; now rarely needed; risk of dysphagia and vocal cord paralysis (Nadbath).

General anaesthesia

  • Children, unco-operative or claustrophobic patients, open globe.

Revise from: Ocular Anaesthesia: Blocks, Complications and Exam Essentials

LC 23. Phacoemulsification in a small pupil — difficulties and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Difficulties

  • Poor visibility, small capsulorhexis, iris trauma and prolapse, floppy iris (tamsulosin), zonular stress, posterior capsule rupture, post-op inflammation and IOP rise.

Causes

  • Pseudoexfoliation, diabetes, uveitis (synechiae), long-term miotics, IFIS (alpha-blockers), previous surgery or trauma.

Management

  • Pre-op: intensive mydriatics, NSAIDs; stop pilocarpine; know the alpha-blocker history.
  • Intra-op: intracameral phenylephrine or Mydrane, dispersive viscoelastic (viscomydriasis), synechiolysis, pupil stretching, sphincterotomies, iris hooks, Malyugin ring; low-flow fluidics; chop techniques.
  • Post-op: steroids and NSAIDs, IOP control.

LC 24. Posterior polar cataract and its management. · outline

Posterior polar cataract

  • Dense, round, white disc-like opacity at the centre of the posterior capsule; often autosomal dominant; bilateral in many; weak or absent posterior capsule under the opacity (risk of PCR 7–36%).

Classification (Schroeder grades; Singh classification)

  • Type I: with posterior subcapsular cataract; II: with sharply defined ring; III: with white spots at the edge (dehiscence); IV: combined with nuclear cataract.

Management principles

  • Counsel on higher risk; pre-op OCT or AS-OCT to assess capsule integrity.
  • Avoid hydrodissection (inside-out hydrodelineation only), small rhexis, low fluidics and bottle height, gentle rotation (no rotation in some techniques), inside-out delineation and lambda technique, layer-by-layer removal, viscodissection of the epinucleus, leave the plaque until last; be ready for anterior vitrectomy; place IOL in the sulcus with optic capture if PCR; consider sutured or later IOL.
LC 25. Principles of cataract surgery in different ocular situations — complicated, post-uveitic, traumatic, diabetic and black cataract; pseudoexfoliation and low endothelial count (soft-shell technique). HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principles in different situations

  • Complicated and post-uveitic: quiet eye for at least 3 months, pre-operative steroids, synechiolysis, careful IOL choice (hydrophobic acrylic in the bag), intensive post-op steroids.
  • Traumatic: assess zonules, posterior capsule, vitreous, IOFB; CTR, anterior vitrectomy, secondary IOL if needed.
  • Diabetic: control retinopathy and maculopathy first (anti-VEGF, PRP), NSAIDs to reduce CME, large capsulorhexis, avoid silicone IOLs.
  • Black (brunescent) cataract: dispersive viscoelastic, trypan blue, chop techniques, higher power with care, or SICS.
  • Pseudoexfoliation: small pupil, weak zonules: CTR, gentle manoeuvres.

Low endothelial count

  • Soft-shell technique (Arshinoff): dispersive viscoelastic against the endothelium, cohesive below; low phaco energy, endocapsular chopping; may need combined endothelial keratoplasty.

LC 26. Critically evaluate a case of complicated cataract and its management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Complicated cataract

  • Cataract secondary to intraocular disease: chronic uveitis, glaucoma, RP, high myopia, RD, intraocular tumours; posterior subcapsular “polychromatic lustre” (breadcrumb appearance).

Evaluation

  • Activity of the primary disease, visual potential (projection of rays, Maddox rod, B-scan, ERG, VEP), IOP, synechiae, macula (OCT).

Management

  • Control the primary disease (steroids and immunosuppression in uveitis), optimise IOP, then cataract surgery with peri-operative steroids, synechiolysis and pupil management, hydrophobic acrylic in-the-bag IOL (avoid in some like JIA infants), combined procedures as needed; guarded prognosis.
LC 27. Viscoelastics — definition, classification, properties and uses. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Definition

  • Viscoelastic substances (OVDs): materials with both viscosity and elasticity, used to maintain space and protect tissues during surgery.

Classification (Arshinoff)

  • Cohesive (high viscosity, sodium hyaluronate 1–2.3%: Healon, Healon GV): maintain space, easy to remove.
  • Dispersive (low viscosity, HPMC 2%, chondroitin–hyaluronate: Viscoat): coat and protect endothelium, harder to remove.
  • Viscoadaptive (Healon 5), combinations (DuoVisc).

Properties

  • Viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity, cohesion, coating ability, molecular weight.

Uses

  • AC maintenance, capsulorhexis, endothelial protection (soft-shell), IOL insertion, viscodissection, viscomydriasis, glaucoma surgery (viscocanalostomy), tamponade in keratoplasty.

Complications

  • Post-operative IOP spike if retained, capsular bag distension syndrome.
LC 28. Surgically induced astigmatism and management of pre-existing astigmatism. DNB / UNIV · outline

Surgically induced astigmatism (SIA)

  • Change in corneal astigmatism caused by the incision; depends on site, size, architecture and distance from the limbus; temporal clear corneal 2.2–2.8 mm incisions induce about 0.3–0.5 D; superior incisions more against-the-rule; larger incisions (ECCE, SICS) induce more.
  • Calculated by vector analysis (Alpins method); online calculators.

Management of pre-existing astigmatism

  • Incision on the steep axis (on-axis incision), opposite clear corneal incisions, limbal relaxing incisions (for up to about 1.5 D), femtosecond arcuate keratotomy, toric IOLs (most predictable for over 1 D: account for posterior corneal astigmatism with Barrett toric calculator), post-operative LASIK or PRK (bioptics).
  • Toric IOL alignment: pre-operative reference marking or image-guided systems (Verion, Callisto), intraoperative aberrometry (ORA).
LC 29. Secondary IOL implantation. DNB / UNIV · outline

Secondary IOL implantation

  • IOL placed in an aphakic eye after previous cataract surgery (with or without capsular support).

Choice depends on capsule support

  • Good capsular bag or anterior rim: in-the-bag (reopened bag) or sulcus IOL (three-piece) with optic capture.
  • No capsular support: anterior chamber IOL (open-loop: Kelman multiflex; check angle and endothelium), iris-claw IOL (anterior or retropupillary: Artisan), iris-sutured IOL, scleral-fixated IOL (sutured with Gore-Tex or 9-0 polypropylene, glued IOL: Agarwal, Yamane flanged intrascleral technique, Carlevale lens).

Work-up

  • Endothelial count, gonioscopy, IOP, retina (CME, RD), biometry, keratometry.

Complications

  • Decentration and tilt, iris chafing and UGH syndrome, CME, RD, suture erosion or breakage, endothelial loss, raised IOP.
LC 30. Femtosecond laser-assisted (blade-free) cataract surgery — advantages and disadvantages. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Steps automated

  • Corneal incisions, capsulotomy (perfectly round, centred), lens fragmentation, arcuate incisions.

Advantages

  • Precise, reproducible capsulotomy; reduced phaco energy in dense cataracts; useful in weak zonules, white cataracts (with care), arcuate incisions for astigmatism.

Disadvantages

  • High cost, longer time, need to move the patient, small pupil and miosis after laser, subconjunctival haemorrhage, capsular block, incomplete capsulotomy; trials (FEMCAT, FACT) show no clear visual or safety advantage over standard phaco.
LC 31. Evolution of cataract surgery — ICCE and ECCE: steps and complications. DNB / UNIV · outline

Evolution of cataract surgery

  • Couching (ancient: Sushruta), extracapsular extraction (Daviel, 1747), intracapsular extraction (cryoextraction, alpha-chymotrypsin zonulysis), first IOL (Ridley, 1949), modern ECCE with PC IOL (1970s–80s), phacoemulsification (Kelman, 1967), foldable IOLs, small incision cataract surgery (MSICS), microincision phaco, femtosecond laser-assisted surgery.

ICCE steps

  • Large incision (about 160–180°), peripheral iridectomy, lens delivered with cryoprobe or forceps after zonulolysis, AC IOL or aphakic glasses, sutures.
  • Complications: vitreous loss, aphakic glasses problems, CME, RD, wound leak, high astigmatism, pupillary block.

ECCE steps

  • Large limbal incision (10–12 mm), can-opener capsulotomy, nucleus expression, cortex aspiration (Simcoe cannula), PC IOL in the bag, sutures.
  • Complications: posterior capsule rupture, PCO, astigmatism, iris prolapse, wound leak, slower recovery.

LC 32. Cataract surgery after vitrectomy with silicone oil. DNB / UNIV · outline

Cataract surgery after vitrectomy with silicone oil

  • Common: cataract develops in most phakic eyes after silicone oil.

Challenges

  • Deep and fluctuating AC (lack of vitreous support: lens-iris diaphragm retropulsion syndrome), loose zonules, posterior capsular plaques, miosis, oil in the AC, biometry errors (oil changes sound velocity: use optical biometry or correct ultrasound; choose IOL power for later oil removal or for the oil-filled eye), oil adherence to silicone IOLs (avoid silicone IOLs).

Techniques

  • Lower bottle height, lift the iris to break reverse pupillary block, careful hydrodissection, CTR if needed, combined silicone oil removal through the pars plana or through a posterior capsulorhexis (triple procedure), acrylic IOL in the bag.

Post-operative

  • Raised IOP, inflammation, oil emulsification; monitor retina for re-detachment.
LC 33. Intraoperative zonular dialysis — diagnosis and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Intraoperative zonular dialysis

  • Signs: capsular wrinkling (striae) during rhexis, movement of the nucleus (phacodonesis), vitreous prolapse at the equator, lens decentration, deepening or shallowing AC.

Causes

  • Pseudoexfoliation, trauma, high myopia, Marfan, previous vitrectomy, surgical trauma (aggressive manoeuvres).

Management

  • Stop and assess extent (clock hours), tamponade vitreous with dispersive OVD, avoid stress on the bag (low flow and bottle height, chop technique, viscodissection), capsular hooks or iris hooks to support the bag, capsular tension ring (insert after cortex removal or using hook-assisted technique; avoid if rhexis is not intact), Cionni ring or capsular tension segments (sutured) for larger dialysis, anterior vitrectomy if vitreous presents, IOL in the bag with CTR or in the sulcus with optic capture, scleral-fixated IOL if unstable.
LC 55. Immediate sequential bilateral cataract surgery — merits, safeguards and evidence. NEW · outline

Immediate sequential bilateral cataract surgery (ISBCS)

  • Both eyes operated in the same session as two completely separate procedures.

Merits

  • Fewer hospital visits, faster visual rehabilitation, no period of anisometropia, lower cost, single anaesthesia (useful for general anaesthesia and children), efficient in high-volume settings; cost-effectiveness shown in studies (UK, Finland, Sweden).

Concerns

  • Bilateral endophthalmitis (very rare), bilateral TASS, bilateral refractive surprise (no learning from the first eye), bilateral CME or complications.

Safeguards (iSBCS general principles)

  • Complete separate preparation, draping, instruments and consumables (different lots of OVD, fluids and drugs); intracameral antibiotics; surgeon changes gloves and gown.
  • Select low-risk patients (accurate biometry, average eyes, no high risk of complications); avoid in post-refractive eyes, unusual axial lengths, uveitis, high risk of PCR; stop the second eye if complications occur in the first.

Evidence

  • Large series and Cochrane review show similar visual outcomes and safety to delayed sequential surgery; endorsed by RCOphth guidance with safeguards.

LC 56. Cataract surgery in high myopia and in short (nanophthalmic) eyes. NEW · outline

High myopia

  • Challenges: long axial length (IOL power errors: use Barrett, Kane, or Wang–Koch-adjusted formulas), deep and fluctuating AC (lens-iris diaphragm retropulsion: lower bottle, lift iris), weak zonules, posterior staphyloma (ultrasound biometry errors), thin sclera (peribulbar injection risk: prefer topical or sub-Tenon), risk of RD after surgery (counsel, peripheral retinal examination), choice of target (mild myopia often desired), low-power or minus IOLs.

Short eyes (nanophthalmos, high hyperopia)

  • Challenges: crowded anterior segment and shallow AC, angle closure, thick sclera, uveal effusion and suprachoroidal haemorrhage, aqueous misdirection, high IOL powers (over +30 D; piggyback IOLs), formula accuracy (Haigis, Hoffer Q, Kane, Barrett).
  • Precautions: pre-operative IOP control and mannitol, low-flow surgery, prophylactic sclerotomies or pars plana vitreous tap in nanophthalmos, careful wound closure, post-operative cycloplegia; laser iridotomy beforehand when angle is closed.

Counselling

  • Refractive outcome less predictable; higher complication rates in both extremes.

Formulae

LC 35. Critically discuss the pros and cons of accommodative and newer IOLs (materials and designs, foldable IOLs) in ophthalmic practice. HIGH-YIELD · outline

IOL materials

  • PMMA (rigid, needs larger incision), silicone (foldable; avoid with silicone oil), hydrophilic acrylic (biocompatible, risk of calcification), hydrophobic acrylic (low PCO, glistenings).

Designs

  • Square-edged optics reduce PCO; aspheric optics improve contrast; blue-light filtering; single-piece vs three-piece (sulcus).

Accommodative and newer IOLs

  • Accommodating IOLs (Crystalens): limited, inconsistent near benefit.
  • Multifocal and trifocal (near independence; haloes), EDOF (intermediate; fewer haloes), toric, light-adjustable IOL (post-operative power adjustment with UV), add-on sulcus IOLs.

Pros and cons

  • Spectacle independence vs cost, photic phenomena, reduced contrast, the need for careful patient selection and exact biometry.
LC 36. Multifocal IOLs — refractive vs diffractive principles, types, advantages, disadvantages and surgical considerations; IOLs to aid near vision. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Principles

  • Refractive: concentric zones of different power (zonal); pupil dependent.
  • Diffractive: diffractive steps split light into distance and near foci (apodised in some); less pupil dependent; trifocal designs add intermediate.

Types

  • Bifocal (refractive or diffractive), trifocal (PanOptix, FineVision), EDOF (Symfony, Vivity), segmental (Lentis Mplus).

Advantages

  • Spectacle independence for most tasks.

Disadvantages

  • Haloes and glare, reduced contrast sensitivity, light loss, neuroadaptation, cost, sensitivity to decentration and residual astigmatism.

Surgical considerations

  • Careful selection (healthy macula and optic nerve, no significant dry eye or corneal irregularity, angle kappa), correct astigmatism over 0.75 D, exact biometry, centred capsulorhexis, avoid in glaucoma and AMD.

Other options for near

  • Monovision, mini-monovision with monofocal or EDOF IOLs.

LC 37. Aspheric IOLs. · outline

Aspheric IOLs

  • The human cornea has positive spherical aberration (about +0.27 µm); traditional spherical IOLs add more positive spherical aberration; aspheric IOLs have a modified profile.

Types

  • Negative spherical aberration IOLs (Tecnis: −0.27 µm; AcrySof IQ: −0.20 µm): compensate corneal SA, improve contrast especially with large pupils and in dim light.
  • Aberration-neutral IOLs (Akreos AO, SofPort AO): no added SA; more forgiving of decentration and tilt.

Clinical considerations

  • Benefit greatest in large pupils and night driving; decentration over 0.5 mm reduces benefit; post-myopic LASIK eyes have high positive SA (negative SA IOL helps), post-hyperopic LASIK eyes have negative SA (neutral IOL preferred); topography-guided selection.
LC 38. Toric and toric multifocal IOLs — indications and complications. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Toric IOL

  • Corrects regular corneal astigmatism, usually 0.75–1 D or more; considered with posterior corneal astigmatism (Barrett toric, Abulafia–Koch).

Indications

  • Regular corneal astigmatism with cataract; stable keratoconus with regular central astigmatism (with caution); after keratoplasty in selected cases.

Technique

  • Pre-operative limbal marking (sitting) or digital image guidance, in-the-bag placement, complete viscoelastic removal, rotate to axis.

Complications

  • Rotation (each 1° off axis loses about 3.3% of correction; 30° loses all), residual astigmatism, need for repositioning (best at 1–3 weeks).

Toric multifocal IOL

  • Combines astigmatism correction and near vision; demands exact centration and axis.

LC 39. Scleral-fixated IOLs. · outline

Scleral-fixated IOLs

  • IOL fixed to the sclera in eyes without capsular support.

Techniques

  • Sutured: 10-0 or 9-0 polypropylene (long-term breakage) or Gore-Tex (CV-8) through eyelets (Akreos AO60, CZ70BD); ab externo or ab interno, 1.5–2 mm behind the limbus with scleral flaps or pockets to bury knots.
  • Sutureless: glued IOL (Agarwal: haptics in scleral tunnels under flaps with fibrin glue), Yamane flanged intrascleral haptic fixation (30-gauge needles, cautery flanges), Carlevale lens (T-shaped plugs).

Indications

  • Aphakia without capsular support, dislocated IOL with no capsule, ectopia lentis, after trauma; when AC IOL is contraindicated (shallow AC, poor endothelium, glaucoma).

Complications

  • Suture erosion, breakage and late dislocation, IOL tilt and decentration, haptic exposure, vitreous haemorrhage, CME, RD, endophthalmitis, hypotony.
LC 40. Decentred IOL — causes and management. · outline

Causes of a decentred IOL

  • Early: asymmetric placement (one haptic in the bag and one in the sulcus), zonular dialysis or PCR, oversized or undersized IOL, small pupil, eccentric rhexis.
  • Late: capsular contraction (pseudoexfoliation, uveitis, RP: capsular phimosis), zonular weakness, sunset or sunrise syndrome, trauma, after YAG capsulotomy, out-of-the-bag dislocation.

Symptoms

  • Glare, haloes, edge effects, monocular diplopia, refractive shift (especially with toric and multifocal IOLs).

Management

  • Mild: observe, miotics; YAG relaxing incisions for capsular phimosis.
  • Repositioning (rotate into the bag or sulcus), optic capture, iris or scleral suture fixation, capsular tension segments, IOL exchange (scleral-fixated or iris-claw), anterior vitrectomy if needed.
LC 41. Extended depth-of-focus IOLs, light-adjustable lenses and newer formulae (Barrett, Kane, Hill-RBF). · outline

Extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) IOLs

  • Create an elongated focus (diffractive echelette, small aperture, or non-diffractive wavefront-shaping designs) giving good distance and intermediate vision with fewer haloes than multifocals (e.g. Tecnis Symfony, AcrySof Vivity); near vision often needs readers or mini-monovision.

Light-adjustable lens (LAL)

  • Silicone IOL with photosensitive macromers; after surgery, UV light treatments (from 2–3 weeks) adjust sphere and astigmatism and then lock in; needs UV-protective glasses until lock-in; good for post-refractive surgery eyes.

Newer formulae

  • Barrett Universal II (thick-lens, theoretical; widely used), Kane formula (theoretical, regression and AI components; very accurate across axial lengths), Hill-RBF (radial basis function: AI pattern recognition; flags out-of-bounds eyes), EVO, Olsen (ray tracing), Pearl-DGS; Barrett True-K and Haigis-L for post-refractive eyes; Barrett toric calculator for posterior corneal astigmatism.

LC 42. Posterior capsular opacification — intraoperative and postoperative methods of prevention; laser capsulotomy. HIGH-YIELD · outline

PCO

  • Proliferation and migration of residual lens epithelial cells: Elschnig pearls (regenerative) and fibrosis.

Intra-operative prevention

  • Thorough cortical cleaning and hydrodissection, capsulorhexis overlapping the optic, in-the-bag fixation, square-edge optic, hydrophobic acrylic material, anterior capsule polishing, primary posterior capsulotomy in children.

Post-operative prevention

  • Control inflammation; research into pharmacological agents.

  • Indications: visually significant PCO. Technique: contact lens (Abraham), cruciate or circular opening about 3–4 mm, minimum energy, focus slightly behind the capsule.
  • Complications: IOP spike, IOL pitting, CME, RD (especially high myopia), IOL dislocation, uveitis.

LC 43. Posterior capsular rupture during phaco — causes, prevention and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Causes and risk factors

  • Patient: posterior polar cataract, pseudoexfoliation, small pupil, hard or white cataract, high myopia, previous vitrectomy, deep AC, poor cooperation.
  • Surgeon: inexperience, incomplete rhexis or extension, vigorous hydrodissection, phaco tip or chopper injury, surge, aspiration of capsule during cortex removal.

Signs

  • Sudden deepening of the AC, momentary pupil dilatation, nucleus falling back or tilting, inability to rotate, vitreous at the tip.

Prevention

  • Good rhexis, gentle hydrodissection, low fluidics in high-risk eyes, keep instruments in view, appropriate OVD.

Management

  • Do not withdraw the phaco tip suddenly; inject dispersive OVD before withdrawing to tamponade vitreous; lower bottle; remove remaining nucleus (convert to MSICS or ECCE if large, or lens glide) and prevent dropped nucleus; triamcinolone-assisted anterior vitrectomy (bimanual, high cut rate, low flow, via pars plana or limbus); cortex removal with dry technique or vitrector; IOL in the bag if small PCR (convert to PCCC), sulcus with optic capture, or AC or scleral-fixated IOL; intracameral miotic, careful wound closure, steroids and NSAIDs (CME risk).
  • Dropped nucleus: refer for pars plana vitrectomy within 1–2 weeks.

LC 44. What are the differential diagnoses of postoperative inflammation after cataract surgery? Relate them to management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Differential diagnosis of postoperative inflammation

  • Normal inflammation, retained lens matter, TASS (toxic anterior segment syndrome: within 24 hours, sterile, diffuse corneal oedema), infective endophthalmitis (usually days 2–7, pain, hypopyon, vitritis), chronic low-grade endophthalmitis (P. acnes, white plaque), uveitis reactivation, phacoanaphylactic uveitis, UGH syndrome, steroid response, CME.

Relation to management

  • TASS: intensive topical steroids, close watch, investigate the cause (instruments, solutions).
  • Endophthalmitis: vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics (vancomycin 1 mg, ceftazidime 2.25 mg); vitrectomy if vision is light perception (EVS).
  • Retained lens matter: steroids and surgical removal if large.
  • Chronic endophthalmitis: capsulectomy, IOL removal or intravitreal vancomycin.
  • CME: topical NSAIDs and steroids.

LC 45. Critically evaluate the management of a dropped nucleus. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Dropped nucleus

  • Posterior capsule rupture with lens fragments falling into the vitreous; risks: inflammation, glaucoma, CME, RD.

Immediate management

  • Do not chase the fragment through the pupil; perform thorough anterior vitrectomy, remove accessible lens matter, implant IOL if support is adequate (sulcus or optic capture), close wounds, steroids.

Definitive

  • Pars plana vitrectomy with removal of fragments (fragmatome or phaco in the vitreous cavity, PFCL to float the nucleus), ideally within 1–2 weeks; peripheral retina examination and laser to breaks.

Outcomes

  • Good vision in most when managed promptly; complications: RD, CME, glaucoma, endophthalmitis.
LC 46. Causes and management of diminished vision after phacoemulsification (early and at six weeks), including in a diabetic. DNB / UNIV · outline

Early diminished vision after phaco (days)

  • Corneal oedema and striate keratopathy, raised IOP, inflammation, hyphaema, retained lens fragments, endophthalmitis (urgent), TASS, wrong IOL power (refractive surprise), IOL decentration, cystoid macular oedema (rarely early), RD, posterior capsular opacity (plaque), suprachoroidal haemorrhage.

At six weeks

  • Refractive error (residual astigmatism), PCO (early), CME (peak 4–12 weeks), ERM, RD, pre-existing macular or optic nerve disease (AMD, glaucoma), dry eye, capsular block syndrome.

In a diabetic

  • Worsening diabetic macular oedema, progression of retinopathy (PDR, vitreous haemorrhage), rubeosis and NVG, CME, endophthalmitis (higher risk), delayed epithelial healing.

Management

  • Full examination, OCT macula, B-scan, refraction; treat cause: topical NSAIDs and steroids for CME, anti-VEGF or steroid implants for DME, YAG capsulotomy, vitreous tap and antibiotics for endophthalmitis, IOL exchange or piggyback for refractive surprise, vitrectomy for retained fragments or RD.

LC 47. Capsular block syndrome — classification and management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Capsular block syndrome

  • Accumulation of fluid in the closed capsular bag behind the IOL because the anterior capsulorhexis edge seals against the IOL optic.

Classification (Miyake)

  • Intraoperative: during hydrodissection (excess fluid can rupture the posterior capsule).
  • Early post-operative: retained OVD in the bag; myopic shift, shallow AC (IOL pushed forward), raised IOP.
  • Late post-operative (liquefied after-cataract): milky white fluid (lacteocrumenasia) from lens epithelial cell products months to years later; reduced vision with little refractive shift.

Management

  • Early: Nd:YAG anterior capsulotomy (peripheral, to drain fluid into AC) or posterior capsulotomy; surgical aspiration of OVD.
  • Late: Nd:YAG posterior capsulotomy, surgical irrigation of the bag.
  • Prevention: complete OVD removal behind the IOL, appropriate rhexis size.
LC 48. Striate keratopathy and postoperative IOP rise after cataract surgery. DNB / UNIV · outline

Striate keratopathy after cataract surgery

  • Folds in Descemet membrane with stromal oedema; from endothelial trauma (phaco energy, hard nucleus, close work to endothelium), OVD, irrigation solutions, intraocular toxins, high or low IOP, Fuchs dystrophy.
  • Management: topical steroids, hypertonic saline, control IOP; usually clears in days to weeks; persistent oedema suggests endothelial decompensation (PBK) or Descemet detachment (AS-OCT: air or gas descemetopexy).

Post-operative IOP rise

  • Early (hours to days): retained OVD (commonest), inflammation, hyphaema, pupillary block, aqueous misdirection, steroid response (weeks), retained lens material, TASS, pre-existing glaucoma, PXF.
  • Management: aqueous suppressants (oral acetazolamide, topical beta-blockers, alpha-agonists), burp the paracentesis (release aqueous at the slit lamp), treat cause; prophylactic IOP lowering in glaucoma patients.
LC 49. Complications of anterior chamber IOLs. DNB / UNIV · outline

Complications of anterior chamber IOLs

  • Corneal: endothelial cell loss and pseudophakic bullous keratopathy (especially closed-loop designs).
  • UGH syndrome: uveitis, glaucoma and hyphaema from chafing of the iris and angle by haptics.
  • Angle structures: synechiae, cocooning of haptics, chronic inflammation, secondary glaucoma.
  • Pupil: ovalisation (wrong size: too long), pupillary block (need peripheral iridectomy).
  • CME, tenderness on touching the globe (oversized), decentration and rotation (undersized: windshield wiper syndrome).
  • Hyphaema, retinal detachment (less common).

Prevention and management

  • Use open-loop flexible designs (Kelman multiflex), correct sizing (white-to-white + 1 mm), peripheral iridectomy; remove or exchange for scleral-fixated or iris-claw IOL when complications arise.
LC 50. Corneal complications of IOLs. · outline

Corneal complications of IOLs

  • Endothelial damage: during insertion (IOL touch), chronic contact (AC IOL, iris-claw IOL with shallow AC, phakic IOLs), UGH syndrome; leads to corneal decompensation (PBK).
  • Descemet membrane detachment at the incision during IOL insertion.
  • IOL-induced astigmatism: large incisions for rigid IOLs.
  • Calcification and opacification of hydrophilic IOLs (after air or gas in the AC: DMEK, DSAEK) mistaken for corneal haze.
  • Silicone oil adherence to silicone IOLs (optical blur).
  • Corneal epithelial ingrowth and wound leak at the main incision.

Management

  • Endothelial keratoplasty, IOL exchange or repositioning, Descemet reattachment with air or gas.
LC 51. Pseudophakic cystoid macular oedema. · outline

Pseudophakic CME (Irvine–Gass syndrome)

  • Macular oedema after cataract surgery; angiographic in about 20%, clinically significant in about 1–2%; peak at 4–12 weeks.

Pathogenesis

  • Inflammatory mediators (prostaglandins) released by surgical trauma diffuse to the macula, breaking the blood–retinal barrier; vitreous traction (vitreous to wound).

Risk factors

  • Diabetes, uveitis, ERM, RVO, PCR and vitreous loss, iris trauma, prostaglandin analogue use (debated), retained lens fragments, AC IOL.

Diagnosis

  • Reduced vision after initial improvement, OCT (cystic spaces, central thickening), FFA (petaloid leakage, late disc staining).

Prevention

  • Topical NSAIDs pre- and post-operatively in high-risk eyes (ESCRS PREMED study: NSAID plus steroid; subconjunctival triamcinolone in diabetics), careful surgery.

Treatment

  • Topical NSAIDs and steroids; sub-Tenon or intravitreal triamcinolone; dexamethasone implant; oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors; vitrectomy for vitreous traction or chronic cases; anti-VEGF for diabetic component.
LC 52. Intralenticular foreign body. · outline

Intralenticular foreign body

  • Foreign body lodged within the lens after penetrating injury (metallic: iron or copper, glass, organic); small corneal entry wound and a self-sealing anterior capsule tear may keep the lens clear for a while.

Evaluation

  • History of hammering, slit lamp (track, capsule rupture, localised opacity), gonioscopy, CT orbit (no MRI if metal suspected), B-scan (posterior capsule integrity, other foreign bodies), ERG for retained iron (siderosis).

Management

  • Small, inert foreign body with clear lens and intact capsule: observation with ERG monitoring.
  • Cataract, capsule rupture, inflammation or metallic foreign body: lens extraction with foreign body removal (forceps, magnet), IOL implantation (primary or secondary), anterior vitrectomy if needed, prophylactic antibiotics (intracameral, systemic).

LC 53. Toxic anterior segment syndrome vs early endophthalmitis. · outline

Toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS)

  • Acute sterile inflammation caused by a toxic substance entering the AC during surgery (detergent residues, endotoxins, preservatives, wrong concentration of drugs, OVD residues, metal ions from instruments, wrong irrigation solutions).
  • Onset 12–48 hours; diffuse limbus-to-limbus corneal oedema, fibrin, hypopyon possible, little pain, no vitritis; IOP may rise; dilated non-reactive pupil (iris ischaemia).
  • Management: intensive topical steroids, IOP control; good response in most; investigate the cause (instrument cleaning, sterilisation protocols); endothelial damage may require keratoplasty.

Early post-operative endophthalmitis

  • Onset 2–7 days; pain, lid swelling, decreased vision, hypopyon, vitritis (B-scan), loss of red reflex, worsening despite steroids.
  • Management: vitreous tap and intravitreal antibiotics, vitrectomy (EVS criteria).

Key differences

  • TASS: earlier (within 24 hours), painless, corneal oedema prominent, anterior segment confined, responds to steroids; endophthalmitis: later, painful, vitreous involved, needs antibiotics.

Revise from: Endophthalmitis: Types, Organisms and Management

LC 54. Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome. · outline

Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS)

  • Triad: billowing flaccid iris stroma, iris prolapse to the incisions, progressive intraoperative miosis despite dilatation.

Causes

  • Alpha-1A antagonists, especially tamsulosin (persists even after stopping), also alfuzosin, doxazosin, finasteride (some), antipsychotics (risperidone), saw palmetto.

Prevention and management

  • Ask about these drugs pre-operatively.
  • Pre-operative atropine for 2–3 days (debated), intracameral phenylephrine with ketorolac (Omidria) or epinephrine (preservative-free, bisulfite-free), dispersive OVD (Healon5 or viscoadaptive), well-constructed longer incisions, low flow and bottle height, iris hooks or pupil expansion rings (Malyugin), avoid stopping drugs abruptly (little benefit).
LC 57. Refractive surprise after cataract surgery — causes, evaluation and management (IOL exchange, piggyback IOL, laser refractive surgery). NEW · outline

Refractive surprise

  • Unexpected post-operative refraction (usually more than 1 D from target).

Causes

  • Biometry errors (axial length, keratometry, wrong data entry), wrong formula for eye type (short, long, post-refractive), wrong IOL inserted (labelling, selection), IOL position (sulcus placement without power change, capsular bag distension, IOL tilt or flipped), corneal factors (unrecognised previous refractive surgery, dry eye affecting keratometry), silicone oil, capsular block syndrome.

Evaluation

  • Manifest refraction and topography, repeat biometry (optical), check IOL details and records, UBM or AS-OCT for IOL position, OCT for CME.

Management

  • Wait for refraction to stabilise (2–4 weeks), spectacles or contact lenses if acceptable.
  • IOL exchange (best within the first weeks before capsular fibrosis), piggyback sulcus IOL (later; three-piece or dedicated sulcus IOL), laser refractive surgery (LASIK or PRK) for small errors and astigmatism, light-adjustable lens in the future.
  • Disclose and document; root-cause analysis (WHO checklist).

Revise from: Refractive Surprise After Cataract Surgery: Causes and Management

LC 58. Positive and negative dysphotopsia. NEW · outline

Positive dysphotopsia

  • Bright artefacts: glare, haloes, streaks, starbursts; often with square-edged, high-index acrylic IOLs, multifocal designs, and large pupils.
  • Management: reassurance (most improve with neuroadaptation), miotics (brimonidine, pilocarpine), polishing the anterior capsule overlap rarely, IOL exchange to lower-index rounded-edge or larger optic IOL in persistent cases.

Negative dysphotopsia

  • Dark temporal crescent or shadow in the far temporal field; about 15% early, persistent in 2–3%; thought to be due to a gap between the light refracted by the IOL and light passing nasally to it, with the nasal anterior capsule edge overlapping the optic.
  • Management: reassurance (many resolve as capsule opacifies), reverse optic capture (optic in front of the anterior capsule), sulcus piggyback IOL, nasal Nd:YAG anterior capsulectomy, IOL exchange to sulcus placement; avoid YAG posterior capsulotomy before deciding on exchange.

Prevention

  • Counsel patients receiving multifocal IOLs; choose IOL design carefully in patients with large pupils.
