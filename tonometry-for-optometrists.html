Tonometry for Optometrists: Non-Contact, Goldmann, Rebound and Perkins, With Sources of Error

Last updated: November 2026

RPC Rounds for Optometrists · B.Optom study guide

Measuring intraocular pressure (IOP) is a screening task optometrists do every day. A good optometrist knows which tonometer to use, how to get a reliable reading, what can make it wrong, and that a "normal" pressure does not rule out glaucoma.

Normal IOP and why it matters

  • Population mean about 15–16 mmHg; the statistical "normal" upper limit is about 21 mmHg. Diurnal variation is usually 3–5 mmHg, often highest in the early morning.
  • Raised IOP is the main modifiable risk factor for glaucoma, but about a third to a half of glaucoma patients have IOP in the normal range at diagnosis (normal-tension glaucoma), so always look at the disc and fields too.

Types of tonometer

TonometerPrincipleStrengthsLimitations
Goldmann applanation (GAT)Force needed to flatten a 3.06 mm diameter area (Imbert-Fick law); fluorescein mires aligned at their inner edgesReference standard; accurateNeeds slit lamp, anaesthetic and fluorescein; affected by corneal thickness and rigidity

Rebound (iCare)Small probe bounces off the cornea; deceleration relates to IOPNo anaesthetic, portable; children and home monitoringReads slightly higher than GAT in some eyes; affected by CCT

Goldmann technique in brief

  1. Instil anaesthetic and fluorescein; disinfect the prism (or use a disposable tip).
  2. Set the dial at about 10 mmHg, cobalt blue light at full intensity, beam at about 60°.
  3. Align the prism (0° for most eyes; set the red mark to the minus-cylinder axis if corneal astigmatism is over 3 D) and touch the centre of the cornea.
  4. Turn the dial until the inner edges of the two semicircles just touch; the mires should pulse slightly. Read the dial × 10 = mmHg.
  5. Too much fluorescein makes thick mires and overestimates; too little underestimates.

Sources of error

FactorEffect
Thin cornea (or after LASIK/PRK)Reads falsely low
Thick corneaReads falsely high (but oedematous corneas often read low)
Corneal astigmatism over 3 DError unless the prism is rotated to the minus-cylinder axis
Squeezing, tight collar, breath holding, pressure on the lidsReads falsely high
Too thick or thin fluorescein miresHigh or low readings
Repeated measurementsIOP falls slightly (tonography effect)

There is no reliable formula to "correct" IOP for central corneal thickness; treat thin CCT as a risk factor. See CCT and IOP.

When to refer

  • IOP over about 21–24 mmHg on repeated readings, or a difference of more than about 5 mmHg between the eyes.
  • Any IOP with a suspicious disc (cup-disc asymmetry, notching, disc haemorrhage) or a field defect.
  • Same day: IOP over about 35–40 mmHg, or high IOP with pain, redness, haloes or a mid-dilated pupil (acute angle closure).

Practical pearls

  • With NCT, take three readings and average them; repeat with GAT if any reading is over 21 mmHg.
  • Before dilating, estimate the angle with van Herick; a narrow angle in a hyperope over 50 needs gonioscopy, not just a pressure check.
  • Write the time of the IOP measurement: diurnal change matters when comparing visits.

Where students slip

  • Assuming normal IOP means no glaucoma.
  • Leaving thick fluorescein mires and recording a falsely high IOP.
  • Pressing on the lids while holding them open.
