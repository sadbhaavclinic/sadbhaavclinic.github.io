Cornea and External Eye OSCE Stations: 10 Spot Stations on Keratitis, Topography, Dry Eye, Grafts and Chemical Injury

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 10 spot stations, 40 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 10 original stations cover the cornea topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. Corneal scraping, 2. Fungal keratitis, 3. Acanthamoeba keratitis, 4. Herpes simplex keratitis, 5. Keratoconus topography, 6. Corneal cross-linking, 7. Dry eye tests, 8. Graft rejection, 9. Types of keratoplasty, 10. Chemical injury

Station 1: Corneal scraping

The examiner shows you: A tray: a 15 blade or 26 G needle, glass slides, blood agar, chocolate agar, Sabouraud dextrose agar and non-nutrient agar.

  1. What is this tray for?
    Corneal scraping for microbial keratitis: smears and cultures before starting antimicrobials.
  2. Which stains and media are used for what?
    Gram stain (bacteria), KOH wet mount or calcofluor white (fungi and cysts), blood and chocolate agar (bacteria), Sabouraud dextrose agar (fungi), non-nutrient agar with an E. coli overlay (Acanthamoeba), and thioglycolate broth (anaerobes).
  3. Where do you scrape?
    The leading edge and base of the ulcer, after topical anaesthetic, avoiding deep scraping of a thin cornea.
  4. When is a corneal biopsy needed?
    When repeated scrapings are negative and the ulcer is worsening, or the infection is deep (for example fungal or Acanthamoeba deep in the stroma).

Station 2: Fungal keratitis

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph of a farmer injured by plant matter: a dry, grey-white infiltrate with feathery margins, satellite lesions and an endothelial plaque.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    Filamentous fungal keratitis (for example Fusarium or Aspergillus).
  2. Which features favour fungus?
    Vegetative trauma, feathery edges, satellite lesions, a raised dry surface, an endothelial plaque and a hypopyon despite a relatively quiet eye.
  3. What is the first-line treatment?
    Topical natamycin 5% hourly (MUTT I showed it was better than voriconazole for filamentous fungi, especially Fusarium).
  4. Should steroids be used?
    No: steroids worsen fungal keratitis. Oral voriconazole added no benefit in MUTT II.

Station 3: Acanthamoeba keratitis

The examiner shows you: A contact lens wearer who swims with lenses has severe pain out of proportion to the signs. There are radial perineural infiltrates and a partial ring infiltrate.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Acanthamoeba keratitis.
  2. Name the classic signs.
    Pain out of proportion, radial keratoneuritis (perineural infiltrates), pseudodendrites early and a ring infiltrate later.
  3. How is it confirmed?
    Corneal scraping or biopsy for culture on non-nutrient agar with E. coli overlay, PCR, and in vivo confocal microscopy showing cysts.
  4. What is the treatment?
    Biguanides (PHMB 0.02% or chlorhexidine 0.02%), with or without a diamidine (propamidine); the newer PHMB 0.08% monotherapy is an option. Treatment lasts months.

Station 4: Herpes simplex keratitis

The examiner shows you: Fluorescein staining shows a branching ulcer with terminal bulbs; corneal sensation is reduced.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Herpes simplex epithelial keratitis (dendritic ulcer).
  2. How does it differ from a pseudodendrite?
    A true dendrite is a true ulcer with terminal bulbs and stains with fluorescein at the base; its swollen edges stain with rose bengal. Pseudodendrites (zoster, Acanthamoeba, healing abrasions) are raised plaques without terminal bulbs.
  3. What is the treatment?
    Topical antiviral (ganciclovir gel or aciclovir ointment) or oral aciclovir; no steroids for active epithelial disease.
  4. What did HEDS show about prophylaxis?
    Long-term oral aciclovir 400 mg twice daily about halved recurrences of ocular HSV, most usefully after stromal keratitis.

Station 5: Keratoconus topography

The examiner shows you: A Scheimpflug map: inferior steepening (K max 52 D), thinnest pachymetry 450 µm displaced inferotemporally, and elevated posterior float.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Keratoconus.
  2. Name the clinical signs.
    Munson sign, Rizzuti sign, Vogt striae, Fleischer ring, scissoring reflex on retinoscopy, and acute hydrops in advanced disease.
  3. How do you document progression?
    Serial tomography: increase in K max, decreasing thinnest pachymetry, change in posterior elevation, or loss of corrected vision (and an increase in the Belin ABCD stage).
  4. How is it managed?
    Glasses, then rigid or scleral contact lenses for vision; corneal cross-linking to halt progression; keratoplasty (usually DALK) for advanced or scarred corneas.

Station 6: Corneal cross-linking

The examiner shows you: A treatment room set-up: riboflavin drops, a UV-A lamp at 365 nm and a pachymeter.

  1. What is the procedure and its aim?
    Corneal collagen cross-linking with riboflavin and UV-A to strengthen the cornea and halt progression of keratoconus or ectasia.
  2. What is the standard (Dresden) protocol?
    Epithelium removed, riboflavin 0.1% every few minutes for about 30 minutes, then UV-A 3 mW/cm² for 30 minutes (5.4 J/cm²).
  3. Why measure corneal thickness?
    The cornea should be at least 400 µm after epithelial removal to protect the endothelium; thinner corneas need hypo-osmolar riboflavin or modified protocols.
  4. Name complications.
    Pain, infection, sterile infiltrates, haze and endothelial damage.

Station 7: Dry eye tests

The examiner shows you: A tear meniscus photograph, Schirmer strips, fluorescein and lissamine green.

  1. How is the Schirmer test done and read?
    A strip in the lateral third of the lower lid for 5 minutes. Without anaesthetic (Schirmer I) 10 mm or less is abnormal; with anaesthetic (basal secretion) 5 mm or less is abnormal.
  2. What is tear break-up time?
    The time between a blink and the first dry spot in the fluorescein-stained tear film; under 10 seconds suggests evaporative instability.
  3. What does lissamine green show?
    Devitalised cells on the conjunctiva and cornea, especially in the interpalpebral zone.
  4. Name the two main types of dry eye.
    Aqueous deficient (for example Sjögren syndrome) and evaporative (mainly meibomian gland dysfunction); most patients have features of both.

Station 8: Graft rejection

The examiner shows you: Eight months after penetrating keratoplasty: a red eye, reduced vision, a line of keratic precipitates on the donor endothelium and stromal oedema.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Endothelial graft rejection, with a Khodadoust line.
  2. Name the types of graft rejection.
    Epithelial, subepithelial (stromal infiltrates), stromal, and endothelial (the most serious).
  3. How do you treat it?
    Intensive topical steroids (for example prednisolone 1% hourly), with systemic or sub-Tenon steroids in severe cases; check IOP.
  4. Which factors increase rejection risk?
    Corneal vascularisation, previous rejection or graft, large or eccentric grafts, inflammation, and young age.

Station 9: Types of keratoplasty

The examiner shows you: Three diagrams: full-thickness graft, a graft replacing the stroma down to Descemet membrane, and a thin posterior lamella.

  1. Name the three procedures.
    Penetrating keratoplasty (PK), deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) and endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK or DMEK).
  2. When is DALK preferred?
    Stromal disease with a healthy endothelium, such as keratoconus or stromal scars: no risk of endothelial rejection.
  3. What are the advantages of DMEK?
    The best visual recovery, the lowest rejection rate and a near-normal corneal shape; used for Fuchs dystrophy and pseudophakic bullous keratopathy.
  4. What was learnt from the Cornea Donor Study?
    Donor corneas up to age 75 can be used safely for endothelial disease.

Station 10: Chemical injury

The examiner shows you: A man splashed with lime (calcium hydroxide) at a building site: limbal ischaemia over 4 clock hours and a large epithelial defect.

  1. What is the first action?
    Immediate copious irrigation (at least 30 minutes or until the pH is neutral), removing particles with fornix sweeps under double eversion.
  2. Why are alkalis worse than acids?
    Alkalis saponify cell membranes and penetrate deeper; acids coagulate surface proteins, which limits penetration.
  3. Name the grading systems.
    Roper-Hall (corneal haze and limbal ischaemia) and Dua (clock hours of limbal involvement and percentage of conjunctival involvement).
  4. What is the medical treatment?
    Topical steroid in the first week, cycloplegic, preservative-free lubricants, topical and oral vitamin C, doxycycline, antibiotic cover, and amniotic membrane in severe cases.
