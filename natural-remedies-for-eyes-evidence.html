Natural Remedies for the Eyes: Pineapple for Floaters, Honey, Saffron, Bilberry and More: What the Evidence Says

For: PG residents · Practising ophthalmologists · Patients and families

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • Pineapple for floaters comes from one small study from Taiwan (2019) with no comparison group. It is not proven. Eating fresh pineapple as food is harmless for most people, but it is not a treatment.
  • Natural remedies with real evidence: time outdoors for children (fewer cases of myopia), the AREDS2 vitamin formula for intermediate AMD, and warm compresses with lid cleaning for blocked oil glands.
  • Often promoted, little or no proof: bilberry for night vision, omega-3 capsules for dry eye, ginkgo for glaucoma, carrots for better eyesight, eye exercises to remove glasses.
  • Never put kitchen products in the eye: ghee, breast milk, plant juices, honey from the kitchen, rose water or herbal washes can cause corneal ulcers and blindness.
  • New floaters with flashes, a shower of spots or a curtain over vision need an eye examination the same day, whatever home remedy you are trying.

Patients often ask, "Is there something natural I can take for my eyes?" Some natural measures really work, many are harmless but unproven, and a few are dangerous. This page sorts them by the strength of the evidence, in plain words for patients and with study details for residents and practitioners.

How to read the verdicts: Proven means good randomised trials show benefit. Some evidence means small or early trials suggest a benefit that is not yet confirmed. Unproven means no good evidence of benefit (it may still be harmless). Harmful means it can damage the eye.

Pineapple for floaters: what the study actually showed

Floaters are small dots, threads or cobwebs that drift across your vision. They are shadows of clumps in the vitreous, the clear jelly that fills the eye. They become common with age and in short-sighted (myopic) people, and most become less noticeable over months as the clumps settle and the brain learns to ignore them.

The idea: pineapple contains bromelain, a group of protein-digesting enzymes (the reason fresh pineapple tenderises meat). The theory is that bromelain might reach the eye and help break down the protein clumps that cause floaters.

The evidence:

  • Horng and colleagues (Taiwan, 2019) asked 190 people with floaters to eat fresh pineapple daily for three months and reported that floaters decreased, with larger amounts linked to more improvement.
  • The study had no comparison group of people with floaters who did not eat pineapple. Because floaters often fade on their own, we cannot tell how much of the improvement was due to pineapple.
  • It is not known whether bromelain from food even reaches the vitreous after digestion. Canned pineapple is heat treated, which inactivates bromelain.
  • The same research group later tested capsules of fruit enzymes (bromelain, papain and ficin) in 2020 and 2022 and again reported fewer floaters, but these studies also came from one group and have not been confirmed independently.

Verdict: Unproven. Eating fresh pineapple as part of a normal diet is safe for most people, so there is no harm in enjoying it. But it should not replace an eye examination, and bromelain supplements are not recommended for floaters.

Who should be careful: people with diabetes (pineapple is sugary), people taking blood thinners such as warfarin, apixaban, clopidogrel or aspirin (bromelain supplements may increase bleeding), people allergic to pineapple or latex, and anyone with mouth ulcers or acidity. Ask your doctor before taking enzyme capsules.

See an eye doctor the same day if floaters appear suddenly, increase suddenly, come with flashes of light, or you notice a shadow or curtain in your vision. These can be signs of a retinal tear or detachment, which needs urgent laser or surgery. See floaters and retinal detachment.

What actually helps floaters: an examination to rule out a tear, reassurance, and time. For a small number of people with severe floaters that affect daily life for months, a vitrectomy operation (removing the vitreous gel) can help; YAG laser vitreolysis is an option for selected large floaters. Both carry risks and are discussed only after careful assessment.

Natural remedies for the eyes: the evidence at a glance

Remedy (claimed for)Verdict and evidence
Time outdoors (daylight)
For: Preventing short sight in children		Proven. A trial in Guangzhou, China, added one 40-minute outdoor class to each school day: over 3 years, fewer children became myopic (30.4% vs 39.5%). Aim for about 2 hours outdoors daily. See myopia control.
AREDS2 vitamin formula (vitamin C, vitamin E, zinc, copper, lutein, zeaxanthin)
For: Age-related macular degeneration (AMD)		Proven (for intermediate AMD). Reduces the risk of progression to advanced AMD by about a quarter in people with intermediate AMD or advanced AMD in one eye. No proven benefit if you have no AMD or only early AMD. See AMD.
Green leafy vegetables, fish, a balanced diet
For: General eye health, AMD		Some evidence. Diets rich in leafy greens (lutein) and fish are linked with lower AMD risk in observational studies. Good for overall health; sensible advice for everyone.
Warm compresses and eyelid cleaning
For: Blocked oil glands (meibomian gland dysfunction), blepharitis, styes		Proven (as part of treatment). Standard first-line care. Use a clean warm compress for 5–10 minutes, then gently massage the lid edge. Not too hot.
Carrots and vitamin A
For: Better eyesight, night vision		Proven only in deficiency. Vitamin A prevents night blindness and xerophthalmia in children who lack it. In well-nourished people, extra carrots do not improve eyesight or remove glasses. Large doses of vitamin A supplements can be harmful.
Omega-3 fish oil capsules
For: Dry eye		Unproven. The DREAM trial (535 people, 3000 mg daily for a year) found omega-3 capsules were no better than placebo for dry eye symptoms or signs. Eating fish is still healthy.
Manuka honey eye drops and gel (sterile, licensed products)
For: Dry eye from blocked oil glands		Some evidence. Small randomised trials of sterile medical-grade manuka honey drops and gel (for example Optimel) showed improved symptoms and signs. This applies only to sterile eye products made for the purpose, never kitchen honey.
Tea tree oil lid wipes
For: Demodex (eyelash mite) blepharitis		Some evidence. Can reduce mites but often irritates the eyes; must be diluted and used as lid wipes, not in the eye. A licensed prescription drop (lotilaner 0.25%) is now proven in trials.
Saffron (about 20–30 mg a day)
For: Dry AMD		Some evidence. Small trials found modest improvements in retinal function tests. Not proven to prevent vision loss. Do not exceed doses used in studies.
Bilberry (and blueberry extracts)
For: Night vision, tired eyes		Unproven. The most rigorous trials found no improvement in night vision. Bilberry may increase bleeding with blood thinners.
Ginkgo biloba
For: Glaucoma (especially normal-tension glaucoma)		Unproven. Trials give conflicting results; a well-designed crossover trial found no effect on visual fields. Can increase bleeding risk (dangerous before surgery). Does not replace glaucoma drops.
Nicotinamide (vitamin B3)
For: Glaucoma		Some evidence (under trial). Early trials suggest short-term improvement in visual function; large trials are still running. High doses can affect the liver, so take it only if your glaucoma specialist advises.
Cucumber slices, cold spoons, chilled tea bags
For: Puffy or tired eyes		Comfort only. Coolness reduces puffiness for a short time. Harmless on closed eyelids if clean; nothing should go inside the eye.
Eye exercises, palming, "vision training" programmes
For: Removing glasses, curing short sight		Unproven. Exercises do not change short sight, long sight or astigmatism. Specific supervised exercises do help convergence insufficiency (difficulty focusing both eyes at near).
Marijuana (cannabis)
For: Glaucoma		Not recommended. It lowers eye pressure for only 3–4 hours, so it would need to be taken many times a day, and it lowers blood pressure, which may harm the optic nerve. Glaucoma societies advise against it.
Castor oil on the lids
For: Dry eye, lashes		Unproven. Small studies only. Not sterile; can cause irritation and blurred vision. Licensed lubricants are safer.

Home remedies that can harm the eye

Anything that goes inside the eye must be sterile. Kitchen and plant products carry bacteria and fungi and can turn a small scratch into a corneal ulcer, which can leave a permanent scar or even need a corneal transplant.

RemedyWhy it is harmful
Breast milk, ghee, butter, oil or animal blood in the eyeNot sterile; can carry bacteria. In a study from South India, almost half of patients with corneal ulcers had used traditional eye medicines, breast milk being the commonest.
Plant juices, leaves, herbal pastes, onion or lemon juiceCan carry fungi (a major cause of corneal ulcers in India) and can burn the surface of the eye.
Kitchen honey, sugar water or salt waterNot sterile; may contain spores. Only licensed sterile honey eye products are used in treatment.
Rose water and herbal eye washes from shopsOften not made to eye-drop standards; contamination can cause infection.
Kajal and surmaCan cause infection and allergy, and some contain lead, which is harmful to children.
Leftover or borrowed eye drops, steroid drops without prescriptionOld bottles can be contaminated; steroids can cause glaucoma, cataract and worsen infections such as fungal or herpes keratitis.
Removing a foreign body with grass, cloth corners or a tongueScratches the cornea and introduces infection. Rinse with clean water and see an eye doctor.

For residents and practitioners: talking about natural remedies

Ask, do not dismiss

  • Ask every patient, without judgement, about supplements, herbal products and home remedies; record them in the notes. Many patients do not volunteer them.
  • Acknowledge the wish for a "natural" option, explain the evidence in simple terms, and offer what is proven (for example outdoor time, AREDS2 where indicated, lid hygiene).
  • Put the eye first: no home remedy should delay assessment of red flags (sudden floaters and flashes, painful red eye, sudden vision loss, chemical injury).

Interactions and safety points

  • Bleeding: ginkgo, bilberry, bromelain, high-dose fish oil, garlic and turmeric supplements may add to the effect of anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs; ask before intravitreal injections, oculoplastic and orbital surgery.
  • Beta-carotene: increased lung cancer risk in smokers (the reason AREDS2 replaced beta-carotene with lutein and zeaxanthin).
  • Vitamin A: avoid high-dose supplements in pregnancy (teratogenic) and in Stargardt disease (may speed lipofuscin build-up).
  • Niacin (nicotinic acid, high dose): can cause cystoid macular oedema; nicotinamide (a different form) is under study for glaucoma and can affect the liver at high doses.
  • Hidden steroids: some unlabelled "herbal" eye drops and creams contain steroids; ask in unexplained raised IOP or cataract.
  • Contaminated products: outbreaks of serious eye infections have been linked to non-sterile eye products; any product used in the eye must be sterile and licensed.

Appraising "natural cure" studies

  • Look for a control group and randomisation. Single-arm studies (like the pineapple study) cannot separate the remedy's effect from natural improvement, regression to the mean or placebo.
  • Check whether the outcome is subjective (floaters counted by patients) or objective, and whether assessors were masked.
  • Check biological plausibility (does the substance reach the eye?), dose, independent replication, conflicts of interest and the journal.
  • Small improvements in a test (such as an ERG with saffron) do not always mean better vision for the patient.

Quick answers

Should I eat pineapple for my floaters? You can enjoy fresh pineapple as food, but there is no proof it removes floaters. Have your eyes examined first, especially if the floaters are new.

Which vitamins should I take for my eyes? Most people do not need eye vitamins. The AREDS2 formula is advised only for people with intermediate or one-sided advanced AMD, after an eye examination.

Can honey or ghee cure an eye infection? No. Putting kitchen products in the eye can cause serious infection. Use only drops prescribed by your eye doctor.

Is there a natural way to stop my child's number increasing? Yes: about 2 hours a day outdoors in daylight helps prevent short sight. Once a child is myopic, ask about proven treatments such as low-dose atropine and special spectacle lenses.
