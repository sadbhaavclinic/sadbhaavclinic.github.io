IOL Power Calculation in Paediatric Cataract: Biometry Under Anaesthesia, Target Refraction by Age and the Myopic Shift

Last updated: November 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • The child's eye keeps growing after surgery, so an IOL that gives emmetropia now gives significant myopia later.
  • Aim for planned hyperopia that reduces with age: a common guide is "age plus target equals about 7" up to about age 7 (for example +5 D at age 2).
  • Measure axial length with immersion A-scan under anaesthesia; contact A-scan compresses the cornea, gives a short reading and leads to a myopic surprise.
  • No formula is clearly best in children; prediction errors are larger in short eyes, so check biometry carefully and use a modern formula consistently.
  • In infants under about 7 months, many surgeons leave the eye aphakic and use a contact lens, based on the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study.

Choosing an IOL for a 2-year-old is not the same as for a 70-year-old. The eye will lengthen by several millimetres and the cornea will flatten, so the refraction will drift towards myopia for years. The surgeon has to choose a power for the adult the child will become while giving the child usable vision now, and the amblyopia treatment depends on that choice.

Why the child's eye is different

FactorEffect on IOL calculation
Axial growthRapid in the first two years, slower until about 10 years; causes a myopic shift after surgery
Steep keratometryInfant corneas are steeper and flatten over the first year or two
Short eyesSmall errors in axial length cause large refractive errors
Biometry under anaesthesiaMeasurements are harder, and contact A-scan often compresses the cornea
Variable growthThe myopic shift differs widely between children and is hard to predict
AmblyopiaResidual refractive error must be corrected immediately with spectacles or contact lenses

Biometry: get the numbers right

  • Axial length: immersion A-scan under anaesthesia is preferred. Contact applanation shortens the axial length by indenting the cornea and leads to too strong an IOL and a myopic result. Optical biometry is excellent in older, cooperative children.
  • Keratometry: hand-held keratometer or a portable topographer under anaesthesia; take several readings and check against the fellow eye.
  • Measure the fellow eye for comparison in unilateral cases; large differences in axial length or keratometry should prompt a repeat.
  • Record the white-to-white or corneal diameter: microcornea and microphthalmos change the plan.

Which formula?

Studies in children show larger prediction errors than in adults, often around 1 D or more, and no formula is consistently best. SRK/T, Hoffer Q, Holladay 1, Holladay 2 and Barrett Universal II are all used; Hoffer Q and Holladay formulas may do slightly better in short eyes in some series. Use a modern theoretical formula, use it consistently, and audit your own results. Avoid old regression formulas such as SRK I.

Choosing the target refraction

Most surgeons aim for planned hyperopia that reduces as the child grows, so that the eye ends up near emmetropia or mild myopia in adulthood. Common guides:

Age at surgeryTypical postoperative target (spectacle plane)
Under 1 year (if an IOL is used)+6 to +10 D, or leave aphakic
1 year+6 D
2 years+5 D
3 years+4 D
4 years+3 D
5 years+2 D
6 years+1 D
7 years and abovePlano to mild hyperopia (around +0.5 D)

This follows the "rule of 7" (age in years plus target refraction equals about 7) described by Wilson and colleagues. An alternative is the Dahan approach: reduce the emmetropic IOL power by about 20% under 2 years of age and by about 10% from 2 to 8 years. Enter the target directly into the formula rather than subtracting dioptres by hand.

Unilateral versus bilateral cataract

  • Bilateral: follow the age-based target and give spectacles (often bifocals once the child can use them) for the residual hyperopia.
  • Unilateral: a large hyperopic target creates anisometropia and makes amblyopia treatment harder. Many surgeons choose a smaller hyperopic target, closer to the fellow eye, accepting more myopia in adulthood that can be corrected later.
  • Consider the family history of myopia and the fellow eye refraction; a child of highly myopic parents may shift more.

Infants: lessons from the Infant Aphakia Treatment Study

The Infant Aphakia Treatment Study (IATS) randomised infants aged 1 to 6 months with unilateral cataract to a primary IOL or aphakia with a contact lens. Visual acuity was similar in the two groups at 4.5 and 10.5 years, but the IOL group had more adverse events and additional operations, and the myopic shift was large and variable. Many surgeons therefore leave infants under about 7 months aphakic, correct with a contact lens and consider a secondary IOL later. Glaucoma after cataract surgery remains a lifelong risk in both groups.

Lens choice and placement

  • Single-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL in the capsular bag is preferred; three-piece IOL in the sulcus or optic capture when the bag is not suitable, reducing the power for sulcus placement as advised by the manufacturer.
  • Primary posterior capsulotomy with anterior vitrectomy in young children, because posterior capsule opacification is almost universal.
  • Later myopia can be managed with spectacles or contact lenses, IOL exchange, a piggyback IOL or refractive surgery in adulthood.

Before the child leaves theatre: write down the expected refraction, the plan for spectacles or contact lenses within days, and the patching plan. The IOL is only half of the treatment; the amblyopia work decides the final vision.

Pearls

  • Use immersion A-scan under anaesthesia; contact A-scan gives a falsely short eye and a myopic surprise.
  • Rule of 7: age plus target refraction about 7, up to age 7.
  • Unilateral cases: consider a smaller hyperopic target to limit anisometropia.
  • Expect wide variation in the myopic shift; tell parents that glasses will change many times.
  • Correct the residual refraction within days of surgery and start patching early.

Common mistakes

  • Aiming for emmetropia in a 1-year-old.
  • Trusting a single contact A-scan reading that does not match the fellow eye.
  • Forgetting to reduce the power for a sulcus-placed IOL.
  • Delaying spectacles or contact lenses after surgery.
