Uveitis OSCE Stations: 8 Spot Stations on KPs, Hypopyon, Synechiae, Toxoplasmosis, CMV, VKH and Biologic Safety

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 8 spot stations, 32 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 8 original stations cover the uveitis topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. Keratic precipitates, 2. Hypopyon, 3. Posterior synechiae, 4. Grading anterior chamber cells, 5. Ocular toxoplasmosis, 6. CMV retinitis, 7. Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease, 8. Before starting a biologic

Station 1: Keratic precipitates

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph: large, greasy, yellow-white precipitates on the inferior corneal endothelium in a triangular pattern.

  1. Describe them.
    Large "mutton-fat" keratic precipitates in Arlt's triangle.
  2. What do they suggest?
    Granulomatous uveitis: sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, syphilis, VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, or herpetic uveitis.
  3. Where are KPs diffuse rather than inferior?
    Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis (fine, stellate KPs over the whole endothelium) and herpetic or CMV anterior uveitis.
  4. What first-line tests do you order for granulomatous uveitis?
    Chest X-ray or CT, ACE and serum calcium, syphilis serology (treponemal test), TB testing (IGRA or Mantoux), and others guided by history.

Station 2: Hypopyon

The examiner shows you: A slit-lamp photograph: a 2 mm white hypopyon with a fibrinous reaction in a young man with back pain; the eye is very red and painful.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    HLA-B27-associated acute anterior uveitis.
  2. How does the hypopyon of Behçet disease differ?
    It often forms in a relatively white eye, shifts with head position and comes and goes quickly; it is associated with retinal vasculitis.
  3. Name non-inflammatory causes of hypopyon.
    Endophthalmitis, infective keratitis, and masquerade syndromes (retinoblastoma in children, lymphoma or leukaemia).
  4. What is the treatment?
    Intensive topical steroid, cycloplegic, and sub-conjunctival mydriatics if synechiae are forming; refer for spondyloarthropathy assessment.

Station 3: Posterior synechiae

The examiner shows you: A dilated pupil with an irregular, flower-shaped ("festooned") margin; pigment deposits on the anterior lens capsule.

  1. What do you see?
    Posterior synechiae: adhesions between iris and lens, with a festooned pupil on dilation.
  2. Why do they matter?
    360° posterior synechiae (seclusio pupillae) cause iris bombé and secondary angle closure.
  3. How are they broken?
    Intensive cycloplegia and mydriatics, sub-conjunctival mydricaine, and controlling inflammation.
  4. What treatment does iris bombé need?
    Urgent laser peripheral iridotomy or surgical iridectomy, with control of inflammation.

Station 4: Grading anterior chamber cells

The examiner shows you: A narrow 1 mm × 1 mm slit beam in a dark room shows about 20 cells in the anterior chamber; iris details are still clear.

  1. Grade the cells and flare.
    Cells 2+ (16–25 cells). Flare: if present with clear iris details, 2+ (moderate).
  2. Which system is this?
    The Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) system.
  3. What counts as improvement under SUN?
    A two-step decrease in inflammation (for example cells from 2+ to 0.5+), or a decrease to grade 0.
  4. Why do flare grades need care?
    Flare from chronic blood–aqueous barrier damage may persist without active inflammation, so treat cells, not flare alone.

Station 5: Ocular toxoplasmosis

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph: a fluffy white area of active retinochoroiditis next to a pigmented scar, with overlying vitreous haze ("headlight in the fog").

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Reactivated ocular toxoplasmosis next to an old scar.
  2. When do you treat?
    Lesions threatening the macula or optic disc, large lesions, severe vitritis, and immunocompromised patients; small peripheral lesions in healthy people may be observed.
  3. Name treatment options.
    Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine and folinic acid ("classic therapy"); or intravitreal clindamycin with dexamethasone. Oral steroid only with antiparasitic cover.
  4. What is the risk of intravitreal or periocular steroid alone?
    Fulminant necrotising retinitis: never give local steroid without antiparasitic treatment.

Station 6: CMV retinitis

The examiner shows you: A fundus photograph of a man with HIV and a CD4 count of 30: a creamy white area of retinitis with haemorrhages along the vessels ("pizza pie" or "cheese and ketchup").

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Cytomegalovirus retinitis.
  2. At what CD4 count does it usually occur?
    Below 50 cells/µL.
  3. What is the treatment?
    Oral valganciclovir or IV ganciclovir, with intravitreal ganciclovir or foscarnet for sight-threatening lesions; start or optimise antiretroviral therapy.
  4. What is immune recovery uveitis?
    Inflammation (vitritis, CME) after immune recovery on antiretroviral therapy in eyes with previous CMV retinitis.

Station 7: Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease

The examiner shows you: Bilateral serous retinal detachments and disc swelling in a young woman with headache and tinnitus. FFA shows multiple pinpoint leaks pooling into the detachments.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Acute Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease.
  2. Name its systemic features.
    Meningism and headache, tinnitus and hearing loss, then later vitiligo, poliosis and alopecia.
  3. What does the chronic stage look like?
    "Sunset glow" fundus, Dalen–Fuchs nodules and peripapillary atrophy.
  4. What is the treatment?
    Early high-dose systemic steroids (often IV first), with early steroid-sparing immunosuppression to prevent chronic recurrent disease.

Station 8: Before starting a biologic

The examiner shows you: A patient with refractory uveitis is to start adalimumab.

  1. Which infections must you screen for?
    Tuberculosis (IGRA or Mantoux and chest X-ray), hepatitis B and C, HIV, and varicella immunity.
  2. Which conditions contraindicate anti-TNF drugs?
    Demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis, moderate to severe heart failure, active infection and some cancers.
  3. What evidence supports adalimumab?
    VISUAL I and II in adults, and SYCAMORE in JIA-associated uveitis.
  4. What about vaccines?
    Avoid live vaccines while on treatment; give inactivated vaccines, ideally before starting.
