Paediatric and Strabismus OSCE Stations: 10 Spot Stations on Cover Tests, Stereotests, Cycloplegia, Leukocoria and Amblyopia

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

How to use these stations

  • 10 spot stations, 40 questions, each with a model answer.
  • Read what the examiner shows you, answer each question aloud, then tap it to reveal the answer.
  • Allow about 5 minutes a station, as in most OSCE circuits.
  • For case-based viva stations across every subspecialty, use the Viva Hot Seat.

OSCE spot stations test whether you can recognise an instrument, an image, a printout or a theatre event, and then answer the questions an examiner follows up with. These 10 original stations cover the paediatric and strabismus topics that come up most often. Key pictures are shown as original teaching drawings; the rest are described in words, as many written OSCE papers do.

Stations: 1. Cover tests, 2. Hirschberg and Krimsky, 3. Stereotests, 4. Worth four-dot test, 5. Cycloplegic refraction, 6. Leukocoria, 7. Amblyopia, 8. Duane retraction syndrome, 9. Paediatric cataract, 10. Infantile esotropia

Station 1: Cover tests

The examiner shows you: A child fixing a target at 33 cm. Covering the left eye makes the right eye move outwards to take up fixation; covering the right eye causes no movement of the left.

  1. What does this show?
    A right esotropia (the right eye moves out to fixate, so it was turned in).
  2. How do the cover test and the cover-uncover test differ?
    The cover test reveals a manifest deviation (tropia). The cover-uncover test reveals a latent deviation (phoria) as the covered eye recovers when uncovered.
  3. How is the deviation measured?
    The alternate prism cover test, with prisms held base-out for esodeviation, at near and distance.
  4. What is the accommodative element?
    Repeat after full cycloplegic correction: if the eyes straighten with glasses, the esotropia is fully accommodative.

Station 2: Hirschberg and Krimsky

The examiner shows you: A photograph of an infant: the corneal light reflex is central in the left eye and at the temporal pupil margin in the right eye.

  1. What is the deviation?
    A right esotropia of about 15° (about 30 prism dioptres).
  2. What are the Hirschberg estimates?
    About 1 mm of reflex decentration equals 7° (about 15 prism dioptres): pupil margin about 15°, mid-iris about 30°, limbus about 45°.
  3. What is the Krimsky test?
    Prisms are placed in front of the fixing eye until the light reflexes are symmetrical; used when cover testing is not possible.
  4. What is a positive angle kappa?
    A nasally displaced reflex in both eyes from the visual axis and pupillary axis not coinciding (for example in ROP with dragged macula), which can mimic exotropia.

Station 3: Stereotests

The examiner shows you: A set of tests: a Titmus fly booklet, a Lang card, a TNO book with red-green glasses, and a Frisby plate.

  1. What do these tests measure?
    Stereoacuity: the finest depth difference seen, in seconds of arc.
  2. Which needs no glasses, and why does that help?
    Lang (random-dot with cylindrical gratings) and Frisby (real depth): no glasses, so useful in young children.
  3. What is normal adult stereoacuity?
    About 40–60 seconds of arc or better.
  4. Why are random-dot tests better than the Titmus fly?
    The fly has monocular clues; random-dot targets cannot be seen without true binocular stereopsis.

Station 4: Worth four-dot test

The examiner shows you: A patient wearing red-green glasses (red lens over the right eye) looks at a box with one red, two green and one white light.

  1. What do the responses mean?
    Four lights: normal fusion (or harmonious ARC with a manifest squint). Two red lights: left eye suppression. Three green lights: right eye suppression. Five lights: diplopia.
  2. If five lights, how can you tell eso from exo?
    Uncrossed diplopia (red on the right, from the right eye) means esodeviation; crossed diplopia means exodeviation.
  3. Why test at near and distance?
    Peripheral fusion may be present at near (the lights subtend a larger angle) while central suppression shows at distance.
  4. Name another test of suppression.
    Bagolini striated lenses, the 4-prism-dioptre base-out test, or the synoptophore.

Station 5: Cycloplegic refraction

The examiner shows you: Three bottles: cyclopentolate 1%, tropicamide 1% and atropine 1% ointment.

  1. Which agent and when?
    Cyclopentolate 1% is the routine cycloplegic for children (two drops 5 minutes apart, refract at 30–45 minutes). Atropine is strongest, used for accommodative esotropia or dark irides. Tropicamide alone gives weak cycloplegia.
  2. How long do their effects last?
    Tropicamide: hours. Cyclopentolate: up to 24 hours. Atropine: 7–14 days.
  3. Name signs of atropine toxicity.
    "Hot as a hare, red as a beet, dry as a bone, mad as a hatter": fever, flushing, dry mouth, tachycardia, and restlessness or confusion.
  4. What dose adjustments do infants need?
    Cyclopentolate 0.5% (or 0.2% with phenylephrine 1%) in infants under about 6 months, with apnoea and feeding precautions in premature babies.

Station 6: Leukocoria

The examiner shows you: A photograph of an 18-month-old: a white pupillary reflex in the right eye and a right esotropia.

  1. What must you rule out first?
    Retinoblastoma.
  2. Give other causes of leukocoria.
    Congenital cataract, persistent fetal vasculature, Coats disease, retinopathy of prematurity, toxocariasis, coloboma and retinal detachment.
  3. How do you investigate?
    Urgent examination under anaesthesia with dilated fundus examination, ultrasound (calcification) and MRI of the orbits and brain (avoid CT radiation in RB1 carriers).
  4. What is the significance of the RB1 gene?
    Germline mutation causes bilateral, multifocal and earlier disease, with a risk of pinealoblastoma ("trilateral") and second cancers such as osteosarcoma; siblings and children need screening.

Station 7: Amblyopia

The examiner shows you: A 5-year-old has 6/24 in the right eye and 6/6 in the left after wearing full glasses for 16 weeks. Cycloplegic refraction: right +4.50 DS, left +0.50 DS.

  1. What type of amblyopia is this?
    Anisometropic amblyopia, moderate (6/12 to 6/24).
  2. What is the first step in treatment?
    Full glasses worn constantly for at least 16–18 weeks (refractive adaptation), which this child has done.
  3. What next?
    Patching the better eye for 2 hours a day (PEDIG) or weekend atropine to the better eye.
  4. Name risks of treatment.
    Reverse amblyopia in the patched eye, poor adherence, skin irritation, and recurrence when treatment stops (wean gradually).

Station 8: Duane retraction syndrome

The examiner shows you: A child with limited abduction of the left eye; on adduction the left eye retracts, the palpebral fissure narrows and the eye shoots up.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Left Duane retraction syndrome, type 1.
  2. What is the mechanism?
    Congenital absence of the sixth nerve nucleus with aberrant innervation of the lateral rectus by a branch of the third nerve, causing co-contraction on adduction.
  3. Describe the three types.
    Type 1: limited abduction (commonest). Type 2: limited adduction. Type 3: both limited.
  4. When is surgery considered?
    Significant primary-position deviation, an abnormal head posture, or marked upshoots or downshoots; recession surgery (not resection) is used.

Station 9: Paediatric cataract

The examiner shows you: A 6-week-old with a dense central cataract in one eye and no red reflex.

  1. Why is this urgent?
    Dense unilateral cataract must be removed early (ideally by 6 weeks) to avoid deep, irreversible stimulus-deprivation amblyopia.
  2. What investigations do you do?
    Family history and examination of parents; TORCH screen, galactosaemia screening and other metabolic or genetic tests, especially when bilateral.
  3. How is the eye made optically clear after surgery?
    Lensectomy with posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy; then contact lens or primary IOL (IATS: similar vision, more reoperations with IOL under 7 months).
  4. Which late complications do you watch for?
    Glaucoma (aphakic glaucoma, lifelong risk), visual axis opacification, amblyopia and strabismus.

Station 10: Infantile esotropia

The examiner shows you: A 9-month-old with a large constant esotropia (50 prism dioptres) since about 4 months, cross-fixation and a small cycloplegic refraction.

  1. What is the diagnosis?
    Infantile (congenital) esotropia.
  2. Name associated features.
    Large stable angle, cross-fixation, latent nystagmus, inferior oblique overaction and dissociated vertical deviation.
  3. What is the treatment?
    Correct significant hyperopia, treat amblyopia, then early surgery (usually bilateral medial rectus recession), often before 18–24 months to improve the chance of some binocularity.
  4. How do you assess abduction?
    The doll's head manoeuvre or brief patching of one eye, to exclude a sixth nerve palsy.
