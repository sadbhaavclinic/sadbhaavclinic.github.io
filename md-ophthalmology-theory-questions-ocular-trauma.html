MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Ocular Trauma (22 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 22 ocular trauma theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Classification & Approach

TR 1. Discuss the classification of ocular trauma (BETTS). Evaluate vision-threatening injuries and the principles of their management for the best possible outcome. HIGH-YIELD · outline

BETTS (Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology System)

  • Closed globe: contusion, lamellar laceration (partial thickness), superficial foreign body.
  • Open globe: rupture (blunt force, inside-out, often at the limbus or behind recti) or laceration (sharp object): penetrating (entry only), perforating (entry and exit), IOFB.
  • Ocular Trauma Classification Group: type, grade (vision), pupil (RAPD), zone (I cornea and limbus; II up to 5 mm behind the limbus; III beyond).

Vision-threatening injuries

  • Open globe, IOFB, endophthalmitis, hyphaema with raised IOP, lens dislocation, commotio, choroidal rupture, macular hole, RD, traumatic optic neuropathy, chemical injury, orbital compartment syndrome.

Principles of management

  • Life before eye (ATLS), tetanus, protect with a shield (no pressure), avoid drops in open globe, CT orbit (no MRI if metal suspected).
  • Primary repair within 24 hours, watertight closure, remove prolapsed non-viable tissue, systemic antibiotics, secondary procedures (lens, vitrectomy, IOFB removal) as needed; ocular trauma score (OTS) for prognosis and counselling.

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema, Open Globe Injury: The BETT Classification

TR 2. Prognostic significance of various manifestations of ocular trauma on the globe and adnexa. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Ocular Trauma Score (OTS)

  • Initial vision (most powerful), rupture, endophthalmitis, perforating injury, RD, RAPD: points give a score 1–5 predicting final vision.

Poor prognostic signs

  • Vision of no light perception or light perception at presentation, RAPD, posterior (zone III) wounds, rupture, perforating injury, large wounds (> 10 mm), endophthalmitis, RD, vitreous haemorrhage, choroidal haemorrhage, uveal prolapse, delay in repair.

Adnexa

  • Canalicular laceration (repair within 48 hours), orbital fractures with entrapment (white-eyed blowout in children: urgent), retrobulbar haemorrhage (lateral canthotomy within 2 hours), lid margin involvement, levator injury; traumatic optic neuropathy predicts poor vision.

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema

TR 3. Three most important ophthalmic emergencies needing immediate management. DNB / UNIV · outline

Three most important ophthalmic emergencies

  • Chemical injury (especially alkali): immediate copious irrigation for at least 30 minutes until pH is neutral, before any examination or referral.
  • Central retinal artery occlusion: urgent stroke pathway, measures to lower IOP and dislodge emboli, exclude giant cell arteritis.
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma: IV acetazolamide and mannitol, topical beta-blockers, alpha-agonists, pilocarpine once IOP falls, then laser iridotomy.

Other emergencies

  • Open globe injury, endophthalmitis, orbital compartment syndrome (retrobulbar haemorrhage: canthotomy and cantholysis), orbital cellulitis, giant cell arteritis with vision loss, macula-on retinal detachment, microbial keratitis (especially corneal ulcer in a contact lens wearer), pituitary apoplexy, papilloedema with vision loss.
TR 4. Ocular Trauma Score and its prognostic value. · outline

Ocular Trauma Score (OTS)

  • Developed from the US and Hungarian Eye Injury Registries (Kuhn, 2002) to predict final visual outcome after trauma.

Calculation

  • Start with raw points for initial vision: NPL 60, PL or HM 70, 1/200–19/200 80, 20/200–20/50 90, 20/40 or better 100.
  • Subtract: globe rupture −23, endophthalmitis −17, perforating injury −14, retinal detachment −11, RAPD −10.
  • Sum converted to OTS category 1 (0–44) to 5 (92–100); each category gives probabilities of final vision (e.g. category 1: about 73% NPL; category 5: about 94% 20/40 or better).

Value

  • Simple, uses initial findings, validated in adults (and in children with modifications: POTS), helps counselling and research; limitations: initial vision may be hard to measure, does not include zone or IOFB.
TR 19. Abusive head trauma (shaken baby syndrome) — ocular findings, documentation and legal duties. NEW · outline

Abusive head trauma (shaken baby syndrome)

  • Inflicted head injury in infants and young children (usually under 2 years) from violent shaking with or without impact.

Ocular findings

  • Retinal haemorrhages: numerous, multilayered (preretinal, intraretinal, subretinal), extending to the ora serrata (highly suggestive of abuse), perimacular folds, haemorrhagic macular retinoschisis (strongly associated with abuse); vitreous haemorrhage; optic nerve sheath haemorrhage (post-mortem); periocular bruising.
  • Other injuries: subdural haemorrhage, encephalopathy, fractures (ribs, metaphyseal), bruises.

Differential diagnosis

  • Birth-related haemorrhages (common, resolve by 4–6 weeks), accidental trauma (usually few posterior haemorrhages), bleeding disorders, leukaemia, meningitis, galactosaemia, glutaric aciduria type 1, Terson syndrome, CPR (rarely causes extensive haemorrhages).

Documentation and legal duties

  • Dilated examination by an ophthalmologist within 24–72 hours, detailed description and drawings, wide-field photographs (RetCam), date and time, careful language.
  • Report suspected child abuse to the police and child protection services (POCSO Act and Juvenile Justice Act in India; mandatory reporting), coordinate with paediatricians and social workers; multidisciplinary evaluation.

Revise from: Medico-Legal Basics for Eye Surgeons in India: Negligence, Consumer Complaints, Indemnity, Documentation and Handling an Unhappy Patient

Open Globe Injury

TR 5. Open globe (penetrating/perforating) injury — management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Assessment

  • Mechanism, time, vision, RAPD, Seidel test, shallow AC, peaked pupil, uveal prolapse, low IOP (do not measure if obvious open globe), CT for IOFB.

Pre-operative

  • Shield, nil by mouth, IV antibiotics (vancomycin with ceftazidime, or moxifloxacin orally), tetanus, antiemetics, general anaesthesia (avoid succinylcholine debated).

Primary repair

  • Corneal: 10-0 nylon, short bites in the periphery, longer centrally, limbus first, then straight segments; reposit viable uvea, excise vitreous at the wound, OVD support.
  • Scleral: explore 360°, close from anterior to posterior, disinsert muscles if needed; tissue glue for small self-sealing wounds.
  • Lens: remove if ruptured; intracameral and intravitreal antibiotics if contaminated.

Secondary

  • Vitrectomy at 7–14 days (PVR prevention), IOFB removal, IOL; consider primary enucleation only for completely destroyed eyes (sympathetic ophthalmia risk discussed).

Revise from: Open Globe Injury: The BETT Classification

TR 6. Corneoscleral perforation with uveal or vitreous prolapse (e.g. bow-and-arrow injury in a child) — repair techniques. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneoscleral perforation with uveal or vitreous prolapse

  • Typical in children (bow-and-arrow, sticks, pencils, firecrackers); general anaesthesia; avoid pressure on the globe; CT for IOFB.

Repair techniques

  • Explore and assess: identify the extent, uveal tissue viability (prolapse under 24 hours and not necrotic: reposit after washing with antibiotic; necrotic or contaminated: excise), vitreous (cut with vitrector or sponge and scissors, never pull).
  • Close the limbus first with 9-0 or 10-0 nylon to restore anatomy, then corneal part (shorter bites centrally, longer peripherally; avoid the visual axis where possible), then scleral part (8-0 nylon or 7-0 silk) exploring posteriorly.
  • Re-form AC with BSS, air or OVD; free iris from wounds; remove lens if ruptured (lensectomy, IOL later).
  • Intracameral or intravitreal antibiotics if contaminated; tetanus; systemic antibiotics.

Post-operative

  • Steroids, cycloplegics, monitor for endophthalmitis, RD, sympathetic ophthalmia; early amblyopia management in children (refraction, contact lens, patching); suture removal under anaesthesia.
TR 7. Outline the types of intraocular foreign bodies, their localisation and critical management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Inert (glass, plastic, stone, gold, silver), reactive (iron: siderosis; copper: chalcosis, pure copper causes endophthalmitis-like reaction), organic (wood, vegetation: infection risk).

Localisation

  • History (hammering metal on metal), slit lamp, gonioscopy, indirect ophthalmoscopy, CT (thin cuts; investigation of choice), B-scan, UBM (anterior), X-ray; MRI contraindicated for metallic foreign bodies.

Management

  • Repair entry wound; prophylactic systemic and intravitreal antibiotics (Bacillus risk).
  • Anterior chamber: removal through limbal incision (magnet or forceps).
  • Lens: remove with lens if opaque.
  • Posterior: pars plana vitrectomy with forceps or intraocular magnet; large IOFB via limbus.
  • Inert, small, well-tolerated: may be observed with ERG monitoring.

Revise from: Intraocular Foreign Body, Traumatic Optic Neuropathy and Lid Lacerations

TR 8. Siderosis bulbi (with ERG changes) and chalcosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Siderosis bulbi

  • Iron deposits in epithelial tissues: rust-brown corneal stromal staining, iris heterochromia (darker), mydriasis, anterior subcapsular brown dots (lens), pigmentary retinopathy, secondary glaucoma.
  • ERG: initial increase in a-wave, then progressive decrease of b-wave (b-wave reduction with negative ERG); flat ERG late.

Chalcosis

  • Copper (less than 85% pure): Kayser–Fleischer-like ring, sunflower cataract, green iris, retinal deposits.
  • Pure copper (more than 85%): acute sterile suppurative inflammation.

Management

  • Remove the foreign body early (ERG changes reversible early); monitor ERG for retained IOFB.

Revise from: Ocular Siderosis: Signs, ERG Findings and Management

TR 9. Corneal and conjunctival foreign bodies. DNB / UNIV · outline

Corneal and conjunctival foreign bodies

  • Common in workers grinding, hammering or in windy conditions; dust, metal, glass, vegetable matter, insect wings.

Evaluation

  • History (high velocity suggests penetration), vision, slit lamp, evert upper lid (subtarsal FB), fluorescein (ice-rink linear abrasions), Seidel test, depth assessment, dilated fundus examination if penetration suspected, X-ray or CT for IOFB.

Removal

  • Topical anaesthesia, irrigation, cotton bud or 26-gauge needle tangentially under slit lamp; burr (Alger brush) for rust rings (remove at the same visit or after 24 hours); deep FB: in theatre.

After removal

  • Topical antibiotic ointment, cycloplegic for pain, avoid patching in contact lens wearers and with vegetable material (fungal risk), review in 24–48 hours; protective eyewear.
TR 10. Penetrating injury involving the posterior segment. DNB / UNIV · outline

Penetrating posterior segment injury

  • Zone III wounds; risks: vitreous haemorrhage, retinal breaks and incarceration, RD, endophthalmitis, PVR, IOFB, choroidal haemorrhage.

Evaluation

  • Vision, RAPD, CT (IOFB), B-scan (gently, once the wound is closed), ERG if dense media.

Management

  • Primary closure of the scleral wound (explore 360°, disinsert muscles if needed), prophylactic intravitreal antibiotics in contaminated injuries (vancomycin with ceftazidime), systemic antibiotics.
  • Secondary vitrectomy at 7–14 days (after PVD and reduced haemorrhage risk; before PVR): clear haemorrhage, remove IOFB, treat breaks, encircling band, tamponade; earlier for endophthalmitis or IOFB.
  • Prophylactic chorioretinectomy at the incarceration site in selected cases.

Prognosis

  • Poor with large wounds, RD, endophthalmitis, poor initial vision.

Closed Globe Injury

TR 11. Hyphaema — grading, investigations, complications (including corneal blood staining), medical management and indications for surgery; with secondary glaucoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Grading

  • Grade 0 microhyphaema (cells), I under one third, II one third to one half, III over one half, IV total (eight-ball, black blood).

Investigations

  • IOP, gonioscopy later (angle recession), B-scan to exclude posterior injury, sickle cell screening in Black patients, coagulation profile.

Complications

  • Rebleed (days 2–5), raised IOP, corneal blood staining (high IOP, endothelial damage, total hyphaema), peripheral anterior synechiae, optic atrophy, angle-recession glaucoma later.

Medical management

  • Rest with head elevated, shield, cycloplegics, topical steroids, IOP lowering (beta-blockers, alpha-agonists; avoid CAIs in sickle cell), antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid: debated), avoid NSAIDs and aspirin.

Indications for surgery (AC washout)

  • IOP over 50 mmHg for 5 days or over 35 mmHg for 7 days (or over 24 mmHg for 24 hours in sickle cell), early corneal blood staining, total hyphaema not resolving by 4–5 days, clot over 50% for 8–10 days.

Secondary glaucoma

  • Early: trabecular blockage, ghost cell glaucoma; late: angle recession (over 180°), synechial closure.

Revise from: Traumatic Hyphema: Current Management

TR 12. Ocular manifestations of blunt (concussion) trauma; Berlin's oedema. DNB / UNIV · outline

Ocular manifestations of blunt trauma

  • Lids and orbit: ecchymosis, lacerations, blowout fractures, retrobulbar haemorrhage.
  • Conjunctiva: subconjunctival haemorrhage, chemosis.
  • Cornea: abrasion, oedema, blood staining.
  • AC and angle: hyphaema, angle recession, iridodialysis, cyclodialysis (hypotony).
  • Iris and pupil: traumatic mydriasis, sphincter tears, Vossius ring (pigment on lens).
  • Lens: cataract (rosette), subluxation or dislocation.
  • Vitreous: haemorrhage, PVD.
  • Retina and choroid: commotio retinae (Berlin oedema), retinal tears and dialysis, macular hole, choroidal rupture (crescentic, concentric to the disc), sclopetaria, Purtscher retinopathy (indirect).
  • Optic nerve: traumatic optic neuropathy, avulsion.
  • Globe rupture (usually at the limbus or behind recti).

Berlin oedema (commotio retinae)

  • Grey-white opacification of the outer retina (photoreceptor outer segment disruption) at the posterior pole after blunt trauma, often with cherry-red-like fovea; resolves in days to weeks; vision usually recovers but pigmentary changes or macular hole may follow.
TR 13. Traumatic cataract. · outline

Traumatic cataract

  • After blunt or penetrating injury, radiation, electric shock, chemicals.

Types

  • Vossius ring (pigment imprint, not a cataract), rosette (flower-shaped: early or late), anterior or posterior subcapsular, total (with capsule rupture: lens matter in AC), localised opacity at the penetration site, subluxated or dislocated lens.

Evaluation

  • Capsule integrity, zonules (phacodonesis), IOP, B-scan for posterior segment, CT for IOFB, visual potential.

Management

  • Ruptured capsule with lens matter in the AC (inflammation, raised IOP): early surgery; otherwise elective.
  • Techniques: lens aspiration (young), phaco or MSICS, CTR or segments for zonular weakness, IOL (primary in selected adults, secondary in contaminated injuries), anterior vitrectomy if vitreous present; aphakic contact lens in children with amblyopia management.
TR 14. Angle recession. · outline

Angle recession

  • Tear between the longitudinal and circular fibres of the ciliary muscle after blunt trauma; often with hyphaema.

Gonioscopy

  • Irregularly widened ciliary body band, torn iris processes, depressed or absent scleral spur appearance; compare with the other eye.

Angle recession glaucoma

  • Risk proportional to extent (over 180° highest); develops months to decades later (trabecular scarring, hyaline membrane); fellow eye has higher POAG risk.

Management

  • Lifelong IOP monitoring; medications (aqueous suppressants, prostaglandins; miotics less effective), SLT (less effective), trabeculectomy with antimetabolites (higher failure), drainage devices.
TR 15. Traumatic optic neuropathy and optic nerve avulsion — clinical features and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Traumatic optic neuropathy

  • Direct (penetrating, bony fragment) or indirect (forces transmitted to the canal; commonest); frontal impact.
  • Features: vision loss, RAPD, normal disc initially (pallor in 3–6 weeks), CT for canal fractures and haematoma.
  • Management: observation (spontaneous recovery in many), steroids (CRASH trial: high-dose steroids harmful in head injury; IONTS: no benefit), optic canal decompression for selected cases with fragment or worsening vision; treat orbital compartment syndrome (canthotomy).

Optic nerve avulsion

  • Sudden rotational force; nerve is pulled from the globe at the lamina cribrosa.
  • Features: profound vision loss, RAPD, pit or cavity at the disc with haemorrhage (partial or complete).
  • No treatment; poor prognosis.

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema

TR 16. Photic and radiational injuries of the eye. DNB / UNIV · outline

Photic injuries

  • Solar retinopathy (eclipse viewing, sun gazing): photochemical damage at the fovea, yellow spot then lamellar hole; no treatment; prevention by proper filters.
  • Laser injuries (laser pointers, industrial, military): thermal or photodisruptive macular damage, haemorrhage; most recover partly.
  • Light toxicity from operating microscope and endoilluminators (photic maculopathy).
  • Welding arc (photokeratitis) and snow blindness: UV keratitis 6–12 hours after exposure; treat with cycloplegics, lubricants, antibiotic ointment, analgesics.

Radiational injuries

  • Ionising radiation (radiotherapy for head and neck tumours, brachytherapy): radiation retinopathy (microangiopathy like diabetic retinopathy, months to years later), optic neuropathy, cataract (posterior subcapsular), dry eye, keratopathy, lid changes.
  • Management of radiation retinopathy: anti-VEGF, intravitreal steroids, laser for neovascularisation.
  • Infrared (glassblowers’ cataract: exfoliation of lens capsule), microwave (cataract), electric shock (anterior subcapsular cataract).
TR 20. Orbital compartment syndrome — lateral canthotomy and cantholysis. NEW · outline

Orbital compartment syndrome (OCS)

  • Rapid rise in orbital pressure (orbit is a closed space bounded by bone and orbital septum) compromising blood flow to the optic nerve and retina; blindness can follow within 60–100 minutes.

Causes

  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage (trauma, orbital fractures, after blepharoplasty, retrobulbar or peribulbar injection, anticoagulants), orbital emphysema, orbital cellulitis and abscess, massive fluid resuscitation (burns), prone surgery.

Clinical features

  • Pain, tense proptosis, resistance to retropulsion, tight lids, reduced vision, RAPD, raised IOP (often over 40 mmHg), ophthalmoplegia, chemosis.

Management

  • Clinical diagnosis: do not delay for imaging.
  • Immediate lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis (superior cantholysis if needed) under local anaesthesia; check IOP and vision after.
  • Adjuncts: IV mannitol, acetazolamide, steroids; head elevation; reverse anticoagulation with physicians; CT later, surgical drainage of haematoma or abscess.
  • Repair of canthus later.

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema

TR 21. Choroidal rupture and traumatic macular hole. NEW · outline

Choroidal rupture

  • Break in the choroid, Bruch’s membrane and RPE after blunt trauma (globe compressed anteroposteriorly).
  • Direct (anterior, at the site of impact, parallel to the ora) or indirect (posterior, crescentic, concentric to the disc, temporal side; may pass through the fovea).
  • Initially hidden by subretinal haemorrhage; later yellow-white crescentic scar.
  • Complication: CNV (in 10–20%, from weeks to years); risk higher if close to fovea and long ruptures.
  • Management: observation; OCT and Amsler monitoring for CNV; anti-VEGF for CNV; pneumatic displacement for large submacular haemorrhage.

Traumatic macular hole

  • Young patients after blunt trauma (contusion: sudden vitreous traction, necrosis, cyst rupture); often larger and irregular.
  • Spontaneous closure in a proportion (especially small holes in young people) within about 3–6 months.
  • Management: observe for a few months; vitrectomy with ILM peel (inverted flap for large holes) and gas tamponade if persisting; good closure rates; vision limited by associated injuries (commotio, choroidal rupture).

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema, Macular Hole Staging: Gass vs IVTS Classification

Chemical Injury

TR 17. Classify chemical injuries and discuss their prognostic indicators, management of the acute alkali burn, recent advances and management of sequelae. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Roper-Hall (Hughes modified): grade I–IV by corneal clarity and limbal ischaemia.
  • Dua: limbal involvement in clock hours and percentage of conjunctival involvement (grades I–VI); better for prognosis.

Prognostic indicators

  • Extent of limbal ischaemia, conjunctival involvement, corneal clarity, depth (iris and lens damage), IOP, agent (alkali worse than acid), duration of contact.

Acute alkali burn management

  • Immediate copious irrigation (at least 30 minutes, until pH 7), sweep fornices, double eversion, remove particles.
  • Topical steroids (first 7–10 days), ascorbate (topical 10% and oral) and citrate, doxycycline (collagenase inhibition), cycloplegics, antibiotics, IOP control, lubricants.
  • Amniotic membrane (ProKera or sutured) for grade III–IV; tenonplasty for limbal necrosis.

Recent advances

  • Amniotic membrane early, autologous serum, platelet-rich plasma, cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation (CLET), simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET, Sangwan), COMET.

Sequelae

  • Symblepharon (fornix reconstruction), dry eye, LSCD (limbal grafts), corneal scarring (keratoplasty after limbal restoration), keratoprosthesis (Boston KPro, OOKP), glaucoma, cataract, lid malposition.

Revise from: Roper-Hall vs Dua Classification of Chemical Eye Injury, Chemical in the Eye: Emergency First Aid for Acid, Alkali and Lime (Chuna)

Prevention

TR 18. Strategies to reduce morbidity and the incidence of preventable ocular injuries in the community. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Strategies

  • Primary prevention: protective eyewear at work (industry, agriculture), sports (racquet sports, cricket helmets), during firecrackers (Diwali) and ban on dangerous fireworks, seatbelts and airbags, safe storage of chemicals.
  • Education: school programmes, workers, parents (sharp toys, bows and arrows, gulli danda), alkali awareness (lime, chuna packets).
  • Legislation: workplace safety regulations, firecracker rules, toy standards.

Secondary prevention

  • Early access to trained care, first aid (irrigation for chemicals), public awareness of not rubbing or self-medicating, avoiding traditional eye remedies.

Tertiary

  • Prompt specialist referral, rehabilitation, low-vision services, registers and audit.

Revise from: Ocular Trauma Notes: BETT, Chemical Injury, Hyphema

TR 22. Firecracker and sports-related eye injuries — patterns and prevention. NEW · outline

Firecracker injuries

  • Common in India around Diwali; mostly young males and children, often bystanders.
  • Patterns: lid and facial burns, corneal and conjunctival thermal or chemical injury, foreign bodies, hyphaema, traumatic cataract, open globe injury, IOFB, retinal damage, orbital fractures; often bilateral.
  • Prevention: awareness campaigns, adult supervision, keeping a safe distance, eye protection, avoid holding crackers in hand, legal regulation of sales and types, community celebrations (public displays) instead of individual use, first-aid education (irrigate, do not rub, rapid referral).

Sports-related eye injuries

  • High-risk sports: cricket, hockey, squash, badminton (shuttlecock), tennis, football, boxing and martial arts, paintball, airguns.
  • Patterns: blunt injuries (hyphaema, angle recession, commotio, retinal dialysis, orbital fractures), corneal abrasions, lid lacerations, open globe (less common).
  • Prevention: certified protective eyewear (polycarbonate; ASTM F803), helmets with face guards (cricket, hockey), mandatory eye protection for functionally one-eyed athletes, rule enforcement, education of coaches and parents, pre-participation eye examination.
