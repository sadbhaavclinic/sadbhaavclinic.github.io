Refractive Surgery for Residents: PRK, LASIK, SMILE and ICL

For: PG residents · Practising ophthalmologists

Last updated: October 2026

Key points in 60 seconds

  • PRK: no flap, suits thinner corneas; slower recovery and haze (MMC reduces it).
  • LASIK: fast recovery; flap complications, dry eye and ectasia risk.
  • SMILE: no flap, small incision; mainly for myopia; ICL for high myopia or thin corneas.
  • Ectasia prevention: screen topography and tomography, and avoid surgery in suspect corneas.

Refractive surgery is elective surgery on healthy eyes, so patient selection matters more than technique. This page covers the main procedures, who is a good candidate, and the complications every resident should recognise.

The main procedures

ProcedureHow it worksStrengthsWatch out for
PRK / surface ablationEpithelium removed, excimer laser reshapes the stroma surfaceNo flap; preserves more stroma; suitable for thinner corneas and contact sportsPain and slower recovery for several days; haze (mitomycin C reduces the risk)
LASIKA flap is created, excimer laser treats the stroma beneath, flap replacedFast recovery, little painFlap complications, dry eye, and ectasia in at-risk corneas
SMILE / lenticule extractionFemtosecond laser cuts a lenticule, removed through a small incisionNo flap; smaller incision; often less dry eyeMainly for myopia and myopic astigmatism; enhancement is more complex
Phakic IOL (ICL)A lens is implanted in front of the natural lensHigh myopia, thin corneas; reversible; excellent quality of visionVault problems, cataract, raised IOP, endothelial loss; needs adequate anterior chamber depth
Refractive lens exchangeThe natural lens is removed and replaced with an IOLHigh hyperopia or presbyopic patientsRetinal detachment risk in young high myopes; loss of accommodation

Lenticule extraction in 2026: SMILE, SMILE Pro, SILK and others

Lenticule extraction has moved from a single platform to several. All cut an intrastromal lenticule with a femtosecond laser and remove it through a small incision, without a flap or an excimer laser. They differ in laser speed, lenticule design, centration and cyclotorsion tools, and in the evidence behind them.

ProcedureLaser (company)Key featuresEvidence and status
SMILEVisuMax 500 (Zeiss)The original small-incision lenticule extraction; lenticule cut in about 25 to 30 secondsThe largest body of evidence, with ten-year follow-up and millions of eyes treated
SMILE ProVisuMax 800 (Zeiss)2 MHz laser; lenticule cut in under 10 seconds; CentraLign centration aid and OcuLign cyclotorsion compensation for astigmatismCE marked; US FDA approval for myopia with or without astigmatism in January 2024; widely available in India. Shorter suction time is the main practical advantage
SILK (smooth incision lenticular keratomileusis)ELITA (Johnson & Johnson Vision)Low-energy, high-frequency pulses designed for smooth lenticule surfaces and easy dissection; biconvex lenticule shapeLaunched at ESCRS 2023; early studies (including from India) show good safety and efficacy for myopia and myopic astigmatism. Not cleared by the US FDA for lenticule procedures at launch. Long-term data are limited
CLEAR (corneal lenticule extraction for advanced refractive correction)FEMTO LDV Z8 (Ziemer)Low-energy, high-frequency laser; mobile platform also used for cataract and keratoplastyCE marked; smaller published series
SmartSightATOS (Schwind)Eye tracking with cyclotorsion control during the cutCE marked; growing published series

What to tell patients: the newer platforms aim for faster, smoother and better-centred cuts, but visual outcomes in the published studies are broadly similar to standard SMILE and to modern LASIK. The choice of surgeon, careful screening for ectasia risk, and the right procedure for the cornea matter more than the brand. Hyperopic lenticule extraction is still being studied and is not widely used.

Enhancement after lenticule extraction is usually by surface ablation (PRK) on top of the cap, or by converting the cap into a flap (CIRCLE) and treating with an excimer laser. Plan the possibility before surgery.

Use the refractive surgery calculator to check residual stromal bed, percent tissue altered, the Randleman ectasia score and ICL screening criteria.

Who is a good candidate?

  • Adult (usually 18 or older) with a stable refraction (typically no more than about 0.5 D change over a year).
  • Realistic expectations, and an understanding that reading glasses will still be needed with age.
  • Healthy ocular surface; treat dry eye and blepharitis before deciding.
  • Normal corneal tomography and adequate corneal thickness for the planned treatment.

Who should not have laser vision correction?

  • Keratoconus, forme fruste keratoconus, or suspicious tomography
  • Unstable refraction, pregnancy or breastfeeding
  • Uncontrolled autoimmune or collagen vascular disease
  • Active ocular infection or inflammation, severe dry eye, or a history of herpetic keratitis (relative)
  • Unrealistic expectations

Ectasia: the complication you must prevent

  • Key risk factors: abnormal pre-operative tomography, thin cornea, thin residual stromal bed, young age, and high myopic correction.
  • A residual stromal bed below about 250–300 µm is generally considered unsafe for LASIK.
  • Percent tissue altered (PTA) of 40% or more is a recognised risk factor.
  • Screening is covered in detail in our topography and tomography tips.

LASIK flap complications to recognise

  • Diffuse lamellar keratitis (DLK): sterile, sand-like inflammation at the interface in the first days. Treat with intensive steroids; grade 4 can cause scarring.
  • Infectious keratitis: focal infiltrate, pain, worsening after the first days. Lift the flap, culture and treat. Atypical mycobacteria are a classic cause.
  • Epithelial ingrowth: grey nests of epithelium under the flap edge. Lift and scrape if progressive or affecting vision.
  • Flap striae and displacement: look for wrinkles in retroillumination. Refloat and smooth early.

Phakic IOL pearls

  • Adequate anterior chamber depth and endothelial cell count are prerequisites; follow the manufacturer's criteria.
  • The vault (distance between the ICL and the natural lens) is checked after surgery. Too low a vault risks cataract; too high risks angle narrowing and raised IOP.
  • Monitor IOP, the anterior chamber angle and endothelial cell density over the long term.

After refractive surgery: remember it forever

  • Standard keratometry is unreliable after corneal refractive surgery. Use special methods for IOL power calculation. See post-refractive IOL calculation.
  • Applanation IOP reads falsely low after myopic laser correction, because the cornea is thinner.
