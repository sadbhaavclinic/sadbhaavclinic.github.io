MD/MS/DNB Ophthalmology Theory Questions: Ocular Oncology (22 Questions with Answer Outlines)

For: PG residents

Last updated: November 2026

All 22 ocular oncology theory questions from the MD, MS and DNB question bank, grouped by topic. Tap a question to open its model answer outline. Every question links to the pages on this site to revise it from.

How to use this page: read the question, write or say your own answer in about ten minutes (introduction, classification, features, investigations, management, recent advances, one diagram), then open the outline and compare. The outlines show the points examiners look for; they are not word-for-word answers. Questions are as recalled by candidates, not official papers.

HIGH-YIELD asked often: prepare these first. DNB / UNIV asked in DNB and other university exams. NEW added in the 2026 update: likely future questions.

Classification

ON 1. Classify the extraocular and intraocular tumours, with a note on their prognosis; malignant tumours of the eye and adnexa. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Classification

  • Eyelid: benign (papilloma, naevi, keratoacanthoma, xanthelasma), malignant (BCC, SCC, sebaceous carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, melanoma).
  • Conjunctiva and cornea: OSSN (CIN to invasive SCC), naevi, primary acquired melanosis, melanoma, lymphoma, dermoids.
  • Uvea: naevi, melanoma, metastases, haemangioma (circumscribed, diffuse in Sturge–Weber), osteoma, melanocytoma.
  • Retina: retinoblastoma, astrocytic hamartoma, capillary haemangioblastoma (VHL), cavernous haemangioma, vitreoretinal lymphoma, CHRPE.
  • Optic nerve: glioma, meningioma, melanocytoma.
  • Orbit: dermoid, haemangiomas, lymphangioma, rhabdomyosarcoma, lymphoma, lacrimal gland tumours, metastases.

Prognosis

  • Retinoblastoma: over 95% survival in high-income settings, lower in LMICs with late presentation (extraocular spread).
  • Uveal melanoma: metastasis to the liver in up to 50%; monosomy 3 and BAP1 loss predict poor prognosis.
  • BCC: excellent; sebaceous carcinoma and Merkel cell carcinoma: guarded; lymphoma: good for low-grade.
ON 2. Describe the investigations for intraocular malignancies in an adult and the options for their management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Investigations for intraocular malignancy in an adult

  • Slit lamp, indirect ophthalmoscopy, fundus photography, B-scan (acoustic hollowness, choroidal excavation, collar-stud shape for melanoma), A-scan (low to medium internal reflectivity), UBM (ciliary body), FFA (double circulation), ICGA, OCT (subretinal fluid), autofluorescence (orange pigment).
  • Systemic: liver ultrasound, LFT, MRI liver (melanoma metastases), CT chest and abdomen, primary search for metastases (breast, lung), PET.
  • Biopsy: fine-needle aspiration biopsy for prognostic genetics (monosomy 3, GEP class), vitreous biopsy for lymphoma (cytology, IL-10:IL-6 ratio, MYD88 mutation).

Options for management

  • Observation (small indeterminate lesions with documented growth risk factors: TFSOM-DIM mnemonic).
  • Melanoma: brachytherapy (ruthenium-106, iodine-125), proton beam, stereotactic radiotherapy, transpupillary thermotherapy (small), local resection, enucleation (large tumours: COMS), tebentafusp for metastatic HLA-A*02:01 disease.
  • Metastases: systemic therapy, external beam radiotherapy, anti-VEGF.
  • Lymphoma: intravitreal methotrexate or rituximab, radiotherapy, systemic chemotherapy with CNS cover.

Leukocoria & Retinoblastoma

ON 3. Leukocoria — differential diagnosis and investigation of a child with leukocoria. DNB / UNIV · outline

Leukocoria

  • White pupillary reflex; first sign of retinoblastoma in most children (urgent referral).

Differential diagnosis

  • Retinoblastoma (must be excluded), congenital cataract, persistent fetal vasculature, Coats disease, ROP (stage 5), toxocariasis, coloboma (choroidal), retinal detachment, myelinated nerve fibres, vitreous haemorrhage (organised), endophthalmitis, FEVR, Norrie disease, incontinentia pigmenti, retinal dysplasia, astrocytic hamartoma, medulloepithelioma.

Investigation of a child with leukocoria

  • History (birth weight, prematurity, family history, pets: toxocara), both eyes examined under anaesthesia with indirect ophthalmoscopy and indentation, RetCam photographs.
  • B-scan ultrasound (calcification: retinoblastoma), MRI orbits and brain (optic nerve invasion, pineal), avoid CT where possible.
  • Serology (toxocara ELISA), FFA under anaesthesia (Coats telangiectasia), genetic testing (RB1, NDP).

Revise from: Leukocoria: A Differential

ON 4. Recent classifications of retinoblastoma (Reese-Ellsworth, ICRB) from clinical and pathological viewpoints, with reference to prognosis and management. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Reese–Ellsworth (historical)

  • Groups I–V for external beam radiotherapy outcome (size and location by disc diameters); poor for chemotherapy era.

International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB)

  • Group A: small tumours (3 mm or less) away from fovea and disc.
  • B: larger tumours, or near fovea or disc, or subretinal fluid within 3 mm.
  • C: localised subretinal or vitreous seeds (within 3 mm of the tumour).
  • D: diffuse seeds (more than 3 mm from the tumour).
  • E: extensive: neovascular glaucoma, anterior segment involvement, massive haemorrhage, phthisis, over 50% of globe, buphthalmos, orbital cellulitis-like presentation.

TNMH and IIRC (8th AJCC)

  • Adds heritable trait (H); pathological staging after enucleation (choroidal, scleral, optic nerve invasion).

Relevance to prognosis and management

  • A: focal therapy (laser, cryo); B–C: chemoreduction plus focal; D: chemoreduction plus focal, intra-arterial or intravitreal chemotherapy; E: enucleation (with adjuvant chemotherapy if high-risk pathology); globe salvage drops as group increases.

Revise from: Leukocoria: A Differential, Retinoblastoma Genetics: RB1, Two-Hit Model, Screening

ON 5. Genetic transmission of retinoblastoma and genetic counselling. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Genetics

  • RB1 tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 13q14; Knudson two-hit hypothesis.
  • Heritable (about 40%): germline mutation; usually bilateral or multifocal, younger age; autosomal dominant with about 90% penetrance; risk of second cancers (osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcomas, melanoma) and pinealoblastoma (trilateral).
  • Non-heritable (about 60%): both hits somatic; unilateral, unifocal; MYCN-amplified retinoblastoma (no RB1 mutation, very young).

Genetic counselling

  • Test the tumour and blood for RB1 mutations.
  • Offspring of survivors: 50% risk if heritable; risk to siblings depends on parental carrier status (parental mosaicism).
  • Unilateral cases without germline mutation found: low risk (about 1%) to offspring.
  • Screening of at-risk children from birth (examination under anaesthesia), prenatal and pre-implantation diagnosis options.

Revise from: Retinoblastoma Genetics: RB1, Two-Hit Model, Screening

ON 6. Pathology and histopathology of retinoblastoma — rosettes, spread and pseudoglioma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Pathology

  • Malignant tumour of primitive retinal cells; endophytic (into vitreous, seeds), exophytic (subretinal, RD), diffuse infiltrating (older children, pseudohypopyon), cavitary.

Histopathology

  • Small round blue cells with high nucleus-cytoplasm ratio, necrosis and calcification.
  • Rosettes: Flexner–Wintersteiner (true rosette with lumen: retinoblastoma specific), Homer Wright (neuroblastoma, medulloblastoma), fleurettes (photoreceptor differentiation, retinocytoma).
  • High-risk features: massive choroidal invasion (3 mm or more), post-laminar optic nerve invasion, scleral or extrascleral invasion, anterior segment invasion.

Spread

  • Direct along the optic nerve to brain, choroid to blood (bone marrow, bones), orbit, lymphatics (with conjunctival involvement).

Pseudoglioma

  • Conditions mimicking retinoblastoma (leukocoria): Coats disease, PHPV, toxocariasis, ROP, congenital cataract, coloboma, retinal dysplasia, endophthalmitis.

Revise from: Leukocoria: A Differential

ON 7. Role of the various investigative modalities in retinoblastoma. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Investigative modalities

  • Examination under anaesthesia with indirect ophthalmoscopy and scleral indentation (both eyes), RetCam photography, measuring IOP and corneal diameter.
  • Ultrasound B-scan: mass with calcification (high reflectivity with shadowing); distinguishes from Coats and PHPV.
  • MRI (best): tumour extent, optic nerve invasion, pineal gland (trilateral); avoid CT (radiation in heritable cases).
  • OCT (handheld): small tumours, foveal involvement; FFA in selected cases.
  • Systemic: bone marrow and CSF cytology for high-risk or extraocular disease; bone scan.
  • Genetic testing (RB1) in blood and tumour; avoid fine-needle biopsy (seeding).

Revise from: Leukocoria: A Differential

ON 8. Critically evaluate treatment strategies for bilateral retinoblastoma in an 18-month-old (or 1-year-old) child — conservative management, chemoreduction, radiotherapy and newer modalities. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Bilateral retinoblastoma in an 18-month-old

  • Assess each eye by ICRB, MRI for pineal, systemic evaluation, genetic testing, counselling.

Conservative management (globe salvage)

  • Systemic chemoreduction (vincristine, etoposide, carboplatin: VEC, 6 cycles) to shrink tumours with focal consolidation (laser, transpupillary thermotherapy, cryotherapy, plaque brachytherapy).
  • Intra-arterial chemotherapy (melphalan via ophthalmic artery: superselective), effective for group D, used in bilateral cases (tandem therapy).
  • Intravitreal chemotherapy (melphalan, topotecan) for vitreous seeds; intracameral for anterior seeds.

Radiotherapy

  • External beam radiotherapy largely abandoned (second cancers in heritable cases, orbital hypoplasia); plaque brachytherapy for focal tumours.

Enucleation

  • Group E eye or failed salvage; adjuvant chemotherapy for high-risk pathology.

Follow-up

  • Repeated EUA every 3–4 weeks until stable, MRI for pineal, lifelong surveillance for second cancers; screening of siblings.

Revise from: Retinoblastoma Genetics: RB1, Two-Hit Model, Screening, Leukocoria: A Differential

ON 9. Intra-arterial and intravitreal chemotherapy; the 8th edition AJCC TNMH classification. · outline

Intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC)

  • Superselective catheterisation of the ophthalmic artery via the femoral artery; melphalan (with topotecan or carboplatin) delivered every 3–4 weeks for 3 cycles.
  • Indications: unilateral group D and some group E eyes, bilateral cases (tandem), recurrences after systemic chemotherapy.
  • Results: globe salvage in about 70–90% of group D; less systemic toxicity.
  • Complications: vascular occlusions (ophthalmic artery, choroidal ischaemia), eyelid oedema, ptosis, transient neutropenia, radiation exposure from fluoroscopy, stroke (rare).

Intravitreal chemotherapy

  • Melphalan (20–30 µg) with or without topotecan, injected via pars plana with safety-enhanced technique (cryotherapy at the needle site, low IOP); weekly for vitreous seeds.
  • Very effective for vitreous seeds; complications: retinal toxicity (salt-and-pepper), hypotony, extraocular spread (rare with precautions).
  • Intracameral chemotherapy for aqueous seeds.

8th edition AJCC TNMH classification

  • cT1 (tumours confined to retina with subretinal fluid within 5 mm), cT2 (with vitreous or subretinal seeding, or RD), cT3 (advanced intraocular disease: phthisis, NVG, anterior segment invasion), cT4 (extraocular: orbit, optic nerve).
  • N (regional nodes), M (distant metastasis).
  • H: heritable trait — H1 (bilateral, trilateral, family history, RB1 germline mutation), H0 (no mutation), HX (unknown); first time heredity is included in a cancer staging system.
  • Pathological pT stages after enucleation (choroidal, optic nerve, scleral invasion).
ON 19. Retinoblastoma in India — late presentation, extraocular disease and strategies to improve survival. NEW · outline

Late presentation in India

  • India has the largest number of children with retinoblastoma in the world (about 1500–2000 new cases a year); most present with leukocoria or proptosis; many with advanced intraocular (group D/E) or extraocular disease.
  • Reasons: lack of awareness, delay in referral, cost and distance, refusal of enucleation, use of traditional remedies, incomplete treatment and loss to follow-up.

Extraocular disease

  • Orbital extension, optic nerve and CNS spread, metastases (bone marrow, bones, lymph nodes).
  • Management: high-dose chemotherapy (neoadjuvant), enucleation or exenteration, orbital radiotherapy, adjuvant chemotherapy; CNS disease: poor prognosis; high-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue in some.

Strategies to improve survival

  • Awareness campaigns (“white reflex” in photographs), red reflex testing at birth and at immunisation visits (RBSK), training of primary care workers and paediatricians, quick referral pathways to retinoblastoma centres, multidisciplinary care, financial support (government schemes, PM-JAY, NGOs), counselling to accept enucleation when needed, reducing abandonment, histopathology-guided adjuvant chemotherapy, genetic testing and screening of siblings, survivorship follow-up, national registries and collaboration (Global Retinoblastoma Study Group).

Revise from: Leukocoria: A Differential, Retinoblastoma Genetics: RB1, Two-Hit Model, Screening

Uveal & Other Tumours

ON 10. Uveal (choroidal) melanoma — types, clinical features, pathology, management and prognosis. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Types

  • Choroidal (commonest), ciliary body, iris; spindle A (naevi), spindle B, epithelioid (poorer prognosis) and mixed (Callender classification).

Clinical features

  • Pigmented or amelanotic dome or collar-stud (mushroom: Bruch membrane breach) mass, subretinal fluid, orange pigment (lipofuscin), sentinel vessels (ciliary body), secondary cataract or glaucoma.
  • Risk factors for growth of small lesions: TFSOM-DIM (thickness over 2 mm, fluid, symptoms, orange pigment, margin near the disc, ultrasound hollowness, halo absence, drusen absence).

Pathology

  • From uveal melanocytes; GNAQ and GNA11 mutations; monosomy 3, BAP1 loss and class 2 GEP predict metastasis; spreads haematogenously to the liver.

Management

  • Small: observation or TTT, brachytherapy; medium: brachytherapy (COMS: survival same as enucleation), proton beam; large: enucleation (pre-enucleation radiotherapy no benefit, COMS); local resection; metastatic disease: tebentafusp (HLA-A*02:01), liver-directed therapy, immunotherapy.

Prognosis

  • 10-year metastatic risk depends on tumour size (COMS), cell type, extrascleral extension, ciliary body involvement, genetics.

Revise from: Choroidal Naevus vs Melanoma: TFSOM-DIM Risk Factors, Imaging and When to Refer, COMS Trial Findings for Choroidal Melanoma

ON 11. Choroidal haemangioma and choroidal osteoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Circumscribed choroidal haemangioma

  • Benign vascular hamartoma; orange-red dome-shaped mass at the posterior pole; middle-aged; serous RD, CME, hyperopic shift.
  • Imaging: B-scan (high internal reflectivity, acoustically solid), ICGA (early hyperfluorescence with late washout), FFA, OCT (subretinal fluid).
  • Treatment: PDT (first line), TTT, radiotherapy (plaque, proton, external beam), anti-VEGF adjunct.

Diffuse choroidal haemangioma

  • Sturge–Weber syndrome: thick diffuse orange-red fundus (tomato ketchup), glaucoma, exudative RD; low-dose radiotherapy, PDT, oral propranolol (trial).

Choroidal osteoma

  • Benign ossifying tumour of the choroid; young women; yellow-white or orange, well-defined, juxtapapillary; bilateral in about 25%.
  • Imaging: B-scan (very high reflectivity with orbital shadowing), CT (bone density), OCT (sponge-like appearance).
  • Complications: decalcification, CNV (anti-VEGF), photoreceptor loss.
  • Management: observation, anti-VEGF for CNV; PDT for decalcification (debated).
ON 12. Retinal vascular tumours — capillary haemangioma (von Hippel-Lindau), cavernous haemangioma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Retinal capillary haemangioblastoma (capillary haemangioma)

  • Benign vascular tumour; solitary sporadic or multiple in von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHL gene, 3p25; autosomal dominant).
  • Features: red-orange round tumour with a dilated feeding artery and draining vein; peripheral (endophytic) or juxtapapillary (exophytic, harder to treat); exudation, macular oedema, exudative and tractional RD.
  • VHL systemic: cerebellar and spinal haemangioblastomas, renal cell carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma, pancreatic cysts; screening of the eye yearly from infancy and genetic testing of relatives.
  • Treatment: laser photocoagulation (small tumours), cryotherapy (peripheral larger tumours), PDT and TTT (juxtapapillary), plaque radiotherapy, vitrectomy for complications, belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor: approved for VHL tumours; shrinks retinal lesions).

Retinal cavernous haemangioma

  • Cluster of grape-like saccular aneurysms filled with dark blood with plasma-erythrocyte separation (fluid levels); no feeder vessels, no exudation; FFA: slow filling with caps of fluorescein.
  • Associated with cerebral and skin cavernous malformations (CCM genes, autosomal dominant).
  • Management: observation; treat vitreous haemorrhage; screen for brain lesions.
ON 13. Pigmented iris lesions and iris cysts — differential diagnosis. DNB / UNIV · outline

Pigmented iris lesions

  • Iris freckles (superficial, flat, common), iris naevus (stromal, may distort the pupil, ectropion uveae), Lisch nodules (NF1; bilateral, dome-shaped), iris melanoma (growth, size over 3 mm, prominent vessels, ectropion uveae, raised IOP, hyphaema, seeding, inferior location), melanocytoma (black, may seed and cause glaucoma), adenoma of the iris pigment epithelium, metastases (pigmented rare), ICE syndrome (Cogan–Reese iris nodules), heterochromia (siderosis, Horner, melanosis oculi), pigment epithelial cysts.

Iris cysts

  • Primary: iris pigment epithelial cysts (commonest; at the pupillary margin, mid-zone or iris root; stable; may cause pseudo-plateau iris), iris stromal cysts (children; may enlarge).
  • Secondary: epithelial implantation cysts after surgery or trauma (grow, glaucoma), miotic-induced cysts (pupillary margin), parasitic (cysticercus), tumour-associated.

Evaluation and management

  • Slit lamp, gonioscopy, photograph, UBM (cyst vs solid tumour), AS-OCT; observation of stable lesions; FNAB for suspicious tumours; excision, laser or aspiration for symptomatic cysts; plaque or proton therapy, iridectomy or enucleation for melanoma.

Revise from: Iris Nodules and Spots: Koeppe, Busacca, Lisch and More

ON 14. Rhabdomyosarcoma — clinical features and histopathology. DNB / UNIV · outline

Rhabdomyosarcoma

  • Commonest primary orbital malignancy of childhood (mean age about 7 years); arises from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells (not from extraocular muscles).

Clinical features

  • Rapidly progressive (days to weeks) unilateral proptosis, often superonasal; globe displacement, lid swelling, ptosis, palpable mass; may mimic orbital cellulitis or trauma.

Histopathology

  • Embryonal (commonest in the orbit; good prognosis; spindle cells, cross-striations), botryoid (subtype in mucosa), alveolar (inferior orbit; worse prognosis; PAX3/7-FOXO1 fusion), pleomorphic (adults).
  • Immunohistochemistry: desmin, myogenin, MyoD1.

Investigations

  • Urgent CT or MRI, biopsy (incisional or excisional), staging (chest CT, bone marrow, bone scan, lumbar puncture for parameningeal disease).

Management

  • Chemotherapy (vincristine, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide: IRS protocols) with radiotherapy (proton beam reduces late effects); survival over 90% for localised orbital disease; late effects: cataract, dry eye, orbital hypoplasia, second cancers.
ON 15. Write briefly on metastatic lesions of the eye. · outline

Metastatic lesions of the eye

  • Commonest intraocular malignancy in adults (often unrecognised); spread haematogenously; choroid most commonly (high blood flow), followed by iris, ciliary body, optic disc, retina, orbit.

Primary sources

  • Women: breast (commonest). Men: lung (commonest). Others: kidney, gastrointestinal tract, prostate, skin melanoma, thyroid, carcinoid; children: neuroblastoma (orbit), leukaemia.
  • In about a third, the eye metastasis appears before the primary is found (often lung).

Features

  • Choroidal: creamy-yellow, flat or mildly elevated, multifocal or bilateral lesions with subretinal fluid; leopard spot pigmentation; less elevated than melanoma.
  • Imaging: B-scan (moderate to high internal reflectivity), OCT (lumpy-bumpy surface), FFA.
  • Orbital: proptosis, diplopia; enophthalmos with scirrhous breast cancer.

Management

  • Systemic work-up (PET-CT), treat primary (chemotherapy, hormonal, targeted, immunotherapy), external beam or plaque radiotherapy, PDT, anti-VEGF; palliation; median survival depends on primary.
ON 16. Kaposi sarcoma. DNB / UNIV · outline

Kaposi sarcoma

  • Malignant vascular tumour associated with human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8); in HIV/AIDS (CD4 low), elderly Mediterranean men (classic), African endemic, and transplant patients (iatrogenic).

Ocular involvement

  • Eyelid: violaceous purple nodules or plaques, lid oedema, entropion, trichiasis.
  • Conjunctiva: bright red, flat or nodular vascular lesion, often in the inferior fornix, mistaken for subconjunctival haemorrhage (does not resolve).
  • Orbit (rare).

Histopathology

  • Spindle cells with slit-like vascular spaces containing red cells, haemosiderin; HHV-8 LANA staining.

Management

  • Antiretroviral therapy (immune recovery often shrinks lesions), local excision, cryotherapy, radiotherapy (very sensitive), intralesional chemotherapy (vinblastine, interferon), systemic chemotherapy (liposomal doxorubicin, paclitaxel) for disseminated disease.
ON 17. Discuss the phakomatoses affecting the eye — syndromes and their systemic and ocular features. HIGH-YIELD · outline

Phakomatoses (neurocutaneous syndromes)

  • Neurofibromatosis type 1 (17q, neurofibromin): café-au-lait spots, neurofibromas, Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas), optic glioma, plexiform neurofibroma of lid (S-shaped lid), sphenoid wing dysplasia (pulsatile proptosis), congenital glaucoma, choroidal nodules on infrared imaging.
  • Neurofibromatosis type 2 (22q, merlin): bilateral vestibular schwannomas, juvenile posterior subcapsular cataract, combined hamartoma of retina and RPE, ERM.
  • Tuberous sclerosis (TSC1, TSC2): adenoma sebaceum, ash-leaf macules, seizures, retinal astrocytic hamartomas (mulberry lesions).
  • Von Hippel–Lindau (3p): retinal capillary haemangioblastoma, cerebellar haemangioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma; belzutifan for VHL tumours.
  • Sturge–Weber (GNAQ somatic): port-wine stain (V1, V2), ipsilateral glaucoma, diffuse choroidal haemangioma (tomato ketchup fundus), leptomeningeal angioma.
  • Wyburn–Mason (racemose haemangioma of retina and midbrain AVM).
  • Ataxia–telangiectasia (conjunctival telangiectasia, oculomotor apraxia).

Management

  • Multidisciplinary; genetic counselling; screening (VHL: annual retinal examination from infancy); treat ocular lesions (laser or PDT for haemangioblastomas, glaucoma surgery, PDT for choroidal haemangioma).
ON 18. COMS study and current management of uveal melanoma. · outline

COMS (Collaborative Ocular Melanoma Study)

  • Medium tumours (2.5–10 mm thick, up to 16 mm diameter): iodine-125 brachytherapy vs enucleation: no difference in 12-year survival (all-cause mortality about 43% vs 41%); melanoma metastasis similar.
  • Large tumours (over 10 mm thick or over 16 mm diameter): pre-enucleation radiotherapy vs enucleation alone: no survival benefit.
  • Small tumours (observational): growth risk factors identified; 5-year melanoma mortality about 1%.
  • Also: quality of life (similar), visual outcomes after brachytherapy (decline with radiation retinopathy), patterns of metastasis (liver commonest).

Current management

  • Small melanocytic lesions: risk factors (TFSOM-DIM), observation with imaging, TTT or plaque if growth.
  • Medium tumours: plaque brachytherapy (iodine-125, ruthenium-106), proton beam, stereotactic radiotherapy; local resection (selected); endoresection.
  • Large tumours: enucleation (or proton beam in selected cases).
  • Prognostic biopsy (FNAB): monosomy 3, BAP1, gene expression profiling (class 1 vs 2), PRAME; guides surveillance (liver MRI every 3–6 months).
  • Metastatic disease: tebentafusp (improved overall survival in HLA-A*02:01-positive patients), immune checkpoint inhibitors, liver-directed therapy (percutaneous hepatic perfusion with melphalan), clinical trials.
  • Radiation complications: radiation retinopathy and optic neuropathy (anti-VEGF, steroids), cataract, neovascular glaucoma.

Revise from: COMS Trial Findings for Choroidal Melanoma, Landmark Trials in Uveitis and Ocular Oncology: MUST, VISUAL, SYCAMORE, POINT, FAST and COMS

ON 20. Choroidal naevus vs small melanoma — risk factors for growth and follow-up. NEW · outline

Choroidal naevus vs small melanoma

  • Choroidal naevus: common (about 5% of adults), flat or minimally elevated (under 2 mm), grey-brown, drusen on surface, halo; malignant transformation about 1 in 8800 per year.
  • Small melanoma: thickness about 1–2.5 mm or more with growth; orange pigment, subretinal fluid, symptoms.

Risk factors for growth (mnemonic: To Find Small Ocular Melanoma Doing Imaging)

  • Thickness over 2 mm (on ultrasound), Fluid (subretinal), Symptoms (vision loss), Orange pigment, Melanoma hollowness (acoustic) on ultrasound, Diameter over 5 mm (on photography).
  • MOLES score (UK): Mushroom shape, Orange pigment, Large size, Enlarging, Subretinal fluid: guides referral (0: common naevus; 1: low-risk; 2: high-risk; 3 or more: probable melanoma).

Follow-up

  • Baseline photographs, OCT, autofluorescence, ultrasound; review at 3–6 months then yearly if stable; refer when risk factors present; treat documented growth (plaque brachytherapy, TTT, PDT for small melanomas in trials).

Revise from: Choroidal Naevus vs Melanoma: TFSOM-DIM Risk Factors, Imaging and When to Refer

ON 21. Congenital hypertrophy of the RPE (CHRPE) and its association with familial adenomatous polyposis. NEW · outline

Congenital hypertrophy of the RPE (CHRPE)

  • Benign, flat, well-demarcated pigmented lesion with depigmented lacunae and a halo; solitary (unilateral), grouped (bear tracks), or atypical multiple bilateral lesions.
  • Histology: enlarged RPE cells with large melanin granules; overlying photoreceptor loss (relative scotoma); very rarely nodular adenoma or adenocarcinoma of the RPE.

Association with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)

  • Multiple, bilateral, oval or pisciform (fish-tail) pigmented lesions with depigmented tails, scattered randomly, are markers of FAP (APC gene, chromosome 5q21) and Gardner syndrome (osteomas, soft tissue tumours) and Turcot syndrome (brain tumours).
  • Four or more lesions, or bilateral lesions, suggest FAP; refer for colonoscopy and genetic testing (colon cancer risk nearly 100% without colectomy).
  • Solitary and grouped CHRPE are not associated with FAP.

Differential diagnosis

  • Choroidal naevus and melanoma (CHRPE is flat with sharp margins and lacunae), combined hamartoma, melanocytoma, RPE hyperplasia.

Revise from: Choroidal Naevus vs Melanoma: TFSOM-DIM Risk Factors, Imaging and When to Refer

ON 22. Ocular side effects of cancer therapies — immune checkpoint inhibitors, MEK inhibitors and chemotherapy. NEW · outline

Ocular side effects of cancer therapies

  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4): dry eye, uveitis (anterior and VKH-like), scleritis, optic neuritis, myasthenia-like ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, orbital inflammation, thyroid eye disease, melanoma-associated retinopathy-like changes.
  • MEK inhibitors (trametinib, binimetinib): MEK inhibitor-associated retinopathy (multifocal serous detachments, usually transient), RVO; BRAF inhibitors: uveitis.
  • FGFR inhibitors (erdafitinib): serous retinopathy; antibody–drug conjugates (belantamab mafodotin: microcyst-like keratopathy; mirvetuximab, tisotumab: keratopathy and conjunctivitis).
  • EGFR inhibitors (cetuximab, erlotinib): trichomegaly, blepharitis, keratitis.
  • Chemotherapy: docetaxel and paclitaxel (epiphora from canalicular stenosis, CME), 5-FU (canalicular fibrosis), cisplatin and carboplatin (retinopathy, optic neuropathy), cytarabine (keratoconjunctivitis: steroid drops prophylaxis), vincristine (cranial nerve palsies), tamoxifen (crystalline retinopathy), methotrexate.
  • Radiotherapy: cataract, radiation retinopathy and optic neuropathy, dry eye.
  • Bone marrow transplant: ocular GVHD.

Management

  • Baseline and periodic eye examination for high-risk drugs, communication with oncologists, grading toxicity (CTCAE), dose adjustment rather than stopping where possible, specific treatment (steroids, lubricants), document.

Revise from: Ocular Side Effects of Systemic Drugs (ADRs): Modern and Classic
